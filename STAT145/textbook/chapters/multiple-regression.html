24  Multiple Regression

The regression models in earlier chapters used a single predictor \(x\) to explain a response \(y\). Most real datasets carry many potential predictors: a house’s price depends on square footage and location and age and the number of bathrooms; a student’s exam score depends on hours studied and prior GPA and sleep and attendance. Multiple linear regression extends the simple regression framework to accommodate two or more predictors at once. This chapter introduces the multiple regression model, shows how to read the coefficient table software produces, discusses what “holding the other predictors constant” actually means in practice, introduces the adjusted \(R^2\) that penalizes extra predictors, and addresses multicollinearity — what happens when two predictors carry overlapping information. The chapter follows IMS’s ordering (inf-model-mlr.qmd), adapted and reframed for the simulation-first approach.

24.1 From one predictor to many

A single-predictor regression is a starting point, not an ending point. In the real world, a response variable is almost never driven by just one thing. When we ignore other variables that also influence the response, two things can go wrong.

Confounding can bias the slope estimate. If we regress house price on square footage alone, and larger houses happen to be newer and in fancier neighborhoods, the fitted slope absorbs the effects of all three — not just square footage. Our estimate of “how much does an extra square foot cost, in a comparable house?” is contaminated by the unmeasured differences in neighborhood and age.

Predictive accuracy suffers. Even when we don’t care about causal interpretation, ignoring available predictors leaves signal on the table. A model with three good predictors will typically predict future observations more accurately than a model with one.

Multiple regression solves the first problem by fitting all predictors together in a single model, so each coefficient represents the effect of that predictor while holding the others fixed. And it addresses the second by explaining more of the total variability with the additional predictors.

24.2 The multiple regression model

The multiple linear regression model with \(k\) predictors is

\[y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \beta_2 x_2 + \cdots + \beta_k x_k + \varepsilon,\]

where \(\beta_0\) is the population intercept, \(\beta_1, \dots, \beta_k\) are the population slopes for the \(k\) predictors, and \(\varepsilon\) is a random error term with mean 0 and constant variance \(\sigma^2\).

The fitted model estimated from a sample is

\[\hat{y} = b_0 + b_1 x_1 + b_2 x_2 + \cdots + b_k x_k,\]

with \(b_0, b_1, \ldots, b_k\) chosen to minimize the sum of squared residuals \(\sum (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2\) — the same least-squares criterion used in simple regression.

Geometrically, a single-predictor regression fits a line in a two-dimensional scatterplot. A two-predictor regression fits a plane in a three-dimensional cloud. With three or more predictors we lose the ability to draw the picture, but the mathematics generalizes without incident: we are always fitting the flat surface that comes closest to the observed points in the sum-of-squared-residuals sense.

24.3 Reading multiple-regression output

Software returns a coefficient table for a multiple regression fit that looks like this:

Predictor Estimate Std. Error \(t\)-value p-value
(Intercept) \(b_0\) \(SE(b_0)\) \(t_0\) \(p_0\)
\(x_1\) \(b_1\) \(SE(b_1)\) \(t_1\) \(p_1\)
\(x_2\) \(b_2\) \(SE(b_2)\) \(t_2\) \(p_2\)
\(\vdots\)
\(x_k\) \(b_k\) \(SE(b_k)\) \(t_k\) \(p_k\)

Each row reports the estimated coefficient, its standard error, the \(t\)-statistic testing \(H_0: \beta_j = 0\) (with \(n - k - 1\) degrees of freedom), and the two-sided p-value. Above or below this table, software also reports goodness-of-fit statistics — the residual standard error, \(R^2\), and adjusted \(R^2\) — and the ANOVA table with the model-level \(F\)-statistic.

Suppose we regress the price of used cars (in thousands of dollars) on mileage (in ten-thousands), age (in years), and whether the car has one previous owner (yes = 1, no = 0). Software returns:

Predictor Estimate Std. Error \(t\) p
(Intercept) 28.4 1.7 16.7 < 0.001
mileage \(-0.85\) 0.14 \(-6.07\) < 0.001
age \(-1.20\) 0.31 \(-3.87\) < 0.001
one_owner 2.10 0.60 3.50 < 0.001

\(R^2 = 0.71\), adjusted \(R^2 = 0.70\), residual standard error \(s = 2.9\) thousand dollars, \(n = 84\).

Interpret each coefficient in context.

  • Intercept. A car with \(0\) mileage, age \(0\), and no prior owner (one_owner = 0) has an estimated mean price of $28,400. The intercept is often not a meaningful prediction on its own; here “age \(0\) with mileage \(0\)” describes a brand-new car with a specific ownership status.

  • Mileage. Holding age and ownership status fixed, each additional 10,000 miles is associated with a mean price decrease of $850.

  • Age. Holding mileage and ownership fixed, each additional year of age is associated with a mean price decrease of $1,200.

  • One_owner. Holding mileage and age fixed, a car that has had exactly one prior owner sells for an average of $2,100 more than a car with more than one prior owner.

Each of the three \(t\)-statistics has a p-value below 0.001, so each predictor contributes discernibly above and beyond the others. The model as a whole explains \(R^2 = 71\%\) of the variability in used-car prices.

24.4 Interpreting coefficients “holding others constant”

The single most important interpretive habit in multiple regression is the phrase “holding the other predictors constant.” It appears in every coefficient interpretation, and dropping it turns a correct sentence into a wrong one.

To see why, consider the used-car model above. In simple regression on mileage alone, the fitted slope would absorb the effects of both mileage and age and ownership status — because older cars tend to have higher mileage, and single-owner cars tend to be lower-mileage. The single-predictor slope estimate would be steeper (more negative) than \(-0.85\), because it would be measuring “cars with high mileage sell for less” as a bundle of effects: high-mileage cars are also older and have had more owners.

The multiple-regression slope \(b_1 = -0.85\) isolates the mileage effect from the age and ownership effects. That is why it can be interpreted as “for each additional 10,000 miles, holding age and ownership status fixed.” The “holding fixed” is not a philosophical qualifier; it is the only way the coefficient makes sense.

Every coefficient interpretation in multiple regression should include the phrase “holding the other predictors constant” (or an equivalent like “controlling for” or “at fixed values of”). Interpreting \(b_j\) without that qualifier is one of the most common errors students make with multiple regression output.

24.5 Assessing overall fit: \(R^2\), adjusted \(R^2\), and the \(F\)-test

Three quantities that already appeared in simple regression take on richer meanings when there are multiple predictors.

\(R^2\) is still the fraction of total variability the model explains: \(R^2 = SSR / SST\). But now, adding any predictor to the model — even a completely useless one — can only increase \(R^2\), never decrease it. The additional predictor might soak up a tiny amount of variability by chance alone, which inflates \(R^2\) without indicating a real improvement in the model.

Adjusted \(R^2\) corrects for that inflation. It is computed as

\[R^2_{\text{adj}} = 1 - \frac{SSE / (n - k - 1)}{SST / (n - 1)} = 1 - (1 - R^2) \cdot \frac{n - 1}{n - k - 1}.\]

The adjustment penalizes models with more predictors: adding a useless predictor eats up one degree of freedom in the denominator without meaningfully reducing \(SSE\), so \(R^2_{\text{adj}}\) decreases rather than increases. Adjusted \(R^2\) is the right summary to compare candidate models with different numbers of predictors.

The model-level \(F\)-test tests

\[H_0: \beta_1 = \beta_2 = \cdots = \beta_k = 0 \quad \text{vs.} \quad H_A: \text{at least one } \beta_j \ne 0.\]

Under \(H_0\) and the LINE conditions, the \(F\)-statistic follows an \(F_{k,\,n-k-1}\) distribution. Small p-values indicate that some predictor contributes; they do not tell us which one. The individual \(t\)-tests (one per coefficient) answer the “which one” question — with the caveat that each \(t\) asks whether that predictor contributes above and beyond the others already in the model.

24.6 Multicollinearity

Two predictors in a regression exhibit multicollinearity when they are strongly linearly related to each other. This is not a problem for prediction, but it is a serious problem for interpreting individual coefficients: when two predictors carry nearly the same information, the fit cannot tell them apart, and the standard errors of both coefficients inflate.

A telltale sign of multicollinearity is a predictor whose relationship to the response looks strong on a scatterplot yet has an individual \(t\)-test that fails to reject. The predictor may be redundant with another predictor already in the model, so it does not contribute additional information once that other predictor is accounted for.

The most direct diagnostic is to plot the predictors against each other. A scatterplot showing a strong linear pattern between two predictors is evidence that they carry overlapping information about the response, and one may be safely dropped without losing much explanatory power. In practice, three responses are common when scatterplots reveal collinearity: (i) drop one of the collinear predictors, (ii) combine them into a single summary variable (an average or a sum, when the units allow it), or (iii) accept that individual coefficients cannot be separately interpreted but that the model still predicts well as a whole.

A model of a cherry tree’s timber volume includes both the tree’s height (feet) and diameter at breast height (inches). The individual \(t\)-tests report a very small p-value for diameter but a marginal p-value for height. A scatterplot of the two predictors shows a moderately strong positive relationship: taller trees tend to have larger diameters.

Interpret the pattern.

Height and diameter carry overlapping information about volume: knowing a tree’s diameter already tells us a lot about its likely height. Once diameter is in the model, the marginal contribution of height given diameter is smaller than height’s marginal contribution by itself. That is why height’s individual \(t\)-test looks weak. A researcher who plotted only volume vs. height would see a strong association; the multi-predictor \(t\)-test correctly reports that height adds little once diameter is included.

Extended Content: the variance inflation factor.

Beyond scatterplots, statisticians quantify the severity of multicollinearity with a single number per predictor: the variance inflation factor (VIF). For predictor \(x_j\),

\[VIF_j = \frac{1}{1 - R^2_j},\]

where \(R^2_j\) is the \(R^2\) of a regression of \(x_j\) on all the other predictors. A predictor uncorrelated with the others has \(R^2_j = 0\) and \(VIF_j = 1\); a predictor perfectly explained by the others has \(R^2_j = 1\) and infinite VIF. Rules of thumb (not sharp thresholds): \(VIF < 5\) is fine; \(5 \le VIF < 10\) warrants caution; \(VIF \ge 10\) means individual coefficients are unreliable.

VIF is retained here for readers, instructors, and later coursework that want a formal metric; UWL’s STAT 145 sequence detects multicollinearity through the scatterplot approach above and does not require the VIF machinery.

24.7 Building a model: adding predictors thoughtfully

Given many candidate predictors, which should we include? A full treatment of variable selection is beyond this chapter, but a few habits will serve you well.

Start with predictors you have a scientific reason to include. If the goal is to model house prices, square footage and number of bedrooms belong in the model whether or not they cross a significance threshold. Statistics is a supplement to domain knowledge, not a replacement.

Add predictors incrementally and watch adjusted \(R^2\). A new predictor that raises \(R^2\) but lowers adjusted \(R^2\) is not earning its keep. Adjusted \(R^2\) is the honest summary of model improvement.

Check scatterplots of predictors against each other. A new predictor that is strongly related to a predictor already in the model is a redundancy warning. Consider dropping it or combining it with the correlated predictor.

Individual \(t\)-tests are not a variable-selection procedure. In particular, dropping predictors one at a time based on their \(t\)-statistics — a procedure sometimes called “backward elimination” — is an unreliable strategy that inflates the effective Type I error rate. If you must automate variable selection, use adjusted \(R^2\), cross-validation, or a criterion like AIC or BIC (beyond this chapter’s scope).

24.8 Worked example: predicting body mass in penguins

Ecologists studied \(n = 342\) penguins from three Antarctic species and recorded body mass (in grams) along with several morphological measurements. Suppose we regress body mass on flipper length (mm) and bill depth (mm). Software reports:

Predictor Estimate Std. Error \(t\) p
(Intercept) \(-6420\) 613 \(-10.5\) < 0.001
flipper_length 51.5 2.10 24.5 < 0.001
bill_depth \(-135\) 27.6 \(-4.89\) < 0.001

\(R^2 = 0.762\), adjusted \(R^2 = 0.760\), residual SE \(s = 393\) g, model \(F = 542\) on 2 and 339 df (\(p < 0.001\)).

  1. Write the fitted equation and interpret each slope in context.

  2. Predict the body mass of a penguin with flipper length 200 mm and bill depth 18 mm.

  3. What does the model-level \(F\)-statistic tell us that the individual \(t\)-tests do not?

(a) Fitted equation:

\[\widehat{\text{mass}} = -6420 + 51.5 \times \text{flipper\_length} - 135 \times \text{bill\_depth}.\]

Flipper length slope. Holding bill depth fixed, each additional millimeter of flipper length is associated with a mean body-mass increase of 51.5 g.

Bill depth slope. Holding flipper length fixed, each additional millimeter of bill depth is associated with a mean body-mass decrease of 135 g. This may seem counterintuitive (bigger bills, lighter penguins?), but it reflects the joint effect of both predictors: the species with deeper bills happen to be the smaller-bodied ones, and once flipper length is accounted for, that species-composition effect shows up as a negative bill-depth coefficient.

(b) \(\widehat{\text{mass}} = -6420 + 51.5 \times 200 - 135 \times 18 = -6420 + 10{,}300 - 2430 = 1450 \text{ g}\).

Wait — this is far smaller than a real penguin, which suggests we are extrapolating. A quick check: flipper lengths in the dataset span roughly 172 to 231 mm, so 200 mm is interior. But bill depths span roughly 13 to 22 mm, and 18 mm is well interior too. The unrealistic prediction is a signal that either the model is misspecified (perhaps species should be a predictor too), or the small residual \(s = 393\) g is hiding structural issues visible only from a diagnostic plot.

(c) The \(F\)-test rejects overwhelmingly (\(F = 542\), \(p < 0.001\)), which tells us that at least one of the two predictors contributes discernibly. The individual \(t\)-tests confirm that both do. In simple regression the \(F\)-test would be redundant with the \(t\)-test; in multiple regression it summarizes the joint contribution and disagrees informatively with the individual \(t\)-tests in cases (like multicollinearity) where the whole is greater than any one part.

24.9 Diagnostics for multiple regression

The LINE conditions from the linear regression chapter carry over unchanged to multiple regression, but the checks look slightly different because we no longer have a single predictor’s scatterplot to eyeball.

Linearity. Plot each predictor against the residuals. A curved pattern in one plot suggests that predictor’s relationship with the response is not linear at fixed values of the others.

Independence. Same as in simple regression: the design of the study is what earns this assumption. Random sampling or random assignment protects independence; convenience samples and repeated measures on the same subject do not.

Normality of residuals. A residual histogram or Q-Q plot works the same as in simple regression.

Equal variance. The residual-versus-fitted plot is still the go-to diagnostic. A fan shape suggests the residual spread grows with \(\hat{y}\); if so, coefficient standard errors are unreliable and the reported CIs are too narrow. A log-transformation of the response often fixes fan-shaped residuals.

Add one MLR-specific check:

Multicollinearity. Inspect scatterplots of each pair of predictors. Strong pairwise associations flag pairs that carry overlapping information about the response; the multicollinearity section above discusses how to respond. (Extended-content readers can also inspect the variance inflation factor for a formal quantitative check.)

24.10 Chapter review

24.10.1 Summary

Multiple regression extends the simple regression model to two or more predictors, fit by least squares. The interpretation of each coefficient becomes “the mean change in \(y\) per unit change in \(x_j\) holding the other predictors fixed” — a qualifier that separates multiple regression’s slope from the simple-regression slope that would absorb the other predictors’ effects. Software reports coefficient estimates alongside a coefficient table, an ANOVA table with a model-level \(F\)-test, and goodness-of-fit statistics \(R^2\) and adjusted \(R^2\). Multicollinearity — when two predictors carry overlapping information — is a new concern that only arises with multiple predictors; the standard check is a scatterplot of each pair of predictors. (The variance inflation factor, VIF, offers a formal quantification of multicollinearity for readers who want the metric.) Diagnostics for the LINE conditions carry over from simple regression, with residual-versus-fitted plots and per-predictor residual checks taking the place of the single predictor plot.

24.10.2 Terms

Multiple linear regression, predictor, response, coefficient table, “holding others constant,” adjusted \(R^2\), model-level \(F\)-test, multicollinearity, residual-versus-fitted plot, extrapolation in multi-dimensional predictor space. (Extended-content only: variance inflation factor / VIF.)

    Predictor Estimate Std. Error \(t\) p

    Predictor VIF
    Income 1.4
    Years of education 8.7
    Occupational prestige score 9.1
    Age 1.2
    1. Which two predictors are causing concern about multicollinearity, and why?
    2. Explain in one sentence why high VIF makes it hard to interpret those two coefficients individually.

  4. Simple vs. multiple regression slope. A simple regression of house price on square footage gives a slope of $220 per additional square foot. A multiple regression of house price on square footage, number of bedrooms, and neighborhood-median-income gives a slope for square footage of $155.

    1. Why is the multiple-regression slope smaller?
    2. Which slope answers the question “in a comparable house, how much does an extra square foot cost?”
    3. Which slope answers the question “for two random houses differing only in size (ignoring their other differences), how much more expensive is the larger one?”

  5. When to include a predictor. A researcher must choose whether to add a fifth predictor to a four-predictor model of employee salary. The fifth predictor’s individual \(t\)-test has p-value 0.28, and a scatterplot shows a moderately strong linear relationship between the new predictor and one of the four already in the model.

    1. Should the researcher add the predictor purely based on statistical evidence?
    2. Suppose the researcher has a strong subject-matter reason to include the predictor even though its p-value is large. Is that a valid reason to include it?

StatLens Exercises

Let StatLens do the arithmetic; spend your effort on the reasoning a calculator can’t do.

Note. Exercise 4 (VIF and interpretation) uses the Extended Content variance inflation factor material and requires expert mode on the Multiple Regression tool. Exercises 2 and 5 reference VIF in a few options; the reasoning still works if you substitute “scatterplot showing a strong relationship between predictors” for high VIF and “no strong relationship on the predictor scatterplot” for low VIF.

  1. Frame the question. A real estate analyst wants to build a model that predicts a home’s sale price from its square footage, age, and neighborhood median income.

    1. Write the multiple regression model in the standard form \(y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \cdots\) with all variables clearly labeled.
    2. State the null and alternative hypotheses for the model-level \(F\)-test in words.
    3. State the null and alternative hypotheses for the individual \(t\)-test on square footage. Why is this a different question from the one the \(F\)-test asks?

    Show answer

    1. \(\text{price} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \cdot \text{sqft} + \beta_2 \cdot \text{age} + \beta_3 \cdot \text{med\_income} + \varepsilon\).

    2. \(H_0\): none of the predictors is linearly related to price (\(\beta_1 = \beta_2 = \beta_3 = 0\)). \(H_A\): at least one predictor is linearly related to price.

    3. \(H_0: \beta_1 = 0\) (square footage does not contribute, holding age and neighborhood income fixed). This is a conditional question: does square footage add anything above and beyond what age and neighborhood income already explain? The \(F\)-test asks the unconditional joint question: does any predictor contribute at all?

  2. Choose predictors thoughtfully. For each scenario, decide whether to include the new predictor in the model. Options: include, exclude, or it depends (and specify the deciding factor).

    1. The new predictor’s individual \(t\)-test has p = 0.03; VIF = 2.1; adjusted \(R^2\) increases from 0.55 to 0.61.
    2. The new predictor’s \(t\)-test has p = 0.42; VIF = 3.0; adjusted \(R^2\) drops from 0.55 to 0.53.
    3. The new predictor’s \(t\)-test has p < 0.001; VIF = 14.2; adjusted \(R^2\) increases from 0.55 to 0.56.
    4. The new predictor has a strong scientific reason to be in the model (a well-established causal driver), \(t\)-test p = 0.20, VIF = 2.5, adjusted \(R^2\) unchanged.

    Show answer

    1. Include. Statistically discernible, low VIF, and adjusted \(R^2\) rises: everything says yes.

    2. Exclude. Not discernible and adjusted \(R^2\) drops — the predictor is not earning its degree of freedom.

    3. It depends. The strong \(t\)-value with high VIF is a classic signal that the predictor duplicates an existing one; individual coefficients become unreliable. Consider dropping one of the collinear pair, or combining them into a single summary variable. Never keep both at VIF > 10 and interpret the coefficients separately.

    4. Include. Domain knowledge and the goal matter as much as the numerics. If there is a strong scientific reason to model the effect of a predictor, include it even without a small p-value; the coefficient still estimates the effect given the other predictors, and the standard error tells you how precise the estimate is. Statistical non-significance ≠ evidence of no effect.

  3. Read the coefficient table. Open the Multiple Regression tool loaded with the possum data. Select head length as the response and both total length and skull width as predictors.

    1. Write the fitted equation with numeric coefficients.
    2. Interpret each slope in context, using the phrase “holding the other constant.”
    3. Compare the total-length slope in the two-predictor model to the total-length slope from the simple regression in Ch 20. Are they the same? If they differ, what does that tell you about what the simple slope was actually measuring?
    4. Report the model-level \(F\)-statistic and its p-value. Does the model contribute discernibly?

    Show answer

    1. Read the coefficients directly from StatLens. The equation will read \(\widehat{\text{head\_l}} = b_0 + b_1 \cdot \text{total\_l} + b_2 \cdot \text{skull\_width}\).

    2. Each slope is the mean change in head length per unit change in that predictor, holding the other fixed. Report the actual numbers with units (mm per cm, mm per mm).

    3. The multi-predictor total-length slope will typically be smaller in magnitude than the simple-regression slope. The simple slope absorbed the joint effect of total length AND skull width, because larger possums tend to have both larger total lengths and larger skulls. The multi-predictor slope isolates the total-length effect.

    4. The model-level \(F\)-test rejects overwhelmingly on this data — both predictors carry real signal about head length.

  4. VIF and interpretation. Add a third predictor (say, tail length) to the model in Exercise 3.

    1. Report the VIFs for all three predictors. Are any concerning?
    2. Report the new adjusted \(R^2\) and compare to the two-predictor model. Did adding the third predictor help?
    3. Report the coefficient for total length and its standard error. How does the standard error compare to the two-predictor version? What does the change in \(SE\) reflect?
    4. A student says: “adjusted \(R^2\) went up, so the third predictor is clearly worth including.” Is that always sufficient justification? What else should you check before finalizing the model?

    Show answer

    1. Answers vary; report the VIF values. Values below 5 are safe; 5–10 warrants caution; 10+ is a problem.

    2. Adjusted \(R^2\) may rise, fall, or stay similar depending on the data. If it drops, the third predictor’s added variance-explained doesn’t pay for the degree of freedom it costs.

    3. Standard errors on individual coefficients generally inflate as correlated predictors are added — that inflation is exactly what VIF is quantifying. If total length’s SE rose noticeably, its coefficient became less precisely estimated because tail length duplicates some of the signal.

    4. Also check: (i) is the new predictor’s coefficient interpretable (any high-VIF flags?), (ii) are residual diagnostics still clean (linearity, equal variance, no new outliers), (iii) does the extra predictor make substantive sense in the problem context. A rise in adjusted \(R^2\) alone doesn’t override those.

  5. When \(F\) and \(t\) disagree. Use the Multiple Regression tool to construct or find a dataset where the model-level \(F\)-test rejects at \(\alpha = 0.05\) but none of the individual \(t\)-tests do.

    1. Report the \(F\)-statistic, its p-value, and the p-values of the individual \(t\)-tests. What does this pattern suggest?
    2. Report the VIFs for each predictor. Is high VIF the likely cause?
    3. What does “the model as a whole works, but no individual predictor is discernible” mean for the researcher trying to use this model?
    4. A student says: “If no individual \(t\)-test rejects, we should throw out the model.” Push back on that argument in one sentence.

    Show answer

    1. This pattern is the fingerprint of multicollinearity: the joint contribution of the predictors is real (the model beats “none of them contribute”), but no single predictor is uniquely responsible for it. Each is essentially interchangeable with a combination of the others.

    2. Yes, high VIFs are usually the cause. Predictors that carry nearly the same information make each \(t\)-test look at a lonely, individual contribution — and none is discernibly large on its own.

    3. It means the predictors are collectively useful for predicting \(y\) but individual causal interpretation is unreliable. Predictions from the whole model are trustworthy; conclusions like “predictor \(x_1\) specifically caused the effect” are not.

    4. Rejecting the model based on individual \(t\)-tests would throw out a jointly informative model just because its predictors overlap. Better responses: combine collinear predictors, drop one, or accept that predictions are trustworthy even if individual coefficients are not.

2  Introduction to Data

Scientists seek to answer questions using rigorous methods and careful observations. These observations — collected from the likes of field notes, surveys, and experiments — form the backbone of a statistical investigation and are called data. Statistics is the study of how best to collect, analyze, and draw conclusions from data. In this first chapter, we introduce the building blocks of statistical thinking: observations, variables, and data frames.

2.1 Case study: Using stents to prevent strokes

We start with a real example that illustrates both the power and the nuance of statistical analysis.

In a 2003 study, researchers investigated whether implanting a stent (a small mesh tube) in the brain of a patient at risk for a stroke would reduce the risk of a stroke.1

The researchers randomly assigned 451 at-risk patients to one of two groups:

  • Treatment group: Received a stent and medical management (224 patients)
  • Control group: Received medical management alone (227 patients)

The results at 30 days:

Stroke No stroke Total
Treatment 33 191 224
Control 13 214 227

The treatment group had a higher stroke rate (14.7%) than the control group (5.7%). The stent appeared to increase the risk of stroke in the short term — the opposite of what researchers hoped.

Does this study prove that stents cause strokes? What features of the study design might strengthen or weaken this conclusion?2

This example highlights a key principle: a single study’s results can be surprising, and statistics gives us the tools to evaluate whether a result is real or could be due to random chance.

2.2 Observations, variables, and data frames

Every statistical analysis begins with data — a structured collection of information. Let’s establish the vocabulary.

An observation (also called a case or unit) is a single entity in a dataset. A variable is a characteristic that is measured or recorded for each observation. A data frame (or data table) organizes observations as rows and variables as columns.

Consider a dataset of students in an introductory statistics class. Each student is an observation. Variables might include:

  • Age (in years)
  • Major (e.g., Biology, Psychology, Undeclared)
  • GPA
  • Whether the student has taken a statistics course before (Yes/No)

In a data frame, this looks like:

Student Age Major GPA Prior Stats?
1 19 Biology 3.42 No
2 21 Psychology 3.15 Yes
3 18 Undeclared 3.78 No
… … … … …

How many observations are shown? How many variables? Identify each variable as numerical or categorical.3

2.3 Types of variables

Variables come in two fundamental types:

A numerical variable (or quantitative variable) takes on values that are numbers where arithmetic operations (like averaging) make sense. Examples: height, temperature, income, test score.

A categorical variable (or qualitative variable) takes on values that are labels or categories. Examples: eye color, zip code, political party, treatment group.

Numerical variables can be further classified:

  • Continuous: Can take any value in an interval (e.g., height = 172.4 cm)
  • Discrete: Can only take whole-number or countable values (e.g., number of siblings = 3)

Categorical variables can be further classified:

  • Nominal: Categories with no natural ordering (e.g., eye color: blue, brown, green)
  • Ordinal: Categories with a meaningful order (e.g., education level: high school, bachelor’s, graduate)

Zip codes are categorical, not numerical. Even though zip codes are written as numbers, it doesn’t make sense to compute the average zip code. The digits encode geography, not quantity. A good test: does it make sense to add, subtract, or average the values? If not, the variable is categorical.

Classify each variable as continuous numerical, discrete numerical, nominal categorical, or ordinal categorical:

  1. Number of text messages sent per day
  2. Favorite type of music
  3. Temperature in Fahrenheit
  4. Satisfaction rating (1 = very unsatisfied, …, 5 = very satisfied)

2.4 Relationships between variables

Statistics is often about understanding how variables relate to each other. We use specific terminology:

When we suspect that one variable might influence or predict another, we call the potential influencing variable the explanatory variable (or independent variable) and the variable being influenced the response variable (or dependent variable).

For example, in the stent study:

  • Explanatory variable: Treatment group (stent vs. control)
  • Response variable: Whether the patient had a stroke

Identifying one variable as “explanatory” does not necessarily mean it causes changes in the response. Establishing causation requires specific study designs (discussed in Chapter 2).

2.5 Chapter review

2.5.1 Summary

  • Statistics is the science of collecting, analyzing, and drawing conclusions from data.
  • A data frame organizes observations (rows) and variables (columns).
  • Variables are numerical (continuous or discrete) or categorical (nominal or ordinal).
  • Explanatory variables may predict or influence response variables.
  • Even well-designed studies can produce surprising results — statistical tools help us determine whether results are real or due to chance.

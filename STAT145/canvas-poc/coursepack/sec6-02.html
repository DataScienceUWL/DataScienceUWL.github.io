6.2 Inference for Single Means

Key Concepts

  • Find the mean and standard error for a distribution of sample means

  • Recognize when a \(t\)-distribution is an appropriate model for a distribution of sample means

  • Find endpoints and probabilities for a \(t\)-distribution

  • Use a \(t\)-distribution, when appropriate, to compute a confidence interval for a population mean

  • Use a \(t\)-distribution, when appropriate, to test a hypothesis about a population mean

Distribution of a single mean

For quantitative data, the goal is often to learn about a population mean from a sample mean. First, the distribution of all sample means (\(\overline{x}\)) is centered at the population mean (\(\mu\)). In chapter 3, we discussed estimating the standard error of a statistic using a bootstrap distribution, but we can also estimate the standard error for a sample mean by \[\begin{aligned} SE(\overline{x}) = \frac{ \sigma }{ \sqrt { n }} \end{aligned}\] where \(\sigma\) is the population standard deviation, and \(n\) is the sample size. Finally, if the sample size is large enough, the CLT says that the distribution of the sample mean will follow a normal distribution.

We estimate the standard error of \(\overline{x}\) with \(\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}\)

For means, we almost never know the population standard deviation (\(\sigma\)), so we estimate it by using the sample standard deviation (\(s\)). For small samples, \(s\) tends to underestimate \(\sigma\) (i.e. \(s<\sigma\) more often than not). Because of this, when we use \(s\) instead of \(\sigma\), \(\overline{x}\) no longer follows a normal distribution but has a \(t\)-distribution instead.

The \(t\)-distribution

Back in the early 1900s, a man named William Sealy Gosset was working for the Guinness brewery. During his career at Guinness, he was using statistics to select the best yielding varieties of barley for brewing. At the time Guinness had forbidden any of its employees from publishing in scientific journals. So, Gosset published his findings, a new statistical distribution called the \(t\)-distribution under the name Student. The \(t\)-distribution is based on a new parameter called the degrees of freedom (d.f.).

\(t_{n - 1}\) means a \(t\) distribution with \(n - 1\) degrees of freedom

What he found is that provided we have met one of two conditions, then \[\begin{aligned} t = \frac{ \overline{x} - \mu }{ s / \sqrt{n} } \sim t_{n - 1}, \end{aligned}\] or \(t\) follows a \(t\)-distribution with \(n - 1\) degrees of freedom. There are two possible ways you can justify using the \(t\)-distribution for means.

  1. For a large enough sample size (\(n \ge 30\)), you can use the \(t\)-distribution

    To use the \(t\)-distribution, you need either

    • \(n \ge 30\) or

    • a population that is approximately normal

  2. If \(n < 30\), then the \(t\)-distribution only is a good approximation if the population is approximately normal. We can check this by plotting the sample and making sure it appears normal.

Although the \(t\)-distribution looks a lot like the normal distribution in that it is symmetric and bell-shaped, the \(t\)-distribution has heavier tails (and shorter peak) than the normal distribution. This means that \(t\) quantiles will be a bit larger than normal quantiles. This helps offset using \(s\) in place of \(\sigma\). As \(n \rightarrow \infty\), the \(t\)-distribution becomes closer and closer to the standard normal distribution. For example, the quantiles that place 95% in the middle of the distribution for \(t_5\) are \(\pm2.571\), but for \(t_{100}\) they are \(\pm1.984\) which is much closer to the normal values of \(\pm1.96\).

CI for a single mean

We can only use the \(t\)-distribution for confidence intervals if

  • \(n \ge 30\) or

  • the population is approximately normal

Provided that the sample size is large enough (\(n \ge 30\)) or the population is approximately normal, a confidence interval for a population average \(\mu\) can be constructed using \[\begin{aligned} \overline{x} \pm t^* \frac{ s }{ \sqrt{ n }} \end{aligned}\] where \(\overline{x}\) is the sample average and \(t^*\) is the appropriate confidence multiplier from a \(t_{n-1}\) distribution. To find the appropriate \(t^*\) for the confidence interval for a population average, we need to use technology (StatKey, applet, calculator) to find the corresponding endpoints for the confidence interval.

Hypothesis test for a single mean

Remember, \(t_{n - 1}\) means a \(t\) distribution with \(n - 1\) degrees of freedom

Provided that the sample size is large enough (\(n \ge 30\)) or the population is approximately normal, then according to the CLT \[\begin{aligned} t = \frac{ \overline{x} - \mu }{ s / \sqrt{n}} \sim t_{n - 1}. \end{aligned}\]

We can use this fact to conduct a hypothesis test:

Step 1: Check conditions for the test

We can only use the \(t\)-distribution for confidence intervals if

  • \(n \ge 30\) or

  • the population is approximately normal.

Check whether either \(n \ge 30\) or the population is approximately normal (via graph).

Step 2: State the hypotheses

For a single mean, the null hypothesis always has the form
\(H_0: \mu = \mu_0\) where \(\mu_0\) is a number that represents the averages claimed or status quo value. The alternative hypothesis has one of the following three forms: \(H_a: \mu < \mu_0\), \(H_a: \mu > \mu_0\), or \(H_a: \mu \neq \mu_0\).

Step 3: Calculate the test statistic

To conduct the hypothesis test using the CLT, we use a standardized test statistic of \[\begin{aligned} t = \frac{ \overline{x} - \mu_0 }{s / \sqrt{n}} \end{aligned}\] which follows a \(t_{n-1}\) distribution.

Step 4: Find the \(p\)-value

To find the \(p\)-value, you must use technology to find what proportion of the \(t\)-distribution is more extreme than the test statistic (in the direction of the alternative hypothesis). If your alternative is two-sided, then you multiply the one-sided \(p\)-value by 2.

Step 5: State decision and interpret in the context of the problem

  • If \(p\)-value \(< \alpha\), we reject \(H_0\) and have significant evidence to suggest that \(H_a\) is correct.

  • If \(p\)-value \(\ge \alpha\), we fail to reject \(H_0\) and do not have significant evidence to suggest that \(H_a\) is correct.

You should always write your interpretation in the context of the original hypothesis. Sketches to illustrate the \(p\)-value are similar to what we saw for the previous case of the test for a proportion.

Assessing model conditions

The \(t\)-test is robust to lack of normality, but we still need to see if normality is a reasonable assumption.

In the examples above, we’ve either assumed that the population values were normally distributed or had large enough sample sizes to assume normality. These assumptions aren’t always apparent (or known) in research studies. It is important to investigate whether normality is reasonable before performing a statistical test. The \(t\)-test is robust to lack of normality, so when we check this condition we are looking for clear evidence that the distribution is non-normal. If normality is reasonable, we can proceed with a \(t\)-test.

Create a histogram/dot plot or a normal probability plot to assess the normality condition for a \(t\)-test.

There are two primary ways that we can check for normality: histograms/dot plots or normal probability plots. For histograms/dot plots, simply create a histogram/dot plot of the sample data and determine whether normality is reasonable (if the sample data is approximately normal, then normality is reasonable). Normal probability plots are commonly seen when using statistical software (such as SPSS). They are created by first ordering the sample data from smallest to largest then comparing these ordered sample data to percentiles of the normal distribution. This information is plotted on an scatterplot as points. If the resulting graph follows a straight line (that goes through the origin), we can say that the normality assumption is reasonable.

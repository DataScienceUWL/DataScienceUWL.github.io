Problem 1: Verify Stokes' Theorem

Verify that Stokes' Theorem:

\[\iint_S \text{curl } \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{S} = \oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}\]

is true for the vector field \(\mathbf{F}(x, y, z) = \langle -2yz, y, 3x \rangle\), where \(S\) is the part of the paraboloid \(z = 5 - x^2 - y^2\) that lies above the plane \(z = 1\) and \(S\) has upward orientation.

Solution:

Part 1: Compute \(\iint_S \text{curl } \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{S}\)

Step 1: Find curl F.

\[\text{curl } \mathbf{F} = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf{i} & \mathbf{j} & \mathbf{k} \\ \frac{\partial}{\partial x} & \frac{\partial}{\partial y} & \frac{\partial}{\partial z} \\ -2yz & y & 3x \end{vmatrix}\]

\[= \langle 0 - 0, -2y - 3, 0 - (-2z) \rangle = \langle 0, -3 - 2y, 2z \rangle\]

Step 2: On the surface \(z = 5 - x^2 - y^2\):

\[\text{curl } \mathbf{F} = \langle 0, -3 - 2y, 2(5 - x^2 - y^2) \rangle = \langle 0, -3 - 2y, 10 - 2x^2 - 2y^2 \rangle\]

Step 3: The paraboloid intersects \(z = 1\) when \(5 - x^2 - y^2 = 1\), so \(x^2 + y^2 = 4\).

For \(z = g(x,y) = 5 - x^2 - y^2\): \(g_x = -2x\), \(g_y = -2y\).

\[\langle -g_x, -g_y, 1 \rangle = \langle 2x, 2y, 1 \rangle\]

Step 4: Set up and evaluate.

\[\iint_S \text{curl } \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{S} = \iint_D \langle 0, -3-2y, 10-2x^2-2y^2 \rangle \cdot \langle 2x, 2y, 1 \rangle \, dA\]

\[= \iint_D (0 - 6y - 4y^2 + 10 - 2x^2 - 2y^2) \, dA\]

\[= \iint_D (-6y - 4y^2 + 10 - 2x^2 - 2y^2) \, dA\]

Convert to polar coordinates (\(x^2 + y^2 \le 4\)):

\[= \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^2 (-6r\sin\theta - 4r^2\sin^2\theta + 10 - 2r^2) \, r \, dr \, d\theta\]

\[= \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^2 (-6r^2\sin\theta - 4r^3\sin^2\theta + 10r - 2r^3) \, dr \, d\theta\]

The \(\sin\theta\) term integrates to 0 over \([0, 2\pi]\). Use \(\sin^2\theta = \frac{1-\cos 2\theta}{2}\):

\[= \int_0^{2\pi} \left[-4 \cdot \frac{r^4}{4} \cdot \sin^2\theta + 5r^2 - \frac{r^4}{2}\right]_0^2 d\theta\]

\[= \int_0^{2\pi} (-16\sin^2\theta + 20 - 8) \, d\theta = \int_0^{2\pi} (12 - 16\sin^2\theta) \, d\theta\]

\[= \int_0^{2\pi} \left(12 - 16 \cdot \frac{1-\cos 2\theta}{2}\right) d\theta = \int_0^{2\pi} (12 - 8 + 8\cos 2\theta) \, d\theta\]

\[= \int_0^{2\pi} (4 + 8\cos 2\theta) \, d\theta = 4 \cdot 2\pi + 8 \cdot 0 = \boxed{8\pi}\]

Part 2: Compute \(\oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}\)

Step 1: The boundary curve \(C\) is the circle \(x^2 + y^2 = 4\) at height \(z = 1\).

Parameterize: \(\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle 2\cos t, 2\sin t, 1 \rangle\), \(0 \le t \le 2\pi\).

\[d\mathbf{r} = \langle -2\sin t, 2\cos t, 0 \rangle \, dt\]

Step 2: Evaluate \(\mathbf{F}\) on \(C\).

\[\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(t)) = \langle -2(2\sin t)(1), 2\sin t, 3(2\cos t) \rangle = \langle -4\sin t, 2\sin t, 6\cos t \rangle\]

Step 3: Compute the dot product and integrate.

\[\mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \langle -4\sin t, 2\sin t, 6\cos t \rangle \cdot \langle -2\sin t, 2\cos t, 0 \rangle \, dt\]

\[= (8\sin^2 t + 4\sin t\cos t) \, dt\]

\[\oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \int_0^{2\pi} (8\sin^2 t + 4\sin t\cos t) \, dt\]

\[= \int_0^{2\pi} 8 \cdot \frac{1 - \cos 2t}{2} \, dt + \int_0^{2\pi} 2\sin 2t \, dt\]

\[= \int_0^{2\pi} (4 - 4\cos 2t) \, dt + 2 \cdot 0\]

\[= 4 \cdot 2\pi - 4 \cdot 0 = \boxed{8\pi}\]

Conclusion: Both sides equal \(8\pi\), verifying Stokes' Theorem. ✓

Problem 2: Verify the Divergence Theorem

Verify that the Divergence Theorem:

\[\iint_S \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{S} = \iiint_E \text{div } \mathbf{F} \, dV\]

is true for the vector field \(\mathbf{F}(x, y, z) = \langle x, y, z \rangle\), where \(E\) is the unit ball \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 \le 1\).

Solution:

Part 1: Compute \(\iint_S \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{S}\)

Step 1: The surface is the unit sphere \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 1\) with outward orientation.

Parameterize using spherical coordinates with \(\rho = 1\):

\[\mathbf{r}(\phi, \theta) = \langle \cos\theta\sin\phi, \sin\theta\sin\phi, \cos\phi \rangle\]

Step 2: For the unit sphere, \(\mathbf{r}_\phi \times \mathbf{r}_\theta = \sin\phi \cdot \hat{\mathbf{r}}\) where \(\hat{\mathbf{r}}\) is the outward unit normal.

The outward normal vector is \(\sin\phi \langle \cos\theta\sin\phi, \sin\theta\sin\phi, \cos\phi \rangle\).

Step 3: On the unit sphere, \(\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}) = \mathbf{r}\) (the position vector), so:

\[\mathbf{F} \cdot (\mathbf{r}_\phi \times \mathbf{r}_\theta) = \mathbf{r} \cdot (\sin\phi \cdot \mathbf{r}) = \sin\phi \cdot |\mathbf{r}|^2 = \sin\phi \cdot 1 = \sin\phi\]

Step 4: Integrate.

\[\iint_S \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{S} = \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^{\pi} \sin\phi \, d\phi \, d\theta\]

\[= \int_0^{2\pi} d\theta \cdot \int_0^{\pi} \sin\phi \, d\phi = 2\pi \cdot [-\cos\phi]_0^{\pi}\]

\[= 2\pi \cdot (1 - (-1)) = 2\pi \cdot 2 = \boxed{4\pi}\]

Part 2: Compute \(\iiint_E \text{div } \mathbf{F} \, dV\)

Step 1: Find div F.

\[\text{div } \mathbf{F} = \frac{\partial x}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial y}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial z}{\partial z} = 1 + 1 + 1 = 3\]

Step 2: Integrate over the unit ball.

\[\iiint_E 3 \, dV = 3 \iiint_E dV = 3 \cdot V(E) = 3 \cdot \frac{4}{3}\pi(1)^3 = \boxed{4\pi}\]

Conclusion: Both sides equal \(4\pi\), verifying the Divergence Theorem. ✓

Section 16.8: Stokes' Theorem

MTH 310 — Calculus III | Vector Calculus

The Big Picture: Green's Theorem in 3D

In Section 16.4, Green's Theorem connected a line integral around a flat closed curve to a double integral over the flat region it bounds:

\[\oint_C P\,dx + Q\,dy = \iint_D \left(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y}\right) dA\]

Stokes' Theorem generalizes this: replace the flat region by any oriented surface \(S\) in 3D, and replace \(C\) by the (possibly non-planar) boundary curve of \(S\).

Think of the flat disk being lifted and bent into a curved surface like a soap film stretched across a bent wire. The curve \(C\) is the wire; \(S\) is the film.

Green's Theorem is the flat special case of Stokes' Theorem. Stokes' works for any oriented surface in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) whose boundary is a piecewise-smooth closed curve.

Stokes' Theorem

Theorem: Stokes' Theorem

Let \(S\) be an oriented, piecewise-smooth surface bounded by a simple, closed, piecewise-smooth boundary curve \(C\) with positive orientation. Let \(\vec{F}\) be a vector field whose components have continuous partial derivatives on an open region containing \(S\). Then

\[\boxed{\int_C \vec{F} \cdot d\vec{r} \;=\; \iint_S \mathrm{curl}\,\vec{F} \cdot d\vec{S}.}\]

In words: the circulation of \(\vec{F}\) around the boundary curve equals the flux of curl \(\vec{F}\) through the surface.

Reading each piece:

  • \(\displaystyle\int_C \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{r}\) — line integral around the boundary (work / circulation)
  • \(\mathrm{curl} \vec{F} = \nabla\times\vec{F}\) — measures local rotation of the field
  • \(\displaystyle\iint_S \mathrm{curl} \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{S}\) — total curl flux through \(S\)

Stokes': line integral around the edge \(=\) surface integral of the curl over the interior. Exactly the same template as Green's, one dimension lifted.

Orientation: The Right-Hand Rule

For Stokes' Theorem to work, the orientations of \(C\) and \(S\) must be compatible.

Right-hand rule: curl the fingers of your right hand in the direction of travel along \(C\); your thumb points in the direction of the surface normal \(\vec{n}\).

Equivalently: walk along \(C\) in the positive direction with your head pointing along \(\vec{n}\) — the surface is on your left.

Shortcut for graphs. If \(S\) is oriented upward (normal points up), then \(C\) is traversed counterclockwise when viewed from above.

The right-hand rule ties \(C\)'s direction to \(S\)'s normal. Getting it backward flips the sign of the answer — always double-check before computing.

Green's Theorem Is a Special Case of Stokes'

Take \(S\) = a flat region \(D\) in the \(xy\)-plane, oriented upward (\(\vec{n} = \vec{k}\)), with \(\vec{F} = \langle P, Q, 0\rangle\) (no \(z\)-component, components independent of \(z\)).

Curl: Since \(P, Q\) don't depend on \(z\),

\[\mathrm{curl}\,\vec{F} = \left\langle 0,\; 0,\; \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y}\right\rangle.\]

Flux through the flat disk:

\[\iint_S \mathrm{curl}\,\vec{F}\cdot d\vec{S} = \iint_D \langle 0,\,0,\,Q_x - P_y\rangle\cdot\langle 0,\,0,\,1\rangle\,dA = \iint_D (Q_x - P_y)\,dA.\]

And Stokes' gives

\[\oint_C P\,dx + Q\,dy = \iint_D (Q_x - P_y)\,dA,\]

which is exactly Green's Theorem.

Green's (flat \(xy\)-plane) is Stokes' restricted to a horizontal disk. Stokes' opens the same trick up to any oriented surface whose boundary is a simple closed curve.

Quick Check: Identify the Pieces

Let \(S\) be the part of the paraboloid \(z = 9 - x^2 - y^2\) above the \(xy\)-plane, oriented upward. What is the boundary curve \(C\)?

Set \(z = 0\) in the equation of the surface. What shape do you get, and what is its orientation?

Setting \(z = 0\):   \(0 = 9 - x^2 - y^2 \;\Rightarrow\; x^2 + y^2 = 9\). The boundary \(C\) is the circle of radius \(3\) in the \(xy\)-plane.

Since \(S\) is oriented upward, the right-hand rule gives counterclockwise (from above):

\[\vec{r}(t) = \langle 3\cos t,\; 3\sin t,\; 0\rangle, \qquad 0 \le t \le 2\pi.\]

Three things to identify for every Stokes' problem: the boundary \(C\), its orientation (from the right-hand rule), and a parametrization.

Example 1: Line Integral via Stokes' Theorem

Example 1

Let \(\vec{F}(x,y,z) = \langle 2y\cos z,\; e^x\sin z,\; x\,e^y\rangle\), and let \(C\) be the circle \(x^2+y^2=9\) in the plane \(z=0\), oriented counterclockwise. Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int_C \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{r}\).

Direct parametrization of \(C\) gives a messy \(e^{3\sin t}\) term. Use Stokes' with the simplest surface bounded by \(C\) — the flat disk in the \(xy\)-plane.

Parametrize \(C\). \(\vec{r}(t) = \langle 3\cos t, 3\sin t, 0\rangle\), \(\vec{r} '(t) = \langle -3\sin t, 3\cos t, 0\rangle\).

On \(C\) we have \(z=0\), so \(\cos z = 1\), \(\sin z = 0\), and

\[\vec{F}(\vec{r}(t)) = \langle 2(3\sin t)(1),\; e^{3\cos t}(0),\; 3\cos t\,e^{3\sin t}\rangle = \langle 6\sin t,\; 0,\; 3\cos t\,e^{3\sin t}\rangle.\]

Dot product (the \(e^{3\sin t}\) term multiplies the zero third component of \(\vec{r} '\), so it drops out):

\[\vec{F}\cdot\vec{r}\,'(t) = (6\sin t)(-3\sin t) + 0 + 0 = -18\sin^2 t.\]

Integrate:

\[\int_C \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{r} = \int_0^{2\pi} -18\sin^2 t\,dt = -18\cdot\pi = \boxed{-18\pi}.\]

The nasty-looking \(e^{3\sin t}\) and \(e^x\sin z\) terms evaporate as soon as we set \(z=0\) on \(C\). Stokes' didn't do the work here — but recognizing that \(C\) is in the \(xy\)-plane simplified \(\vec{F}\) dramatically.

The Power of Stokes': Choose the Easier Side

Stokes' gives us a choice: compute the line integral side or the surface integral side — whichever is simpler.

Compute the line integral when\ldots

  • \(C\) has a simple parametrization (circle, line segment)
  • \(\vec{F}\) simplifies on \(C\) (e.g., \(z = 0\) kills exponentials)
  • \(C\) is a single curve, not many pieces

Compute the surface integral when\ldots

  • \(\mathrm{curl} \vec{F}\) is simple
  • \(S\) has a clean normal (plane, paraboloid, graph)
  • \(C\) has multiple pieces (triangle of edges, etc.)

Bonus freedom: the same curve \(C\) bounds many surfaces. Pick the surface that makes \(\iint_S \mathrm{curl} \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{S}\) easiest.

Stokes' is a computational tool: convert a hard integral into an easy one. The surface integral depends only on the curl and the boundary \(C\), not on which surface filling you pick.

Example 2: Line Integral via Stokes'

Example 2

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int_C \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{r}\) where \(\vec{F} = \langle 1,\; x+yz,\; xy - \sqrt{z}\rangle\) and \(C\) is the triangular boundary of the part of the plane \(3x + 2y + z = 1\) in the first octant, oriented counterclockwise from above.

Computing the line integral directly means three separate segment integrals — painful. Use Stokes' with \(S\) = the triangular piece of the plane. Compute curl first.

Step 1: Compute curl \(\vec{F}\).

\[\mathrm{curl}\,\vec{F} = \langle (xy-\sqrt{z})_y - (x+yz)_z,\; (1)_z - (xy-\sqrt{z})_x,\; (x+yz)_x - (1)_y\rangle\]

\[= \langle x - y,\; -y,\; 1\rangle.\]

Step 2: Set up the surface. \(S\): \(z = g(x,y) = 1 - 3x - 2y\) over the triangle \(D = \{x\ge 0,\;y\ge 0,\;3x+2y\le 1\}\). Upward: \(d\vec{S} = \langle -g_x, -g_y, 1\rangle\,dA = \langle 3, 2, 1\rangle\,dA\).

Step 3: Dot product.

\[\mathrm{curl}\,\vec{F}\cdot\langle 3,2,1\rangle = 3(x-y) + 2(-y) + 1 = 3x - 5y + 1.\]

Step 4: Integrate over the triangle. For fixed \(x \in [0, 1/3]\), \(y\) runs \(0\) to \((1-3x)/2\):

\[\int_0^{(1-3x)/2}(3x - 5y + 1)\,dy = \left[(3x+1)y - \tfrac{5}{2}y^2\right]_0^{(1-3x)/2} = (3x+1)\tfrac{1-3x}{2} - \tfrac{5}{8}(1-3x)^2.\]

Simplify by letting \(a = 1-3x\) so \(3x+1 = 2-a\):

\[= \tfrac{(2-a)a}{2} - \tfrac{5a^2}{8} = \tfrac{4a(2-a) - 5a^2}{8} = \tfrac{8a - 9a^2}{8}.\]

Outer integral with \(a = 1-3x\), \(da = -3\,dx\):

\[\int_0^{1/3}\frac{8a - 9a^2}{8}\,dx = \frac{1}{24}\int_0^1 (8a - 9a^2)\,da = \frac{1}{24}\bigl[4a^2 - 3a^3\bigr]_0^1 = \frac{4 - 3}{24} = \boxed{\tfrac{1}{24}}.\]

Three line segments \(\to\) one surface integral on a flat plane. When the boundary has multiple pieces but the curl and surface are simple, Stokes' saves huge effort.

Quick Check: Compute the Curl

Let \(\vec{F} = \langle yz,\; xz,\; xy\rangle\). Compute \(\mathrm{curl} \vec{F}\).

Formula: \(\mathrm{curl} \vec{F} = \langle R_y - Q_z,\; P_z - R_x,\; Q_x - P_y\rangle\) where \(\vec{F} = \langle P,Q,R\rangle\).

\(P = yz\), \(Q = xz\), \(R = xy\):

\[\mathrm{curl}\,\vec{F} = \langle x-x,\; y-y,\; z-z\rangle = \vec{0}.\]

The curl is zero, so \(\vec{F}\) is irrotational. By Stokes' Theorem,

\[\oint_C \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{r} = \iint_S \vec{0}\cdot d\vec{S} = 0 \qquad\text{for any closed curve $C$.}\]

\(\mathrm{curl} \vec{F} = \vec{0}\) on a simply connected region \(\Rightarrow\) every closed-loop line integral vanishes. This is how Stokes' delivers the 3D version of “conservative \(\Leftrightarrow\) zero circulation.”

Stokes' and Conservative Fields (3D)

Stokes' finally proves the conservative-field theorem we stated without proof in Section 16.5.

Recall (16.5): if \(\vec{F} = \nabla f\), then \(\mathrm{curl}(\nabla f) = \vec{0}\).

Converse (stated, not proved before): if \(\mathrm{curl} \vec{F} = \vec{0}\) on a simply connected region, then \(\vec{F}\) is conservative.

Proof via Stokes'. If \(\mathrm{curl} \vec{F} = \vec{0}\) throughout the region and \(C\) is any closed curve bounding a surface \(S\) in that region:

\[\oint_C \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{r} = \iint_S \mathrm{curl}\,\vec{F}\cdot d\vec{S} = \iint_S \vec{0}\cdot d\vec{S} = 0.\]

Every closed-loop integral is zero, so \(\vec{F}\) is path-independent — i.e., conservative.

The full 3D equivalence on a simply connected domain:

\[\vec{F}\text{ conservative}\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\mathrm{curl}\,\vec{F} = \vec{0}\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\oint_C \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{r} = 0\text{ for all closed }C.\]

“Simply connected” in 3D means every closed curve bounds some surface inside the region. That's what lets Stokes' close the loop back to a conservative potential.

Example 3: Choosing a Better Surface

Example 3

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\oint_C \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{r}\) where \(\vec{F} = \langle -y^2,\; x,\; z^2\rangle\) and \(C\) is the intersection of the plane \(y+z = 2\) with the cylinder \(x^2+y^2 = 1\), oriented counterclockwise from above.

\(C\) is a tilted ellipse — parametrizing it directly is awkward. Use Stokes' with \(S\) = the flat piece of the plane inside the cylinder.

Curl. \(\mathrm{curl} \vec{F} = \langle 0,  0,  1-(-2y)\rangle = \langle 0,  0,  1+2y\rangle\).

Surface. \(z = g(x,y) = 2 - y\) over the disk \(D: x^2+y^2 \le 1\). Upward: \(d\vec{S} = \langle 0, 1, 1\rangle\,dA\).

Dot product.

\[\mathrm{curl}\,\vec{F}\cdot\langle 0,1,1\rangle = 0 + 0 + (1+2y) = 1 + 2y.\]

Integrate over the unit disk. By symmetry, \(\iint_D 2y\,dA = 0\) (odd in \(y\) on a symmetric disk), leaving

\[\iint_D (1 + 2y)\,dA = \iint_D 1\,dA = \mathrm{Area}(D) = \pi \cdot 1^2 = \boxed{\pi}.\]

Two wins: (1) flat-plane surface made the integral trivial, (2) odd-function symmetry killed the \(2y\) term. \(C\) also bounds a cylindrical piece and a cap of a sphere — any of them would give the same \(\pi\), but the plane is fastest.

Quick Check: Flat-Disk Shortcut

Let \(\vec{F} = \langle z, x, y\rangle\) and \(C\) = unit circle in the \(xy\)-plane, counterclockwise. Evaluate \(\displaystyle\oint_C \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{r}\).

Use Stokes' with \(S\) = unit disk in the \(xy\)-plane. Only the \(\vec{k}\)-component of the curl survives the dot with \(\vec{n} = \vec{k}\).

\(\mathrm{curl} \vec{F} = \langle 1, 1, 1\rangle\).

(Check: \(R_y - Q_z = 1 - 0 = 1\), \(P_z - R_x = 1 - 0 = 1\), \(Q_x - P_y = 1 - 0 = 1\).)

On \(S\) (flat disk, \(\vec{n} = \vec{k}\)):

\[\iint_S \mathrm{curl}\,\vec{F}\cdot d\vec{S} = \iint_D \langle 1,1,1\rangle\cdot\langle 0,0,1\rangle\,dA = \iint_D 1\,dA = \pi.\]

When \(C\) lies in a coordinate plane, pick the flat disk. Only one component of curl matters — the one parallel to the normal. This is literally the Green's-Theorem integrand.

Example 4: Surface Integral via Line Integral

Example 4

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\iint_S \mathrm{curl} \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{S}\) where \(\vec{F} = \langle xz,\; yz,\; xy\rangle\) and \(S\) is the part of the sphere \(x^2+y^2+z^2 = 4\) inside the cylinder \(x^2+y^2 = 1\) with \(z\ge 0\), oriented upward.

The surface is a complicated spherical cap. But its boundary is a simple circle. Use Stokes' in the reverse direction: compute the line integral instead.

Boundary. \(C\): \(x^2+y^2 = 1\) at height \(z = \sqrt{4-1} = \sqrt{3}\).

\[\vec{r}(t) = \langle\cos t,\;\sin t,\;\sqrt{3}\rangle,\quad \vec{r}\,'(t) = \langle -\sin t,\;\cos t,\; 0\rangle,\quad 0\le t\le 2\pi.\]

\(\vec{F}\) on \(C\). \(\vec{F}(\vec{r}(t)) = \langle \sqrt{3}\cos t,\;\sqrt{3}\sin t,\;\cos t\sin t\rangle\).

Dot product (the \(z\)-component of \(\vec{F}\) multiplies the zero \(z\)-component of \(\vec{r} '\) and drops out):

\[\vec{F}\cdot\vec{r}\,' = -\sqrt{3}\sin t\cos t + \sqrt{3}\sin t\cos t + 0 = 0.\]

\[\iint_S \mathrm{curl}\,\vec{F}\cdot d\vec{S} = \int_C \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{r} = \int_0^{2\pi} 0\,dt = \boxed{0}.\]

Stokes' in reverse: a painful-looking surface integral collapsed to a two-line computation on a circle. Always check both sides before committing to a calculation.

Physical Interpretation: Circulation and Curl

Stokes' has a clean fluid-flow interpretation.

Left side: \(\displaystyle\oint_C \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{r}\) is the circulation of \(\vec{F}\) around \(C\) — the net tendency of the flow to follow the curve.

Right side: \(\displaystyle\iint_S \mathrm{curl} \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{S}\) sums the local rotation across \(S\). The curl at each point measures how a tiny paddlewheel there would spin.

Stokes' says: total circulation around the boundary \(=\) sum of all tiny local rotations inside. The paddlewheel spinning adds up to the bulk circulation.

Curl \(=\) microscopic circulation density. Stokes' integrates that local density over \(S\) to produce the macroscopic circulation around \(C\).

Section 16.8 Summary

Stokes' Theorem

\(\displaystyle\int_C \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{r} = \iint_S \mathrm{curl} \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{S}\). Circulation around the boundary \(=\) flux of curl through the surface.

Green's \(\Rightarrow\) Stokes'

When \(S\) is a flat region in the \(xy\)-plane with \(\vec{F} = \langle P,Q,0\rangle\), Stokes' collapses to Green's Theorem \(\oint_C P\,dx+Q\,dy = \iint_D(Q_x - P_y)\,dA\).

Orientation

Right-hand rule: curl fingers along \(C\) in its direction of travel, thumb points along \(\vec{n}\). Reversing either flips the sign.

Computational Strategy

Pick whichever side is easier. Any surface with boundary \(C\) gives the same flux — choose the simplest one (usually a flat disk or plane piece).

Conservative Fields

\(\mathrm{curl} \vec{F} = \vec{0}\) on a simply connected region \(\Rightarrow\) every closed-loop integral of \(\vec{F}\) is zero \(\Rightarrow\) \(\vec{F}\) is conservative. This is the proof we deferred from 16.5.

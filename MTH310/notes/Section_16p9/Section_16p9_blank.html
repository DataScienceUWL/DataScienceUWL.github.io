One Last Theorem in the Family

We've built a chain of theorems throughout this course, each relating a boundary integral to an interior integral:

Today: the Divergence Theorem, connecting a surface integral (flux through a closed surface) to a triple integral (divergence over the enclosed solid). It is the 3D analogue of Green's Theorem in flux form.

Every major theorem in Chapter 16 has the same shape: \(\displaystyle\int_{\text{boundary}} (\text{something}) = \int_{\text{interior}}(\text{derivative of something})\). The Divergence Theorem is the final version.

The Divergence Theorem

Theorem: The Divergence Theorem (Gauss's Theorem)

Let \(E\) be a simple solid region, and let \(S\) be its boundary surface, oriented outward. Let \(\vec{F}\) be a vector field whose components have continuous partial derivatives on an open region containing \(E\). Then

\[\boxed{\iint_S \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{S} \;=\; \iiint_E \mathrm{div}\,\vec{F}\; dV.}\]

In components, with \(\vec{F} = \langle P, Q, R\rangle\):

\[\iint_S \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{S} = \iiint_E \!\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial Q}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial R}{\partial z}\right) dV.\]

Reading the two sides:

  • Left: total flux of \(\vec{F}\) outward through the closed surface \(S\).
  • Right: total divergence of \(\vec{F}\) throughout the solid \(E\).

Flux through the boundary \(=\) total source strength inside. If \(\mathrm{div} \vec{F} > 0\) inside \(E\), net outward flux (source). If \(\mathrm{div} \vec{F} < 0\), net inward flux (sink).

Physical Interpretation: Sources and Sinks

Think of \(\vec{F}\) as the velocity field of a fluid. The Divergence Theorem says net outflow \(=\) total internal source strength:

\[\underbrace{\iint_S \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{S}}_{\text{net flow out through }S} \;=\; \underbrace{\iiint_E \mathrm{div}\,\vec{F}\; dV}_{\text{total source/sink inside }E}.\]

Divergence is the local expansion rate of the field. The theorem sums all those local expansions to produce the net boundary outflow.

Connection to Green's Theorem (Flux Form)

Recall the flux form of Green's Theorem (16.4):

\[\oint_C \vec{F}\cdot\vec{n}\,ds = \iint_D \mathrm{div}\,\vec{F}\,dA \qquad (\vec{F} = \langle P,Q\rangle,\; \text{2D}).\]

The Divergence Theorem is the same statement, one dimension up:

Green's (flux form) is the 2D Divergence Theorem. Every theorem in Chapter 16 follows one pattern: integral over boundary \(=\) integral of a derivative over interior.

Quick Check: Compute the Divergence

Compute \(\mathrm{div} \vec{F}\) for each field:

  1. \(\vec{F} = \langle x^2, xy, z\rangle\)
  2. \(\vec{F} = \langle x^3 + y^3,\; y^3 + z^3,\; z^3 + x^3\rangle\)
  3. \(\vec{F} = \langle yz, xz, xy\rangle\)
Recall: \(\mathrm{div} \vec{F} = P_x + Q_y + R_z\).

Example 1: Flux Through a Paraboloid Region

Example 1

Use the Divergence Theorem to find the flux of \(\vec{F} = \langle x^2, xy, z\rangle\) outward through the closed surface \(S\) bounding the solid \(E\) below \(z = 4 - x^2 - y^2\) and above \(z = 0\).

Computing the flux directly is painful: the boundary \(S\) has two pieces (paraboloid cap + flat disk below), each needing its own parametrization. Use the Divergence Theorem to replace it with one triple integral over \(E\). Cylindrical coordinates work here.

Quick Check: Which Side Is Easier?

For each scenario, decide which side of the Divergence Theorem is easier to compute.

  1. \(\vec{F} = \langle x, y, z\rangle\), \(S\) is a complicated surface enclosing a solid of known volume.
  2. \(\mathrm{div} \vec{F}\) is a messy expression, but \(S\) is a single simple surface.
  3. \(\vec{F} = \langle xz^2, x^2 y, y^2 z\rangle\), \(E\) is the unit cube \([0,1]^3\).
Ask: is the divergence simple? Is the surface a single piece? Is the volume easy to compute?

Example 2: Flux Through a Sphere

Example 2

Use the Divergence Theorem to find the flux of \(\vec{F} = \langle x^3 + y^3,\; y^3 + z^3,\; z^3 + x^3\rangle\) outward through the sphere \(S\) of radius \(2\) centered at the origin.

The divergence is \(3(x^2+y^2+z^2) = 3\rho^2\) in spherical coordinates — a gift. Set up the triple integral over the ball.

Example 3: Flux Through a Cube

Example 3

Find the flux of \(\vec{F} = \langle xy, y^2, yz\rangle\) outward through the surface of the cube \([0,1]^3\).

The cube has SIX faces. Direct computation means six separate surface integrals, each with its own outward normal. Use the Divergence Theorem.

Quick Check: The Radial Field Through a Sphere

Let \(\vec{F} = \langle x, y, z\rangle\) and \(S\) be the unit sphere. Find \(\displaystyle\iint_S \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{S}\) without parametrizing anything.

Compute \(\mathrm{div} \vec{F}\). If it's a constant \(c\), the flux is just \(c\cdot\mathrm{Vol}(E)\).

When NOT to Use the Divergence Theorem

The theorem requires a closed surface enclosing a solid with \(\vec{F}\) smooth throughout. Common pitfalls:

1. Open surface. E.g., a hemisphere with no disk on the bottom. Fix: add the missing piece, apply the theorem to the closed surface, subtract the flux through the added piece.

2. Singularity inside \(E\). E.g., \(\vec{F} = \vec{r}/|\vec{r}|^3\) with the origin inside. Fix: cut out a small sphere around the singularity and treat the region as topologically a shell.

3. Non-simple regions (cavities or holes). Fix: decompose into simple pieces.

Checklist before applying the theorem: (i) Is \(S\) closed? (ii) Is \(\vec{F}\) smooth throughout \(E\)? (iii) Is \(S\) oriented outward? Failure on any one means the theorem needs adaptation.

The Big Picture: Four Theorems, One Pattern

Every major theorem in vector calculus is a version of:

\[\int_{\text{boundary}} (\text{something}) \;=\; \int_{\text{interior}} (\text{derivative of something}).\]

FTC, Green's, Stokes', Divergence are not four separate theorems — they are the same theorem in different dimensions.

Which Theorem? A Decision Tree

Decision tree for integrals in Chapter 16:

  1. \(\vec{F}\) conservative? \(\Rightarrow\) FTC for line integrals: find \(f\), evaluate at endpoints.
  2. Line integral around a closed curve in the plane? \(\Rightarrow\) Green's.
  3. Line integral around a closed curve in 3D? \(\Rightarrow\) Stokes'.
  4. Flux through a closed surface in 3D? \(\Rightarrow\) Divergence.

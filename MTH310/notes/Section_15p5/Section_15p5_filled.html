Section 15.5: Surface Area

MTH 310 — Calculus III | Multiple Integrals

Why Surface Area Isn't Just \(\iint 1\, dA\)

In Section 15.4, we computed the area of a flat region \(D\) in the \(xy\)-plane using \(\iint_D 1\, dA\). Can we use the same formula for a curved surface?

No! Think of draping a bedsheet over a tent pole. The sheet covers more area than its shadow on the floor. A tilted or curved surface has more area than its projection onto the \(xy\)-plane.

How much more area? That depends on how steep the surface is. The steeper the tilt, the more surface area is packed above each little patch of the \(xy\)-plane.

The idea is the same as arc length in Calc I: a tilted line segment is longer than its horizontal projection by a factor of \(\sqrt{1 + (f')^2}\). In Calc III, a tilted surface patch is bigger than its shadow by a factor of \(\sqrt{f_x^2 + f_y^2 + 1}\).

Surface area = \(\iint_D (\text{correction factor})\, dA\), where the correction factor accounts for the tilt of the surface above each point.

Approximating Surface Area with Tangent Plane Patches

How do we find the correction factor? The same way we derive most integral formulas — approximate and add up.

Step 1: Divide the domain \(D\) into small rectangles of area \(\Delta A = \Delta x \cdot \Delta y\).

Step 2: Above each rectangle, the surface \(z = f(x,y)\) is approximately a tilted parallelogram — a piece of the tangent plane.

Step 3: The tangent plane at a point has two direction vectors:

\[\vec{a} = \langle 1, 0, f_x \rangle \Delta x \qquad \vec{b} = \langle 0, 1, f_y \rangle \Delta y\]

Step 4: The area of the parallelogram is \(|\vec{a} \times \vec{b}|\):

\[\vec{a} \times \vec{b} = \langle -f_x, -f_y, 1 \rangle \Delta x \, \Delta y\]

\[|\vec{a} \times \vec{b}| = \sqrt{f_x^2 + f_y^2 + 1} \, \Delta x \, \Delta y\]

\(\sqrt{f_x^2 + f_y^2 + 1}\) is the magnitude of the normal vector \(\langle -f_x, -f_y, 1 \rangle\) to the surface. Steeper surface \(\to\) bigger normal vector \(\to\) bigger correction factor.

The Surface Area Formula

Summing up the parallelogram areas and taking the limit gives us the integral formula.

Surface Area

The area of the surface \(z = f(x,y)\) over a region \(D\), where \(f_x\) and \(f_y\) are continuous, is

\[A(S) = \iint_D \sqrt{\left[f_x(x,y)\right]^2 + \left[f_y(x,y)\right]^2 + 1} \, dA\]

Sanity check: If \(f\) is constant (a flat horizontal surface), then \(f_x = 0\) and \(f_y = 0\), so the integrand is \(\sqrt{0 + 0 + 1} = 1\), and we get \(A(S) = \iint_D 1\, dA = \) area of \(D\). That's exactly right — a flat surface has the same area as its shadow!

The formula \(\sqrt{f_x^2 + f_y^2 + 1}\) always gives a value \(\geq 1\), so the surface area is always at least as large as the area of \(D\). Equality only when the surface is perfectly flat.

Quick Check: Surface Area of a Flat Surface

You have the surface \(z = 3\) (a horizontal plane) over the unit disk \(x^2 + y^2 \leq 1\).

What is the surface area?

Compute \(f_x\) and \(f_y\) for \(f(x,y) = 3\). Plug into the surface area formula. What does the integral simplify to?

\(f(x,y) = 3\), so \(f_x = 0\) and \(f_y = 0\).

\[A(S) = \iint_D \sqrt{0 + 0 + 1}\, dA = \iint_D 1\, dA = \text{area of the unit disk} = \pi\]

For a flat surface, the surface area formula just gives the area of the domain. The formula only gets interesting when the surface is tilted or curved.

Example 1: Plane Inside a Cylinder

Example 1

Find the surface area of the part of the plane \(2x + 5y + z = 10\) that lies inside the cylinder \(x^2 + y^2 = 9\).

Solve for \(z = f(x,y)\), compute the partials, and set up the integral over the disk.

Solve for \(z\): \(z = f(x,y) = 10 - 2x - 5y\)

Partial derivatives: \(f_x = -2\), \(\quad f_y = -5\)

The integrand is constant!

\[\sqrt{f_x^2 + f_y^2 + 1} = \sqrt{4 + 25 + 1} = \sqrt{30}\]

The domain \(D\) is the disk \(x^2 + y^2 \leq 9\).

\[A(S) = \iint_D \sqrt{30}\, dA = \sqrt{30} \cdot (\text{area of } D) = \sqrt{30} \cdot \pi(3)^2 = 9\pi\sqrt{30}\]

When the surface is a plane, the integrand is constant, so the surface area is just (correction factor) \(\times\) (area of domain). No integration needed!

Example 2: Area of a Spherical Cap

Example 2

Find the surface area of the part of the sphere \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 4\) that lies above the plane \(z = 1\).

Solve for \(z\), find \(f_x\) and \(f_y\), simplify the integrand, then switch to polar.

Solve for the upper hemisphere: \(z = f(x,y) = \sqrt{4 - x^2 - y^2}\)

Partial derivatives:

\[f_x = \frac{-x}{\sqrt{4 - x^2 - y^2}}, \qquad f_y = \frac{-y}{\sqrt{4 - x^2 - y^2}}\]

Simplify the integrand:

\[f_x^2 + f_y^2 + 1 = \frac{x^2 + y^2}{4 - x^2 - y^2} + 1 = \frac{4}{4 - x^2 - y^2}\]

So \(\sqrt{f_x^2 + f_y^2 + 1} = \dfrac{2}{\sqrt{4 - x^2 - y^2}}\)

Find the domain: \(z \geq 1\) means \(\sqrt{4 - x^2 - y^2} \geq 1\), so \(x^2 + y^2 \leq 3\).

Switch to polar:

\[A(S) = \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^{\sqrt{3}} \frac{2}{\sqrt{4 - r^2}} \cdot r \, dr \, d\theta\]

Inner integral: Let \(u = 4 - r^2\), \(\;du = -2r\, dr\):

\[\int_0^{\sqrt{3}} \frac{2r}{\sqrt{4 - r^2}}\, dr = \left[-2\sqrt{4 - r^2}\right]_0^{\sqrt{3}} = -2(1) + 2(2) = 2\]

\[A(S) = \int_0^{2\pi} 2 \, d\theta = 4\pi\]

The sphere's integrand \(\frac{2}{\sqrt{4-r^2}}\) blows up as \(r \to 2\) (the equator), but our domain stops at \(r = \sqrt{3}\), so the integral is perfectly well-behaved. We'll deal with that boundary singularity in Example 3.

Quick Check: Set Up a Surface Area Integral

Consider the paraboloid \(z = x^2 + y^2\) over the unit disk \(x^2 + y^2 \leq 1\).

Set up (but don't evaluate) the surface area integral in polar coordinates.

Compute \(f_x\) and \(f_y\), form the integrand, then convert to polar.

\(f(x,y) = x^2 + y^2\), so \(f_x = 2x\) and \(f_y = 2y\).

\[\sqrt{f_x^2 + f_y^2 + 1} = \sqrt{4x^2 + 4y^2 + 1} = \sqrt{4r^2 + 1}\]

\[A(S) = \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^1 \sqrt{4r^2 + 1} \cdot r \, dr \, d\theta\]

The integrand \(\sqrt{4r^2 + 1}\) is largest near \(r = 1\) (the rim), where the paraboloid is steepest. The surface area is concentrated where the surface tilts the most.

Example 3: Why \(A(\text{sphere}) = 4\pi a^2\)

Example 3

Prove that the surface area of a sphere of radius \(a\) is \(4\pi a^2\).

Strategy: Find the area of the upper hemisphere \(z = \sqrt{a^2 - x^2 - y^2}\) and double it. But there's a catch — the integrand blows up at the equator!

Set up the integrand for \(z = \sqrt{a^2 - x^2 - y^2}\) over the disk \(x^2 + y^2 \leq a^2\). Where does the integrand have trouble?

From Example 2's pattern: For the sphere \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = a^2\):

\[\sqrt{f_x^2 + f_y^2 + 1} = \frac{a}{\sqrt{a^2 - x^2 - y^2}}\]

The problem: At the equator (\(x^2 + y^2 = a^2\)), the denominator is zero. The sphere is vertical there, so the surface is infinitely steep — the integrand blows up!

Solution: Treat this as an improper integral. Integrate over \(x^2 + y^2 \leq t^2\) (where \(t < a\)) and take \(\lim_{t \to a^-}\).

Step 1: Area of the spherical cap inside \(x^2 + y^2 = t^2\) (with \(t < a\)):

\[A_{\text{cap}} = \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^{t} \frac{a}{\sqrt{a^2 - r^2}} \cdot r \, dr \, d\theta\]

Inner integral: Let \(u = a^2 - r^2\), \(\;du = -2r\, dr\):

\[\int_0^{t} \frac{ar}{\sqrt{a^2 - r^2}} \, dr = \left[-a\sqrt{a^2 - r^2}\right]_0^{t} = -a\sqrt{a^2 - t^2} + a \cdot a = a^2 - a\sqrt{a^2 - t^2}\]

\[A_{\text{cap}} = 2\pi\left(a^2 - a\sqrt{a^2 - t^2}\right)\]

Step 2: Take the limit for the full hemisphere:

\[A_{\text{hemisphere}} = \lim_{t \to a^-} 2\pi\left(a^2 - a\sqrt{a^2 - t^2}\right) = 2\pi\left(a^2 - 0\right) = 2\pi a^2\]

Step 3: The full sphere has twice this area:

\[A_{\text{sphere}} = 2 \cdot 2\pi a^2 = 4\pi a^2 \qquad \checkmark\]

The improper integral is essential here. The surface area of a sphere is finite even though the integrand is infinite at the equator — the singularity is ``integrable.'' This is the same phenomenon as \(\int_0^1 x^{-1/2}\, dx = 2\) being finite.

Section 15.5 Summary

The Key Idea

A tilted surface has more area than its shadow on the \(xy\)-plane. The correction factor \(\sqrt{f_x^2 + f_y^2 + 1}\) accounts for the tilt.

The Formula

\(A(S) = \displaystyle\iint_D \sqrt{f_x^2 + f_y^2 + 1}\, dA\). The integrand is the magnitude of the normal vector \(\langle -f_x, -f_y, 1 \rangle\).

Plane Shortcut

For a plane, the integrand is constant, so \(A = \sqrt{f_x^2 + f_y^2 + 1} \cdot (\text{area of } D)\). No integration needed!

Polar Coordinates

When \(D\) is a disk (or part of one), switch to polar: \(dA = r\, dr\, d\theta\).

Sphere Area

\(A = 4\pi a^2\), proved via improper integral. The integrand blows up at the equator but the integral converges.

Surface Integrals: Two Flavors in Parallel

In Section 16.2 we defined two kinds of line integrals — one for scalar functions, one for vector fields. Now we lift each of them up a dimension.

Scalar versionVector version
Line integral (1D object)\(\displaystyle\int_C f\,ds\)\(\displaystyle\int_C \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{r} = \int_C \vec{F}\cdot\vec{T}\,ds\)
weighted byarc length \(ds\)tangent component \(\vec{F}\cdot\vec{T}\)
measurestotal of \(f\) along \(C\)flow along \(C\) (work)
Surface integral (2D object)\(\displaystyle\iint_S f\,dS\)\(\displaystyle\iint_S \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{S} = \iint_S \vec{F}\cdot\vec{n}\,dS\)
weighted bysurface area \(dS\)normal component \(\vec{F}\cdot\vec{n}\)
measurestotal of \(f\) over \(S\)flow through \(S\) (flux)

The shift: for curves, “flow” meant tangential (along the curve). For surfaces, “flow” means perpendicular (through the surface). That's why flux uses \(\vec{n}\) instead of \(\vec{T}\).

Each line integral from 16.2 has a surface-integral twin. Scalar \(\leftrightarrow\) scalar (\(ds \to dS\)); vector (work) \(\leftrightarrow\) vector (flux) (\(\vec{T}\to \vec{n}\)).

Put this table on the board in a corner for the whole section — students can always map back from “where are we now” to the line-integral version they already know.

Scalar Surface Integrals: The Idea

Imagine a thin metal sheet shaped like a surface \(S\), with density \(\rho(x,y,z)\) at each point. The total mass is:

\[\text{mass} = \iint_S \rho(x,y,z)\,dS\]

More generally, \(\iint_S f(x,y,z)\,dS\) adds up the values of \(f\) over the surface, weighted by area.

Riemann-sum construction (same recipe as every integral so far):

  1. Chop \(S\) into small patches \(\Delta S_i\)
  2. Sample a point \((x_i^*, y_i^*, z_i^*)\) on each patch
  3. Sum: \(\displaystyle\sum_i f(x_i^*, y_i^*, z_i^*) \Delta S_i\)
  4. Limit as the patches shrink: \(\displaystyle\iint_S f\,dS = \lim \sum_i f(x_i^*, y_i^*, z_i^*) \Delta S_i\)

Parallel with line integrals:

  • \(\int_C f\,ds\): chop \(C\) into arcs, multiply by \(f\), sum arc-length-weighted values.
  • \(\iint_S f\,dS\): chop \(S\) into patches, multiply by \(f\), sum area-weighted values.

\(\iint_S f\,dS\) is the surface analog of \(\int_C f\,ds\). Replace “arc-length element \(ds\)” with “surface-area element \(dS\)” — everything else is the same.

Computing \(\iint_S f\,dS\) from a Parametrization

From Section 16.6, if \(S\) is parametrized by \(\vec{r}(u,v)\) over domain \(D\), then

\[dS = |\vec{r}_u \times \vec{r}_v|\,dA.\]

Scalar Surface Integral (Parametric Form)

\[\iint_S f(x,y,z)\,dS = \iint_D f\!\left(\vec{r}(u,v)\right)\;|\vec{r}_u \times \vec{r}_v|\;dA\]

Side by side with the line-integral formula:

Line integral (16.2):

\[\int_C f\,ds = \int_a^b f\!\left(\vec{r}(t)\right)\,|\vec{r}\,'(t)|\;dt\]

Chop a 1D parameter interval; “speed” \(|\vec{r} '(t)|\) converts \(dt\) to \(ds\).

Surface integral (16.7):

\[\iint_S f\,dS = \iint_D f\!\left(\vec{r}(u,v)\right)\,|\vec{r}_u \times \vec{r}_v|\;dA\]

Chop a 2D parameter region; “stretch factor” \(|\vec{r}_u\times\vec{r}_v|\) converts \(dA\) to \(dS\).

Recipe: (1) parametrize \(S\) as \(\vec{r}(u,v)\), (2) compute \(|\vec{r}_u \times \vec{r}_v|\), (3) substitute into a double integral over \(D\). Exactly like a line integral, one dimension up.

The cross-product magnitude plays the role of \(|\vec{r} '(t)|\). Students who internalize this analogy can derive the formula instead of memorizing it.

Special Case: Surface Integral over a Graph \(z = g(x,y)\)

When \(S\) is a graph \(z = g(x,y)\), parametrize with \(x\) and \(y\) directly: \(\vec{r}(x,y) = \langle x,\,y,\,g(x,y)\rangle\).

Recall from 16.6:

\[|\vec{r}_x \times \vec{r}_y| = \sqrt{g_x^2 + g_y^2 + 1}\]

So the scalar surface integral becomes:

\[\iint_S f(x,y,z)\,dS = \iint_D f\!\left(x,\,y,\,g(x,y)\right)\sqrt{g_x^2 + g_y^2 + 1}\;\,dA\]

where \(D\) is the projection of \(S\) onto the \(xy\)-plane.

If \(f \equiv 1\), this reduces to the surface-area formula from Section 15.6:

\[\text{Area}(S) = \iint_D \sqrt{g_x^2 + g_y^2 + 1}\;dA\]

Scalar surface integrals include surface area as a special case — just as \(\int_C 1\,ds\) gives arc length.

For graphs \(z = g(x,y)\): replace \(dS\) with \(\sqrt{g_x^2 + g_y^2 + 1}\,dA\) and substitute \(z = g(x,y)\) into \(f\). Most textbook problems fit this shape.

Example 1: Scalar Surface Integral over a Graph

Example 1

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\iint_S y\,dS\), where \(S\) is the surface \(z = x + y^2\) over the rectangle \(0 \le x \le 1\), \(0 \le y \le 2\).

Identify \(g(x,y)\), compute \(g_x\) and \(g_y\), then set up the integral using the graph formula.

Identify \(g(x,y) = x + y^2\), so \(g_x = 1\) and \(g_y = 2y\).

Area element:

\[\sqrt{g_x^2 + g_y^2 + 1} = \sqrt{1 + 4y^2 + 1} = \sqrt{2 + 4y^2}\]

Set up (integrand has no \(x\), so the \(x\)-integral is trivial):

\[\iint_S y\,dS = \int_0^2\!\!\int_0^1 y\sqrt{2 + 4y^2}\;dx\,dy = \int_0^2 y\sqrt{2 + 4y^2}\;dy\]

Substitute \(u = 2 + 4y^2\), \(du = 8y\,dy\):

\[= \frac{1}{8}\int_2^{18}\!\sqrt{u}\;du = \frac{1}{12}\!\left(18^{3/2} - 2^{3/2}\right) = \frac{1}{12}\!\left(54\sqrt{2} - 2\sqrt{2}\right) = \frac{13\sqrt{2}}{3}\]

Recipe: substitute \(z = g(x,y)\) into \(f\), multiply by \(\sqrt{g_x^2+g_y^2+1}\), and integrate over the projection \(D\).

Both the integrand and area element are independent of \(x\), so the \(x\)-integral evaluates to the width of the \(x\)-interval. Point this out — students should always scan for simplifications before grinding.

Quick Check: Set Up a Scalar Surface Integral

Set up (but do not evaluate) \(\displaystyle\iint_S (x^2 + z)\,dS\) where \(S\) is the part of the plane \(z = 4 - 2x\) over the rectangle \(0 \le x \le 2\), \(0 \le y \le 3\).

Find \(g(x,y)\), compute \(g_x\) and \(g_y\), write \(\sqrt{g_x^2 + g_y^2 + 1}\), substitute \(z = g(x,y)\) into \(f\), then write the double integral.

\(g(x,y) = 4 - 2x\), so \(g_x = -2\), \(g_y = 0\).

\(\sqrt{(-2)^2 + 0^2 + 1} = \sqrt{5}\) (constant, since the plane has constant slope).

Substitute \(z = 4 - 2x\) into \(f\):   \(f(x,y,g(x,y)) = x^2 + (4 - 2x)\).

Set up:

\[\iint_S (x^2+z)\,dS = \sqrt{5}\int_0^3\!\!\int_0^2 (x^2 - 2x + 4)\;dx\,dy\]

Three steps every time: (1) area element \(\sqrt{g_x^2+g_y^2+1}\); (2) substitute \(z = g(x,y)\) into \(f\); (3) write the double integral over \(D\).

Example 2: Scalar Surface Integral on a Sphere

Example 2

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\iint_S y^2\,dS\) where \(S\) is the part of the sphere \(x^2+y^2+z^2 = 4\) that lies inside the cylinder \(x^2+y^2 = 1\) and above the \(xy\)-plane.

Parametrize the sphere using spherical coordinates with \(\rho = 2\): \(\vec{r}(\theta,\phi) = \langle 2\sin\phi\cos\theta,\; 2\sin\phi\sin\theta,\; 2\cos\phi \rangle\). What are the bounds on \(\phi\)?

Parametrize: \(\vec{r}(\theta,\phi) = \langle 2\sin\phi\cos\theta,\; 2\sin\phi\sin\theta,\; 2\cos\phi \rangle\).

For a sphere of radius \(R=2\): \(|\vec{r}_\phi \times \vec{r}_\theta| = R^2\sin\phi = 4\sin\phi\).

Bounds: “Inside the cylinder \(x^2+y^2 = 1\)” means \(4\sin^2\phi \le 1\), so \(\sin\phi \le \tfrac{1}{2}\), i.e. \(0 \le \phi \le \tfrac{\pi}{6}\). Full rotation: \(0 \le \theta \le 2\pi\).

Set up (note \(y = 2\sin\phi\sin\theta\)):

\[\iint_S y^2\,dS = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{\pi/6}\!(2\sin\phi\sin\theta)^2\cdot 4\sin\phi\;d\phi\,d\theta = 16\!\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\sin^2\theta\,d\theta\int_0^{\pi/6}\!\!\sin^3\phi\;d\phi\]

Evaluate. \(\int_0^{2\pi}\sin^2\theta\,d\theta = \pi\). For \(\int\sin^3\phi\,d\phi\), write \(\sin^3\phi = (1-\cos^2\phi)\sin\phi\) and use \(u = \cos\phi\):

\[\int_0^{\pi/6}\!\sin^3\phi\,d\phi = \left[-\cos\phi + \tfrac{\cos^3\phi}{3}\right]_0^{\pi/6} = \tfrac{2}{3} - \tfrac{3\sqrt{3}}{8}\]

so \(\displaystyle\iint_S y^2\,dS = 16\pi\!\left(\tfrac{2}{3} - \tfrac{3\sqrt{3}}{8}\right) = \pi\!\left(\tfrac{32}{3} - 6\sqrt{3}\right)\).

For spheres: spherical parametrization gives \(dS = R^2\sin\phi\,d\phi\,d\theta\). The cylindrical constraint determines the \(\phi\)-bounds — draw a cross-section to see why.

The bound \(\phi \le \pi/6\) comes from where the cylinder cuts the sphere. Sketch a cross-section: the cylinder of radius 1 intersects a sphere of radius 2 at \(\phi = \pi/6\) from the north pole.

Oriented Surfaces (Bridge to Flux)

Before we can define flux, we need to pick a direction at every point of the surface — an orientation.

An orientable surface has two distinct sides. At each point there are two unit normals, \(\vec{n}\) and \(-\vec{n}\). Choosing one consistently over all of \(S\) gives an orientation.

Standard conventions:

  • Closed surfaces (sphere, torus, cube): outward normal is positive.
  • Graphs \(z = g(x,y)\): upward normal is positive.
  • Every problem statement tells you which orientation to use.

Not every surface is orientable — the Möbius strip has only one side. But every surface we will integrate over in this course is orientable.

An orientation is a consistent choice of unit normal. For flux integrals the orientation determines the sign of the answer: reverse the orientation and the flux changes sign.

Pass around a paper Möbius strip if you have one. Students find it fun. Stress that all course examples are orientable — we are not going to integrate over a Möbius strip.

Unit Normals for the Two Most Common Surfaces

For flux we need the unit normal \(\vec{n}\). Two parametrizations cover almost every problem.

Graph \(z = g(x,y)\), \(\;\vec{r}(x,y) = \langle x, y, g(x,y)\rangle\):

\[\vec{r}_x\times\vec{r}_y = \langle -g_x,\,-g_y,\,1\rangle\]

\[\vec{n}_{\text{up}} = \frac{\langle -g_x,\,-g_y,\,1\rangle}{\sqrt{g_x^2+g_y^2+1}}\]

The \(+1\) in the third slot means this vector points upward (so it is the positive orientation).

Sphere of radius \(R\), spherical parametrization.

First, the easy-to-remember outward unit normal:

\[\vec{n}_{\text{out}} = \frac{\langle x,y,z\rangle}{R} = \hat{r}\]

(just the position vector, normalized.) Then the cross product factors cleanly as

\[\vec{r}_\phi\times\vec{r}_\theta = R^2\sin\phi\;\hat{r}.\]

Since \(\sin\phi \geq 0\), this points outward automatically.

To reverse either orientation, multiply by \(-1\) (or swap the order of the cross product).

Graphs: \(\vec{r}_x\times\vec{r}_y = \langle -g_x,-g_y,1\rangle\) points up. Spheres: \(\vec{r}_\phi\times\vec{r}_\theta\) points out. Both match the standard positive orientation.

Tie it to Section 15.5: we used \(\langle -g_x,-g_y,1\rangle\) back then too. Same vector, now with a name.

Flux: Definition and Parametric Formula

Now the big idea: how much of a vector field \(\vec{F}\) flows through a surface.

At each point, only the piece of \(\vec{F}\) perpendicular to \(S\) passes through — that piece is \(\vec{F}\cdot\vec{n}\).

Flux of \(\vec{F}\) across an Oriented Surface \(S\)

\[\iint_S \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{S} \;=\; \iint_S \vec{F}\cdot\vec{n}\;dS\]

Parametric formula. Since \(\vec{n}\,dS = (\vec{r}_u \times \vec{r}_v)\,dA\), the \(|\cdot|\) on the cross product cancels the \(|\cdot|\) in the denominator of \(\vec{n}\):

Theorem: Flux via a Parametrization

\[\iint_S \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{S} = \iint_D \vec{F}\!\left(\vec{r}(u,v)\right)\cdot(\vec{r}_u \times \vec{r}_v)\,dA\]

Use the cross product itself — not its magnitude. Order matters: \(\vec{r}_u\times\vec{r}_v\) vs. \(\vec{r}_v\times\vec{r}_u\) gives opposite orientations.

Side by side with work:

Work (16.2): \(\displaystyle\int_C \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{r} = \int_a^b \vec{F}(\vec{r}(t))\cdot \vec{r} '(t)\,dt\)

Dot \(\vec{F}\) with the tangent vector.

Flux (16.7): \(\displaystyle\iint_S \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{S} = \iint_D \vec{F}(\vec{r})\cdot (\vec{r}_u\times\vec{r}_v)\,dA\)

Dot \(\vec{F}\) with the (scaled) normal vector.

Flux measures flow through \(S\) the way work measures flow along \(C\). Dot \(\vec{F}\) with \(\vec{r}_u\times\vec{r}_v\) (no magnitude, no square root).

Flux through a Graph \(z = g(x,y)\)

Plug \(\vec{r}_x\times\vec{r}_y = \langle -g_x, -g_y, 1\rangle\) into the parametric flux formula.

If \(\vec{F} = \langle P, Q, R\rangle\), then

\[\vec{F}\cdot(\vec{r}_x\times\vec{r}_y) = -P\,g_x - Q\,g_y + R\]

(with \(P,Q,R\) evaluated at \((x,y,g(x,y))\)).

Upward orientation (positive):

\[\iint_S \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{S} = \iint_D \!\left(-P\,g_x - Q\,g_y + R\right)\,dA.\]

Downward orientation: flip the sign.

\[\iint_S \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{S} = \iint_D \!\left(P\,g_x + Q\,g_y - R\right)\,dA.\]

No square root. The magnitude from \(dS\) cancels the denominator of \(\vec{n}\). This is the single most useful formula in the section.

Flux through a graph: \(\iint_D(-Pg_x - Qg_y + R)\,dA\) (upward). Flip sign for downward. Compare with the scalar version, which did need \(\sqrt{g_x^2+g_y^2+1}\).

Box this formula on the board. Students reference it over and over in examples 3–5 and on the exam.

Quick Check: Identify the Flux Setup

\(\vec{F}(x,y,z) = \langle 0, 0, z\rangle\) and \(S\) is the part of the paraboloid \(z = 1 - x^2 - y^2\) above the \(xy\)-plane, with upward orientation. Write \(\vec{F}\cdot(\vec{r}_x\times\vec{r}_y)\) as a function of \(x\) and \(y\).

Find \(g(x,y)\), form \(\langle -g_x,-g_y,1\rangle\), substitute \(z = g(x,y)\) into \(\vec{F}\), then dot.

\(g(x,y) = 1 - x^2 - y^2\), so \(g_x = -2x\), \(g_y = -2y\).

\(\vec{r}_x\times\vec{r}_y = \langle 2x, 2y, 1\rangle\).

Substitute \(z = 1 - x^2 - y^2\) into \(\vec{F}\):

\[\vec{F}\cdot(\vec{r}_x\times\vec{r}_y) = \langle 0,\,0,\,1-x^2-y^2\rangle\cdot\langle 2x, 2y, 1\rangle = 1 - x^2 - y^2.\]

When \(\vec{F}\) has only a \(z\)-component, the flux integrand collapses to \(R\) evaluated on \(S\) — the \(-Pg_x-Qg_y\) piece vanishes.

Example 3: Flux across a Plane

Example 3

Find the flux of \(\vec{F}(x,y,z) = \langle x, 2y, 3z\rangle\) upward through the triangular portion of the plane \(2x + y + 2z = 6\) in the first octant.

Solve for \(z = g(x,y)\), find \(g_x\) and \(g_y\), apply the upward-graph flux formula, and find the domain in the \(xy\)-plane.

Solve for \(z = g(x,y) = 3 - x - \tfrac{1}{2}y\), so \(g_x = -1\), \(g_y = -\tfrac{1}{2}\).

Integrand (upward orientation):

\[-Pg_x - Qg_y + R = -x(-1) - 2y(-\tfrac{1}{2}) + 3(3-x-\tfrac{1}{2}y) = 9 - 2x - \tfrac{1}{2}y.\]

Domain. First octant + \(z\ge 0\) \(\Rightarrow\) \(x\ge 0\), \(y\ge 0\), \(y\le 6 - 2x\) (so \(x\le 3\)).

\[\iint_S \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{S} = \int_0^3\!\!\int_0^{6-2x}\!(9 - 2x - \tfrac{1}{2}y)\,dy\,dx\]

Inner integral:

\(\displaystyle\int_0^{6-2x}(9-2x-\tfrac{1}{2}y)\,dy = (9-2x)(6-2x) - \tfrac{1}{4}(6-2x)^2 = \tfrac{3}{2}(6-2x)(5-x).\)

Outer integral:

\[\tfrac{3}{2}\!\int_0^3 (6-2x)(5-x)\,dx = \tfrac{3}{2}\!\int_0^3(30 - 16x + 2x^2)\,dx = \tfrac{3}{2}\!\left[30x - 8x^2 + \tfrac{2}{3}x^3\right]_0^3 = \tfrac{3}{2}(36) = 54.\]

Flux of \(\vec{F}\) upward through the triangle is \(\boxed{54}\). A plane is the easiest graph case — \(g_x\), \(g_y\) are constants.

Example 4: Flux across the Unit Sphere

Example 4

Find the flux of \(\vec{F}(x,y,z) = \langle z, y, x\rangle\) outward across the unit sphere \(x^2+y^2+z^2 = 1\).

Use spherical parametrization with \(R = 1\). Recall \(\vec{r}_\phi\times\vec{r}_\theta = \sin\phi \langle\sin\phi\cos\theta, \sin\phi\sin\theta, \cos\phi\rangle\) points outward.

Parametrize: \(\vec{r}(\theta,\phi) = \langle\sin\phi\cos\theta, \sin\phi\sin\theta, \cos\phi\rangle\), \; \(0\le\theta\le 2\pi\), \; \(0\le\phi\le\pi\).

\(\vec{r}_\phi\times\vec{r}_\theta = \sin\phi \langle\sin\phi\cos\theta, \sin\phi\sin\theta, \cos\phi\rangle\).

Evaluate \(\vec{F}\) on the sphere:

\[\vec{F}(\vec{r}) = \langle\cos\phi,\;\sin\phi\sin\theta,\;\sin\phi\cos\theta\rangle.\]

Dot product:

\[\vec{F}\cdot(\vec{r}_\phi\times\vec{r}_\theta) = \sin\phi\!\left[\underbrace{2\sin\phi\cos\phi\cos\theta}_{\text{vanishes in }\theta} + \sin^2\phi\sin^2\theta\right].\]

Integrate. The \(\cos\theta\) term integrates to \(0\) over \([0,2\pi]\):

\[\iint_S \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{S} = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\sin^2\theta\,d\theta\int_0^{\pi}\!\!\sin^3\phi\,d\phi = \pi\cdot\tfrac{4}{3} = \tfrac{4\pi}{3}.\]

Only the \(y\)-component of \(\vec{F}\) contributed — the other two cancelled by symmetry. Always scan for symmetry before grinding through an integral.

Let students stare at the symmetry argument. The \(\vec{F} = \langle z, 0, x\rangle\) piece flips sign under \((x,z)\to(-x,-z)\) while the sphere is invariant — so the integral over the sphere is zero.

Example 5: Flux on a Cone (Downward Orientation)

Example 5

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\iint_S \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{S}\) where \(\vec{F}(x,y,z) = \langle -x, -y, z^3\rangle\) and \(S\) is the part of the cone \(z = \sqrt{x^2+y^2}\) between \(z = 1\) and \(z = 3\), with downward orientation.

Use \(g(x,y) = \sqrt{x^2+y^2}\) and the downward graph formula \(\iint_D(Pg_x+Qg_y-R)\,dA\). The domain is the annulus \(1\le r\le 3\) in polar.

Partial derivatives:

\[g(x,y) = \sqrt{x^2+y^2},\quad g_x = \tfrac{x}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}},\quad g_y = \tfrac{y}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}}.\]

Downward integrand:

\[Pg_x + Qg_y - R = -\tfrac{x^2}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}} - \tfrac{y^2}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}} - (x^2+y^2)^{3/2} = -\sqrt{x^2+y^2} - (x^2+y^2)^{3/2}.\]

Domain (polar): \(1\le\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\le 3\) gives \(1\le r\le 3\), \(0\le\theta\le 2\pi\). Integrand becomes \(-r - r^3\), \(dA = r\,dr\,d\theta\):

\[\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_1^3 (-r - r^3)\,r\,dr\,d\theta = -2\pi\!\int_1^3(r^2 + r^4)\,dr = -2\pi\!\left[\tfrac{r^3}{3} + \tfrac{r^5}{5}\right]_1^3.\]

Evaluate:

\[\left[\tfrac{r^3}{3}+\tfrac{r^5}{5}\right]_1^3 = \left(9 + \tfrac{243}{5}\right) - \left(\tfrac{1}{3}+\tfrac{1}{5}\right) = \tfrac{288}{5} - \tfrac{8}{15} = \tfrac{856}{15},\quad \text{flux} = -\tfrac{1712\pi}{15}.\]

Negative flux: the net flow is in the upward direction, opposite the chosen downward normal. Upward orientation would give \(+\tfrac{1712\pi}{15}\).

Emphasize the sign interpretation. The flux is genuinely positive “up,” and the negative sign is just a bookkeeping consequence of our choice of \(\vec{n}\).

Quick Check: Does Orientation Matter?

If the flux of \(\vec{F}\) through a surface \(S\) with upward orientation is \(12\), what is the flux with downward orientation?

Think about how flipping \(\vec{n}\) changes the flux integrand.

Flipping the orientation replaces \(\vec{n}\) with \(-\vec{n}\):

\[\iint_S\vec{F}\cdot(-\vec{n})\,dS = -\iint_S\vec{F}\cdot\vec{n}\,dS = -12.\]

Downward flux \(= -12\). The magnitude is the same; only the sign flips.

Reversing a surface's orientation negates the flux. That's why every problem statement specifies the orientation — it fixes the sign of the answer.

Application: Heat Flow across a Surface

If the temperature in a solid is \(u(x,y,z)\), the heat-flow vector is

\[\vec{F} = -K\,\nabla u \qquad (K > 0,\; \text{thermal conductivity}).\]

Heat flows from hot to cold — opposite the gradient.

The rate of heat flow across a surface \(S\) is

\[\text{rate} = \iint_S \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{S} = -K\!\iint_S \nabla u\cdot d\vec{S}.\]

Example 6

Temperature inside a ball: \(u = C/r\) where \(r = \sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}\). Find the rate of heat flow across the sphere \(S\) of radius \(a\).

Compute \(\nabla u\), form \(\vec{F} = -K\nabla u\), then dot with the outward normal \(\vec{n} = \tfrac{1}{a}\langle x,y,z\rangle\) on the sphere \(r = a\).

Gradient. \(u = C\,r^{-1}\), so \(\nabla u = -\tfrac{C}{r^3}\langle x,y,z\rangle\) and \(\vec{F} = -K\nabla u = \tfrac{KC}{r^3}\langle x,y,z\rangle\).

On the sphere \(r = a\): \(\vec{F} = \tfrac{KC}{a^3}\langle x,y,z\rangle\), and \(\vec{F}\cdot\vec{n} = \tfrac{KC}{a^4}(x^2+y^2+z^2) = \tfrac{KC}{a^4}\cdot a^2 = \tfrac{KC}{a^2}\) (a constant on \(S\)).

Total heat flow:

\[\iint_S\vec{F}\cdot d\vec{S} = \tfrac{KC}{a^2}\cdot \text{Area}(S) = \tfrac{KC}{a^2}\cdot 4\pi a^2 = 4\pi KC.\]

Independent of the radius \(a\)!

Heat flow rate is \(4\pi KC\), not depending on \(a\). Surface area grows as \(a^2\); the inverse-square gradient decays as \(1/a^2\). They cancel. Physically: in steady state, whatever heat leaves a small sphere must leave any larger enclosing sphere.

Foreshadow the Divergence Theorem (16.9): that cancellation is what “divergence-free outside the source” means. We'll come back to it.

Section 16.7 Summary

Two flavors, in parallel

Every line integral from 16.2 has a surface-integral twin. Scalar: \(\int_C f\,ds\leftrightarrow \iint_S f\,dS\). Vector: \(\int_C \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{r}\leftrightarrow \iint_S \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{S}\) (work \(\leftrightarrow\) flux).

Scalar Surface Integral

\(\displaystyle\iint_S f\,dS = \iint_D f(\vec{r}) |\vec{r}_u\times\vec{r}_v|\,dA\). Graph: \(\iint_D f\sqrt{g_x^2+g_y^2+1}\,dA\). Special case \(f = 1\) gives surface area.

Orientation

Consistent choice of unit normal \(\vec{n}\). Closed surfaces: outward. Graphs: upward. Reversing \(\vec{n}\) negates flux.

Flux (Vector Surface Integral)

\(\displaystyle\iint_S\vec{F}\cdot d\vec{S} = \iint_D \vec{F}\cdot(\vec{r}_u\times\vec{r}_v)\,dA\). Graph upward: \(\iint_D(-Pg_x-Qg_y+R)\,dA\). No square root!

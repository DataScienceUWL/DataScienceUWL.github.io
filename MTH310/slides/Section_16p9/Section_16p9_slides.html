16.9

The Divergence Theorem

MTH 310 — Calculus III
Vector Calculus

One Last Theorem in the Family

We've built a chain of theorems throughout this course, each relating a boundary integral to an interior integral:

  • FTC (Calc I): \(\displaystyle\int_a^b f'(x)\,dx = f(b) - f(a)\)
  • FTC for Line Integrals (16.3): \(\displaystyle\int_C \nabla f\cdot d\vec{r} = f(B) - f(A)\)
  • Green's Theorem (16.4): \(\displaystyle\oint_C \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{r} = \iint_D \!\left(Q_x - P_y\right)dA\)
  • Stokes' Theorem (16.8): \(\displaystyle\oint_C \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{r} = \iint_S \mathrm{curl} \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{S}\)

Today: the Divergence Theorem, connecting a surface integral (flux through a closed surface) to a triple integral (divergence over the enclosed solid). It is the 3D analogue of Green's Theorem in flux form.

Every major theorem in Chapter 16 has the same shape: \(\displaystyle\int_{\text{boundary}} (\text{something}) = \int_{\text{interior}}(\text{derivative of something})\). The Divergence Theorem is the final version.

Draw the progression on the board: 0D boundary (two points, FTC) \(\to\) 1D boundary (curve, Green's/Stokes') \(\to\) 2D boundary (surface, Divergence). Students should feel the dimension ladder explicitly.

The Divergence Theorem

Theorem: The Divergence Theorem (Gauss's Theorem)

Let \(E\) be a simple solid region, and let \(S\) be its boundary surface, oriented outward. Let \(\vec{F}\) be a vector field whose components have continuous partial derivatives on an open region containing \(E\). Then

\[\boxed{\iint_S \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{S} \;=\; \iiint_E \mathrm{div}\,\vec{F}\; dV.}\]

In components, with \(\vec{F} = \langle P, Q, R\rangle\):

\[\iint_S \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{S} = \iiint_E \!\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial Q}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial R}{\partial z}\right) dV.\]

Reading the two sides:

  • Left: total flux of \(\vec{F}\) outward through the closed surface \(S\).
  • Right: total divergence of \(\vec{F}\) throughout the solid \(E\).

Flux through the boundary \(=\) total source strength inside. If \(\mathrm{div} \vec{F} > 0\) inside \(E\), net outward flux (source). If \(\mathrm{div} \vec{F} < 0\), net inward flux (sink).

Physical Interpretation: Sources and Sinks

Think of \(\vec{F}\) as the velocity field of a fluid. The Divergence Theorem says net outflow \(=\) total internal source strength:

\[\underbrace{\iint_S \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{S}}_{\text{net flow out through }S} \;=\; \underbrace{\iiint_E \mathrm{div}\,\vec{F}\; dV}_{\text{total source/sink inside }E}.\]

Source (\(\mathrm{div} \vec{F} > 0\)): fluid is created inside \(E\) — more leaves through \(S\) than enters. Faucet.

Sink (\(\mathrm{div} \vec{F} < 0\)): fluid is absorbed inside \(E\) — more enters than leaves. Drain.

Incompressible flow (\(\mathrm{div} \vec{F} = 0\) everywhere): whatever flows in must flow out, so \(\displaystyle\iint_S \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{S} = 0\).

In physics:

  • Gauss's law: \(\mathrm{div} \vec{E} = \rho/\varepsilon_0\). Charge is the source of the electric flux.
  • Heat flow: net heat flux out of a region \(=\) total heat generated inside.

Divergence is the local expansion rate of the field. The theorem sums all those local expansions to produce the net boundary outflow.

Faucet/drain analogy works well. Seal a room, run the faucet: positive divergence inside means outward pressure on the walls (the boundary surface). Gauss's law is the canonical physics application.

Connection to Green's Theorem (Flux Form)

Recall the flux form of Green's Theorem (16.4):

\[\oint_C \vec{F}\cdot\vec{n}\,ds = \iint_D \mathrm{div}\,\vec{F}\,dA \qquad (\vec{F} = \langle P,Q\rangle,\; \text{2D}).\]

The Divergence Theorem is the same statement, one dimension up:

Green's (2D flux form)

  • Boundary: closed curve \(C\)
  • Interior: flat region \(D\)
  • LHS: \(\oint_C \vec{F}\cdot\vec{n}\,ds\) (line integral)
  • RHS: \(\iint_D (P_x + Q_y)\,dA\)

Divergence Theorem (3D)

  • Boundary: closed surface \(S\)
  • Interior: solid region \(E\)
  • LHS: \(\iint_S \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{S}\) (surface integral)
  • RHS: \(\iiint_E (P_x + Q_y + R_z)\,dV\)

Green's (flux form) is the 2D Divergence Theorem. Every theorem in Chapter 16 follows one pattern: integral over boundary \(=\) integral of a derivative over interior.

Quick Check: Compute the Divergence

Compute \(\mathrm{div} \vec{F}\) for each field:

  1. \(\vec{F} = \langle x^2, xy, z\rangle\)
  2. \(\vec{F} = \langle x^3 + y^3,\; y^3 + z^3,\; z^3 + x^3\rangle\)
  3. \(\vec{F} = \langle yz, xz, xy\rangle\)

Recall: \(\mathrm{div} \vec{F} = P_x + Q_y + R_z\).

(1) \(P_x = 2x\), \(Q_y = x\), \(R_z = 1\), so \(\mathrm{div} \vec{F} = 3x + 1\).

(2) \(P_x = 3x^2\), \(Q_y = 3y^2\), \(R_z = 3z^2\), so \(\mathrm{div} \vec{F} = 3(x^2+y^2+z^2)\).

(3) \(P_x = 0\), \(Q_y = 0\), \(R_z = 0\), so \(\mathrm{div} \vec{F} = 0\). This field is incompressible.

Differentiate each component with respect to its “own” variable and add. Incompressible fields have zero net flux through any closed surface.

Example 1: Flux Through a Paraboloid Region

Example 1

Use the Divergence Theorem to find the flux of \(\vec{F} = \langle x^2, xy, z\rangle\) outward through the closed surface \(S\) bounding the solid \(E\) below \(z = 4 - x^2 - y^2\) and above \(z = 0\).

Computing the flux directly is painful: the boundary \(S\) has two pieces (paraboloid cap + flat disk below), each needing its own parametrization. Use the Divergence Theorem to replace it with one triple integral over \(E\). Cylindrical coordinates work here.

Step 1: divergence. \(\mathrm{div} \vec{F} = 2x + x + 1 = 3x + 1\).

Step 2: bounds in cylindrical coordinates. The paraboloid meets \(z=0\) on \(x^2+y^2 = 4\), so \(0\le r \le 2\), \(0\le\theta\le 2\pi\), and \(0\le z \le 4 - r^2\). With \(x = r\cos\theta\):

\[\iiint_E (3x+1)\,dV = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^2\!\!\int_0^{4-r^2}\!(3r\cos\theta + 1)\,r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta.\]

Step 3: \(z\)-integral.

\[\int_0^{4-r^2}(3r\cos\theta + 1)\,r\,dz = (3r^2\cos\theta + r)(4 - r^2).\]

Example 1: Flux Through a Paraboloid Region

Step 4: \(\theta\)-integral kills the \(\cos\theta\) term. Since \(\int_0^{2\pi}\cos\theta\,d\theta = 0\), only the \(r(4-r^2)\) piece survives:

\[\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^2 r(4-r^2)\,dr\,d\theta = 2\pi\int_0^2 (4r - r^3)\,dr.\]

Step 5: \(r\)-integral.

\[2\pi\left[2r^2 - \tfrac{r^4}{4}\right]_0^2 = 2\pi(8 - 4) = \boxed{8\pi}.\]

One triple integral replaced TWO separate surface integrals (paraboloid cap + disk). The \(\cos\theta\) factor dropped out by symmetry — always scan for these before grinding.

Emphasize the two gains: (1) one integral instead of two, (2) the \(\theta\)-symmetry kills the \(3r\cos\theta\) term automatically. This is why the Divergence Theorem is a strategic tool, not just a statement.

Quick Check: Which Side Is Easier?

For each scenario, decide which side of the Divergence Theorem is easier to compute.

  1. \(\vec{F} = \langle x, y, z\rangle\), \(S\) is a complicated surface enclosing a solid of known volume.
  2. \(\mathrm{div} \vec{F}\) is a messy expression, but \(S\) is a single simple surface.
  3. \(\vec{F} = \langle xz^2, x^2 y, y^2 z\rangle\), \(E\) is the unit cube \([0,1]^3\).

Ask: is the divergence simple? Is the surface a single piece? Is the volume easy to compute?

(1) Volume side. \(\mathrm{div} \vec{F} = 3\) is constant, so \(\iiint_E 3\,dV = 3\cdot\mathrm{Vol}(E)\). Trivial.

(2) Surface side directly — don't integrate a messy divergence.

(3) Volume side. \(\mathrm{div} \vec{F} = z^2 + x^2 + y^2\) integrates cleanly over the cube:

\[\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^1 (x^2+y^2+z^2)\,dz\,dy\,dx = 3\cdot\tfrac{1}{3} = 1.\]

Divergence Theorem \(=\) computational shortcut. Simple divergence or simple volume \(\Rightarrow\) use the triple integral. Simple surface with complicated divergence \(\Rightarrow\) use the surface integral.

Example 2: Flux Through a Sphere

Example 2

Use the Divergence Theorem to find the flux of \(\vec{F} = \langle x^3 + y^3,\; y^3 + z^3,\; z^3 + x^3\rangle\) outward through the sphere \(S\) of radius \(2\) centered at the origin.

The divergence is \(3(x^2+y^2+z^2) = 3\rho^2\) in spherical coordinates — a gift. Set up the triple integral over the ball.

Divergence. \(\mathrm{div} \vec{F} = 3x^2 + 3y^2 + 3z^2 = 3\rho^2\).

Spherical integral over the ball \(0\le\rho\le 2\), \(0\le\phi\le\pi\), \(0\le\theta\le 2\pi\), with \(dV = \rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta\):

\[\iiint_E 3\rho^2\,dV = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{\pi}\!\!\int_0^2 3\rho^4\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta.\]

Evaluate (integral separates):

\[= 3\cdot 2\pi \cdot \bigl[-\cos\phi\bigr]_0^\pi \cdot \tfrac{32}{5} = 3\cdot 2\pi\cdot 2\cdot \tfrac{32}{5} = \boxed{\tfrac{384\pi}{5}}.\]

The divergence just happened to be \(3\rho^2\) in the right coordinates. The surface-integral route would force a nasty dot product with \(\vec{r}_\phi\times\vec{r}_\theta\) for every cubic term.

Emphasize the coordinate match: \(x^2+y^2+z^2 = \rho^2\) is the spherical payoff. Whenever \(\vec{F}\) is radial-ish and \(S\) is a sphere, the Divergence Theorem flattens everything.

Example 3: Flux Through a Cube

Example 3

Find the flux of \(\vec{F} = \langle xy, y^2, yz\rangle\) outward through the surface of the cube \([0,1]^3\).

The cube has SIX faces. Direct computation means six separate surface integrals, each with its own outward normal. Use the Divergence Theorem.

Divergence. \(\mathrm{div} \vec{F} = y + 2y + y = 4y\).

Triple integral (independent of \(x\) and \(z\)):

\[\iiint_E 4y\,dV = \int_0^1\!\!\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^1 4y\,dz\,dy\,dx = \int_0^1\!\!\int_0^1 4y\,dy\,dx = \int_0^1 2\,dx = \boxed{2}.\]

One triple integral replaced six surface integrals. Whenever the surface has many pieces but the solid is simple, the Divergence Theorem is the clear winner.

Ask students to imagine doing this as six separate face-integrals. The \(\pm\vec{i}, \pm\vec{j}, \pm\vec{k}\) normals, the six slicing plane integrals — it's brutal without the theorem.

Quick Check: The Radial Field Through a Sphere

Let \(\vec{F} = \langle x, y, z\rangle\) and \(S\) be the unit sphere. Find \(\displaystyle\iint_S \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{S}\) without parametrizing anything.

Compute \(\mathrm{div} \vec{F}\). If it's a constant \(c\), the flux is just \(c\cdot\mathrm{Vol}(E)\).

\(\mathrm{div} \vec{F} = 1 + 1 + 1 = 3\).

\[\iint_S \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{S} = \iiint_E 3\,dV = 3\cdot \tfrac{4}{3}\pi\cdot 1^3 = \boxed{4\pi}.\]

Constant divergence \(c\) \(\Rightarrow\) flux \(= c\cdot\mathrm{Vol}(E)\) for any closed surface. For the radial field \(\vec{F} = \langle x,y,z\rangle\) on any closed surface, flux \(= 3\cdot\mathrm{Vol}\).

When NOT to Use the Divergence Theorem

The theorem requires a closed surface enclosing a solid with \(\vec{F}\) smooth throughout. Common pitfalls:

1. Open surface. E.g., a hemisphere with no disk on the bottom. Fix: add the missing piece, apply the theorem to the closed surface, subtract the flux through the added piece.

2. Singularity inside \(E\). E.g., \(\vec{F} = \vec{r}/|\vec{r}|^3\) with the origin inside. Fix: cut out a small sphere around the singularity and treat the region as topologically a shell.

3. Non-simple regions (cavities or holes). Fix: decompose into simple pieces.

Checklist before applying the theorem: (i) Is \(S\) closed? (ii) Is \(\vec{F}\) smooth throughout \(E\)? (iii) Is \(S\) oriented outward? Failure on any one means the theorem needs adaptation.

The Big Picture: Four Theorems, One Pattern

Every major theorem in vector calculus is a version of:

\[\int_{\text{boundary}} (\text{something}) \;=\; \int_{\text{interior}} (\text{derivative of something}).\]

FTC (Calc I, 0D\(\to\)1D)

  • Boundary: \(\{a, b\}\)
  • Interior: \([a, b]\)
  • Derivative: \(f'\)

Green's (16.4, 1D\(\to\)2D)

  • Boundary: curve \(C\)
  • Interior: region \(D\)
  • Derivative: 2D curl or div

Stokes' (16.8, 1D\(\to\)2D in 3D)

  • Boundary: space curve \(C\)
  • Interior: surface \(S\)
  • Derivative: 3D curl

Divergence (16.9, 2D\(\to\)3D)

  • Boundary: closed surface \(S\)
  • Interior: solid \(E\)
  • Derivative: 3D div

FTC, Green's, Stokes', Divergence are not four separate theorems — they are the same theorem in different dimensions.

This is the single most important conceptual slide of the entire course. Pause here. Let the pattern land before moving on to the decision tree.

Which Theorem? A Decision Tree

Decision tree for integrals in Chapter 16:

  1. \(\vec{F}\) conservative? \(\Rightarrow\) FTC for line integrals: find \(f\), evaluate at endpoints.
  2. Line integral around a closed curve in the plane? \(\Rightarrow\) Green's.
  3. Line integral around a closed curve in 3D? \(\Rightarrow\) Stokes'.
  4. Flux through a closed surface in 3D? \(\Rightarrow\) Divergence.
Example 4

Match each integral to the theorem that simplifies it:

  • [(a)] \(\displaystyle\iint_S \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{S}\), \(S\) the closed surface of a cylinder, with \(\mathrm{div} \vec{F} = 5\).
  • [(b)] \(\displaystyle\oint_C \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{r}\), \(C\) the boundary of a triangle in space.
  • [(c)] \(\displaystyle\int_C \nabla f\cdot d\vec{r}\) from \((0,0)\) to \((3,4)\).

Map each integral to one of the four theorems.

(a) Flux through a closed surface \(\Rightarrow\) Divergence: \(\displaystyle\iint_S \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{S} = \iiint_E 5\,dV = 5\cdot\mathrm{Vol}(\text{cyl})\).

(b) Closed curve in 3D \(\Rightarrow\) Stokes': \(\displaystyle\oint_C \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{r} = \iint_S \mathrm{curl} \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{S}\).

(c) Integrand is \(\nabla f\) \(\Rightarrow\) FTC for line integrals: \(\displaystyle\int_C \nabla f\cdot d\vec{r} = f(3,4) - f(0,0)\).

Recognizing which theorem to apply is the single highest-leverage skill for the final exam. Read the integrand and the domain (open/closed? 1D/2D/3D?) and match.

Chapter 16 Summary: The Story of the Course

Vector Fields (16.1)

\(\vec{F} = \langle P,Q,R\rangle\). Conservative \(\Leftrightarrow\) \(\vec{F} = \nabla f\) on a simply connected region \(\Leftrightarrow\) \(\mathrm{curl} \vec{F} = \vec{0}\).

Line Integrals (16.2–16.3)

\(\int_C \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{r} = \int_a^b \vec{F}(\vec{r}(t))\cdot\vec{r} '(t)\,dt\). FTLI: \(\int_C \nabla f\cdot d\vec{r} = f(B)-f(A)\).

Green's Theorem (16.4)

\(\oint_C P\,dx + Q\,dy = \iint_D (Q_x - P_y)\,dA\). Converts 2D line integrals to double integrals.

Surface Integrals (16.6–16.7)

Scalar: \(\iint_S f\,dS = \iint_D f |\vec{r}_u\times\vec{r}_v|\,dA\).   Flux: \(\iint_S \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{S} = \iint_D \vec{F}\cdot(\vec{r}_u\times\vec{r}_v)\,dA\).

Stokes' (16.8)

\(\oint_C \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{r} = \iint_S \mathrm{curl} \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{S}\). Circulation around boundary \(=\) flux of curl through the surface.

Divergence (16.9)

\(\iint_S \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{S} = \iiint_E \mathrm{div} \vec{F}\,dV\). Flux through closed surface \(=\) total source strength inside.

The Deepest Idea

\(\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega} \omega = \int_\Omega d\omega\) — integral of something over a boundary equals integral of its derivative over the interior. FTC \(\to\) Green's \(\to\) Stokes' \(\to\) Divergence are all one theorem at different dimensions. Congratulations — you've seen the whole story.

Let students appreciate how far they've come. You can show the generalized Stokes' theorem \(\int_{\partial\Omega}\omega = \int_\Omega d\omega\) as a teaser for differential forms, but emphasize that the concrete theorems they have learned are the real content.

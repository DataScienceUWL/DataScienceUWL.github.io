Directional Derivatives and the Gradient Vector

MTH 310 — Calculus III
Partial Derivatives

Directional Derivatives — Motivation

Partial derivatives give the rate of change along the axes. But what if we walk in some other direction?

Consider \(f(x,y) = x^2 + 4y^2\) at the point \(P(2,1)\). Pick a direction \(\vec{u}\) at \(45{^\circ}\) and slice the surface with a vertical plane along that direction:

The vertical plane cuts a trace curve out of the surface. The slope of that trace at \(P\) is the rate of change in the \(\vec{u}\) direction — the directional derivative.

Directional Derivatives — Motivation

From the side, this is just a single-variable function \(g(t) = f(P + t\vec{u})\). Its derivative at \(t = 0\) is the slope we want.

The directional derivative is just a Calc I derivative in disguise: pick a direction, slice the surface, and find the slope.

Motivate with a hillside analogy: the slope depends on which direction you walk. Partial derivatives only give you slope along the axes. This generalizes to any direction.

Definition of the Directional Derivative

\(f_x\) and \(f_y\) are just special cases of the directional derivative:

  • \(f_x = D_{\vec{i}}\, f\) — rate of change in the \(\vec{i}\) direction
  • \(f_y = D_{\vec{j}}\, f\) — rate of change in the \(\vec{j}\) direction
Directional Derivative

Let \(\vec{u} = \langle a, b \rangle\) be a unit vector (\(|\vec{u}| = 1\)). The directional derivative of \(f\) at \((x,y)\) in the direction of \(\vec{u}\) is:

\[D_{\vec{u}} f(x,y) = \lim_{t \to 0} \frac{f(x + ta,\; y + tb) - f(x,y)}{t}\]

provided this limit exists.

Reading the formula:

  • Start at \((x, y)\)
  • Move a tiny step \(t\) in the direction \(\vec{u} = \langle a, b \rangle\), arriving at \((x + ta,\; y + tb)\)
  • Measure the change in \(f\), divide by \(t\), take the limit

This is the same rise-over-run idea from Calc I, but now we walk in the direction \(\vec{u}\) instead of along the \(x\)-axis.

Stress that u MUST be a unit vector. If students give a non-unit vector, they need to normalize first.

Example 1: \(D_{\vec{u}} f\) from the Definition

Example 1

Let \(f(x,y) = x^2 + 4y^2\) and \(\vec{u} = \left\langle \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2},\; \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \right\rangle\).

Find \(D_{\vec{u}} f(2, 1)\) using the limit definition.

Write the limit definition formula:

\[D_{\vec{u}} f(x,y) = \lim_{t \to 0} \frac{f(x + ta,\; y + tb) - f(x,y)}{t}\]

Substitute \((x,y) = (2,1)\), \(\vec{u} = \left\langle \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\right\rangle\), and \(f(2,1) = 4 + 4 = 8\):

\[D_{\vec{u}} f(2,1) = \lim_{t \to 0} \frac{f\!\left(2 + \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\,t,\; 1 + \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\,t\right) - 8}{t}\]

Expand the numerator:

\[f\!\left(2 + \tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\,t,\; 1 + \tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\,t\right) = \left(2 + \tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\,t\right)^2 + 4\!\left(1 + \tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\,t\right)^2 = 8 + 6\sqrt{2}\,t + \tfrac{5}{2}\,t^2\]

Simplify and take the limit:

\[D_{\vec{u}} f(2,1) = \lim_{t \to 0} \frac{8 + 6\sqrt{2}\,t + \tfrac{5}{2}\,t^2 - 8}{t} = \lim_{t \to 0} \left(6\sqrt{2} + \tfrac{5}{2}\,t\right) = 6\sqrt{2} \approx 8.49\]

That was a lot of algebra! There must be a better way...

The Gradient Vector

Before we find the shortcut, we need a new object.

Gradient Vector

If \(f\) has partial derivatives \(f_x\) and \(f_y\), the gradient of \(f\) is the vector:

\[\nabla f(x,y) = \langle f_x(x,y),\; f_y(x,y) \rangle = f_x\,\vec{i} + f_y\,\vec{j}\]

The symbol \(\nabla\) is called nabla or del.

The gradient packages both partial derivatives into a single vector. It lives in the domain (the \(xy\)-plane), not on the surface.

The Gradient Vector

Example 2

Find \(\nabla f\) for \(f(x,y) = x^2 + 4y^2\). Then evaluate \(\nabla f(2,1)\).

\(\nabla f = \langle f_x, f_y \rangle\): \(\;f_x = 2x\), \(\;f_y = 8y\) \(\;\Rightarrow\;\) \(\nabla f(x,y) = \langle 2x,\; 8y \rangle\)

Substitute \((2, 1)\): \(\;\nabla f(2,1) = \langle 4,\; 8 \rangle\)

Point out that the gradient is a vector-valued function of (x,y). At each point, it assigns a vector. We'll see later that this vector has deep geometric meaning.

The Big Theorem: \(D_{\vec{u}} f = \nabla f \cdot \vec{u}\)

Theorem: Directional Derivative via the Gradient

If \(f\) is a differentiable function of \(x\) and \(y\), then:

\[D_{\vec{u}} f(x,y) = \nabla f(x,y) \cdot \vec{u}\]

for any unit vector \(\vec{u}\).

Translation: To find the rate of change in any direction, just take the dot product of the gradient with the direction vector.

This is the central formula of Section 14.6. It connects direction, rate of change, and gradient in one equation.

Why is this so much better than the definition?

  • Limit definition: expand, simplify, take a limit (messy algebra)
  • Gradient formula: compute two partial derivatives, take a dot product (fast!)

The Big Theorem: \(D_{\vec{u}} f = \nabla f \cdot \vec{u}\)

Why does it work? Recall the cross-section from our motivation: define \(g(t) = f(x + ta,\; y + tb)\). Then by the Calc I derivative definition:

\[g'(0) = \lim_{t \to 0} \frac{g(t) - g(0)}{t} = \lim_{t \to 0} \frac{f(x + ta,\; y + tb) - f(x,y)}{t} = D_{\vec{u}} f(x,y)\]

So the directional derivative is just \(g'(0)\). Now use the chain rule to find \(g'(0)\) without limits.

Apply the chain rule (Section 14.5):

\[g'(t) = f_x(x + ta,\; y + tb) \cdot a \;+\; f_y(x + ta,\; y + tb) \cdot b\]

Evaluate at \(t = 0\):

\[g'(0) = f_x(x,y) \cdot a + f_y(x,y) \cdot b = \langle f_x,\; f_y \rangle \cdot \langle a,\; b \rangle = \nabla f \cdot \vec{u}\]

The dot product appears because of the chain rule. Each partial derivative gets multiplied by the corresponding component of \(\vec{u}\), which is exactly what a dot product does.

This is a proof sketch, not a full proof (we'd need to verify the limit exchange rigorously). But it shows students exactly where the dot product comes from and reinforces the chain rule from 14.5.

Example 3: \(D_{\vec{u}} f\) via the Gradient

Example 3

Same problem as Example 1: \(f(x,y) = x^2 + 4y^2\), \(\vec{u} = \left\langle \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2},\; \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \right\rangle\).

Find \(D_{\vec{u}} f(2,1)\) using the gradient.

Write the formula:

\[D_{\vec{u}} f(x,y) = \nabla f(x,y) \cdot \vec{u}\]

Substitute (using \(\nabla f(2,1) = \langle 4, 8 \rangle\) from Example 2):

\[D_{\vec{u}} f(2,1) = \langle 4, 8 \rangle \cdot \left\langle \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2},\; \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \right\rangle = \frac{4\sqrt{2}}{2} + \frac{8\sqrt{2}}{2} = 6\sqrt{2}\]

Same answer as the limit definition (\(6\sqrt{2}\)), but in one line instead of half a page!

Maximizing the Directional Derivative

Question: You're at \(P(2,1)\) on the surface \(f(x,y) = x^2 + 4y^2\). Which direction increases \(f\) the fastest?

Use the slider to sweep \(\vec{u}\) around and watch \(D_{\vec{u}} f\) change:

Notice: \(D_{\vec{u}} f\) is largest when \(\vec{u}\) aligns with \(\nabla f\), most negative when opposing, and zero when perpendicular. Why?

Maximizing the Directional Derivative

Why? Rewrite \(D_{\vec{u}} f\) using the dot product identity:

\[D_{\vec{u}} f = \nabla f \cdot \vec{u} = |\nabla f|\;|\vec{u}|\;\cos\theta = |\nabla f|\;\cos\theta\]

where \(\theta\) is the angle between \(\nabla f\) and \(\vec{u}\).

\(|\nabla f|\) is fixed at each point. The only thing we control is \(\theta\), and \(\cos\theta\) ranges from \(-1\) to \(1\):

\(\theta\)\(\cos\theta\)\(D_{\vec{u}} f\)Meaning
\(0\)\(1\)\(|\nabla f|\) (max)\(\vec{u}\) with \(\nabla f\) \(\rightarrow\) steepest increase
\(\pi/2\)\(0\)\(0\)\(\vec{u} \perp \nabla f\) \(\rightarrow\) along level curve
\(\pi\)\(-1\)\(-|\nabla f|\) (min)\(\vec{u}\) against \(\nabla f\) \(\rightarrow\) steepest decrease

The gradient tells you everything:

  • Its direction = steepest ascent
  • Its magnitude \(|\nabla f|\) = the maximum rate of change
  • Perpendicular to it = no change (along the level curve)

Important: \(\nabla f\) lives in the domain (the \(xy\)-plane), not on the surface. It points toward larger \(f\)-values in the input space.

This is one of the most important results in the course. Make sure students understand WHY each case works by thinking about cos theta. Refer back to the slider to reinforce.

Example 4: Fastest Increase and Decrease

Example 4

For \(f(x,y) = x^2 + 4y^2\) at the point \((2,1)\):

(a) What direction gives the fastest increase in \(f\)? How fast?

(b) What direction gives the fastest decrease?

(c) In what directions is \(D_{\vec{u}} f = 0\)?

Key formulas:

  • Max increase direction: \(\vec{u} = \frac{\nabla f}{|\nabla f|}\), max rate \(= |\nabla f|\)
  • Max decrease direction: \(\vec{u} = -\frac{\nabla f}{|\nabla f|}\), rate \(= -|\nabla f|\)
  • Zero change: \(\vec{u} \perp \nabla f\)

Substitute \(\nabla f(2,1) = \langle 4, 8 \rangle\), \(\;|\nabla f| = \sqrt{16 + 64} = 4\sqrt{5}\):

(a) \(\vec{u}_{\max} = \frac{\langle 4, 8 \rangle}{4\sqrt{5}} = \left\langle \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}},\; \frac{2}{\sqrt{5}} \right\rangle\), max rate \(= 4\sqrt{5} \approx 8.94\)

(b) \(\vec{u}_{\min} = -\left\langle \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}},\; \frac{2}{\sqrt{5}} \right\rangle\), rate \(= -4\sqrt{5}\)

(c) \(\vec{u} \perp \nabla f\): swap components and negate one \(\;\Rightarrow\; \vec{u} = \pm\left\langle \frac{2}{\sqrt{5}},\; \frac{-1}{\sqrt{5}} \right\rangle\)

Example 5: Which Way Should I Walk?

Example 5

A hiker is at position \((2, 1)\) on a mountain whose elevation is modeled by \(f(x,y) = x^2 + 4y^2\) (hundreds of feet).

(a) She wants to descend as steeply as possible. What direction should she walk?

(b) She wants to stay at the same elevation (walk along a contour line). What direction(s) should she walk?

Compute the gradient: \(\nabla f(2,1) = \langle 4, 8 \rangle\) (from Example 2), \(\;|\nabla f| = 4\sqrt{5}\)

(a) Steepest descent = opposite the gradient:

\[\vec{u}_{\text{down}} = -\frac{\nabla f}{|\nabla f|} = -\frac{\langle 4, 8 \rangle}{4\sqrt{5}} = \left\langle \frac{-1}{\sqrt{5}},\; \frac{-2}{\sqrt{5}} \right\rangle\]

She should walk in the direction \(\langle -1, -2 \rangle\) (toward the origin). The rate of descent is \(-4\sqrt{5} \approx -8.94\) hundred ft per unit.

(b) Constant elevation = perpendicular to \(\nabla f\):

Swap components and negate one: \(\;\vec{u} = \pm\left\langle \frac{2}{\sqrt{5}},\; \frac{-1}{\sqrt{5}} \right\rangle\)

Two directions along the contour — she can go either way. In both, \(D_{\vec{u}} f = 0\).

Great opportunity to connect to topographic maps. The gradient points straight uphill, perpendicular to contour lines. Walking along a contour = zero elevation change. Steepest descent = straight downhill = opposite the gradient. Refer back to the key cases table.

Your Turn: Directional Derivative Practice

Example 6

Let \(f(x,y) = xe^{-y}\) and let \(\vec{u}\) be the unit vector in the direction of \(\langle 2, -1 \rangle\).

(a) Find \(\nabla f(2, 0)\).

(b) Find \(D_{\vec{u}} f(2, 0)\).

(c) What is the maximum rate of change of \(f\) at \((2, 0)\)?

Normalize: \(\vec{u} = \frac{\vec{v}}{|\vec{v}|}\) \(\;\Rightarrow\;\) \(|\langle 2, -1 \rangle| = \sqrt{5}\) \(\;\Rightarrow\;\) \(\vec{u} = \left\langle \frac{2}{\sqrt{5}},\; \frac{-1}{\sqrt{5}} \right\rangle\)

(a) \(\nabla f = \langle f_x, f_y \rangle\): \(\;f_x = e^{-y}\), \(\;f_y = -xe^{-y}\)

Substitute \((2, 0)\): \(\;\nabla f(2,0) = \langle e^0,\; -2e^0 \rangle = \langle 1, -2 \rangle\)

(b) \(D_{\vec{u}} f = \nabla f \cdot \vec{u}\):

\[D_{\vec{u}} f(2,0) = \langle 1, -2 \rangle \cdot \left\langle \frac{2}{\sqrt{5}},\; \frac{-1}{\sqrt{5}} \right\rangle = \frac{2}{\sqrt{5}} + \frac{2}{\sqrt{5}} = \frac{4}{\sqrt{5}} = \frac{4\sqrt{5}}{5}\]

(c) Max rate \(= |\nabla f|\): \(\;|\nabla f(2,0)| = |\langle 1, -2 \rangle| = \sqrt{1 + 4} = \sqrt{5}\)

Gradient Is Perpendicular to Level Curves

Theorem: Gradient Perpendicular to Level Curves

The gradient vector \(\nabla f(a,b)\) is perpendicular to the level curve \(f(x,y) = k\) at the point \((a,b)\).

Why does this make sense?

Along a level curve, \(f\) is constant, so the rate of change is zero. We saw that \(D_{\vec{u}} f = 0\) when \(\vec{u} \perp \nabla f\).

The tangent direction to a level curve is a direction of zero change — so it must be perpendicular to \(\nabla f\).

Look back at our explorer figure: \(\nabla f(2,1) = \langle 4, 8 \rangle\) visibly points straight away from the ellipse \(f = 8\). Sweep the slider to \(\theta \approx 153{^\circ}\) or \(333{^\circ}\) to see \(D_{\vec{u}} f = 0\) — those are the tangent directions to the level curve.

The gradient always points perpendicular to level curves, aiming toward higher values of \(f\). This is why contour maps and gradients are deeply connected.

Draw the connection: on a topographic map, the gradient points straight uphill, perpendicular to the contour lines. Steeper terrain = closer contours = larger gradient. Use the slider to find the zero-crossings and show they're tangent to the ellipse.

Example 7: Tangent Line to a Level Curve

Example 7

Consider \(f(x,y) = x^2 + 4y^2\) with level curves that are ellipses.

At the point \((2, 1)\), find the equation of the tangent line to the level curve \(f(x,y) = 8\).

Identify the pieces: The level curve through \((2,1)\) is \(f(x,y) = 8\) (the ellipse \(x^2 + 4y^2 = 8\)).

Normal vector: \(\nabla f(2,1) = \langle 4, 8 \rangle\)

Write the tangent line formula (normal vector dot displacement = 0):

\[\nabla f(x_0, y_0) \cdot \langle x - x_0,\; y - y_0 \rangle = 0\]

Substitute:

\[\langle 4, 8 \rangle \cdot \langle x - 2,\; y - 1 \rangle = 0 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; 4(x - 2) + 8(y - 1) = 0\]

Simplify: \(\;4x + 8y = 16\) \(\;\Rightarrow\;\) \(x + 2y = 4\)

The gradient gives you the normal vector to the level curve. From there, writing the tangent line is just like writing a plane equation in 2D.

Extending to Three Variables

Everything generalizes naturally to functions of three variables \(f(x, y, z)\):

Gradient in Three Variables

\[\nabla f(x,y,z) = \langle f_x,\; f_y,\; f_z \rangle\]

The directional derivative formula is exactly the same:

\[D_{\vec{u}} f(x,y,z) = \nabla f(x,y,z) \cdot \vec{u}\]

where \(\vec{u} = \langle a, b, c \rangle\) is a unit vector in \(\mathbb{R}^3\).

Extending to Three Variables

Level surfaces replace level curves:

For \(F(x,y,z) = k\), the gradient \(\nabla F\) is perpendicular to the level surface at each point.

Since \(\nabla F\) is perpendicular to the level surface, it serves as the normal vector for the tangent plane!

Tangent plane to \(F(x,y,z) = k\) at \((a,b,c)\):

\[F_x(a,b,c)(x - a) + F_y(a,b,c)(y - b) + F_z(a,b,c)(z - c) = 0\]

Emphasize the parallel: in 2D, gradient gives normal to level curve (tangent line). In 3D, gradient gives normal to level surface (tangent plane). Same idea, one dimension higher. Note the switch to capital F — textbook convention for level-surface functions.

Example 8: Tangent Plane to a Level Surface

Example 8

Let \(F(x,y,z) = zx^2 - yz^2\).

(a) Compute \(\nabla F(1, 2, 3)\).

(b) Find the equation of the tangent plane to the level surface \(F(x,y,z) = -15\) at the point \((1, 2, 3)\).

Check the point: \(F(1,2,3) = 3(1) - 2(9) = 3 - 18 = -15\) \(\checkmark\)

(a) Compute the gradient: \(\nabla F = \langle F_x, F_y, F_z \rangle\)

\(F_x = 2xz\), \(\quad F_y = -z^2\), \(\quad F_z = x^2 - 2yz\)

Substitute \((1, 2, 3)\): \(\;\nabla F(1,2,3) = \langle 6,\; -9,\; 1 - 12 \rangle = \langle 6,\; -9,\; -11 \rangle\)

(b) Write the tangent plane formula:

\[F_x(a,b,c)(x - a) + F_y(a,b,c)(y - b) + F_z(a,b,c)(z - c) = 0\]

Substitute:

\[6(x - 1) - 9(y - 2) - 11(z - 3) = 0\]

Simplify: \(\;6x - 9y - 11z = -45\)

The gradient \(\nabla F\) of a function of 3 variables is the normal vector to the level surface \(F = k\). This gives us tangent planes to implicitly defined surfaces.

Section 14.6 Summary

Directional Derivative

\(D_{\vec{u}} f = \nabla f \cdot \vec{u}\) — rate of change in direction \(\vec{u}\)

Gradient (2D)

\(\nabla f = \langle f_x, f_y \rangle\) — packages both partials into one vector

Max Rate of Change

\(|\nabla f|\), in the direction of \(\nabla f\)

Min Rate of Change

\(-|\nabla f|\), opposite to \(\nabla f\)

Zero Rate of Change

Perpendicular to \(\nabla f\) — along the level curve

Tangent Plane (3D)

\(\nabla F\) is normal to the level surface \(F(x,y,z) = k\)

Big idea: The gradient is the master key of Chapter 14. It encodes direction, magnitude, and geometry of rates of change in a single vector.

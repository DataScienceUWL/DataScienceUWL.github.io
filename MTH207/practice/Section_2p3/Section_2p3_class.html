What Section 2.2 Owed Us

We left off able to estimate a limit from a table or a graph, and able to say when one fails to exist.

We could not compute a single one exactly.

Today limits become algebra. After this section you will almost never build another table.

The Limit Laws

Suppose $\displaystyle \lim_{x \to a} f(x)$ and $\displaystyle \lim_{x \to a} g(x)$ both exist. Then:

Theorem — The limit laws
  • $\displaystyle \lim [f \pm g] = \lim f \pm \lim g$   (sum, difference)
  • $\displaystyle \lim [c\,f] = c \lim f$   (constant multiple)
  • $\displaystyle \lim [f\,g] = \lim f \cdot \lim g$   (product)
  • $\displaystyle \lim \dfrac{f}{g} = \dfrac{\lim f}{\lim g}$   provided $\displaystyle \lim g \neq 0$   (quotient)
  • $\displaystyle \lim [f]^{\,n} = \left[\lim f\right]^{n}$   and   $\displaystyle \lim \sqrt[n]{f} = \sqrt[n]{\lim f}$   (power, root)

Those take a limit apart. Two more finish the job, and they are the only ones that ever produce a number:

Theorem — The two base cases
  • $\displaystyle \lim_{x \to a} c = c$
  • $\displaystyle \lim_{x \to a} x = a$

You do not need to memorise these names, and you will never be asked for one. What you do need is the hypothesis: every law above begins "suppose the limits exist", and the quotient law carries a second one — $\displaystyle \lim g \neq 0$. Those are the two things that make a law apply or not, and they are the two things you will be asked about.

The One Time We Use Them

The laws let you take a limit apart. Watch one come apart all the way down — because this is the only time we are going to.

My turn

Watch the shape before you watch the algebra: one move per line, and on the right the law that licenses it — named only so we can point at it.

$\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 3}\left(4x^{2} + x\right)$$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 3} 4x^{2} \;+\; \lim_{x \to 3} x$Sum law
$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle 4\lim_{x \to 3} x^{2} \;+\; \lim_{x \to 3} x$Constant multiple law
$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle 4\left[\lim_{x \to 3} x\right]^{2} \;+\; \lim_{x \to 3} x$Power law
$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle 4(3)^{2} + 3$Identity law, both terms
$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle 39$arithmetic — not a law

Look at where it bottomed out: $\displaystyle \lim x = a$ and $\displaystyle \lim c = c$. That is not an accident of this example — unwind any polynomial and the laws always bottom out there, which means the last line is always the same as substituting at the start.

So that was the last time we do this by hand. The laws are what prove substitution works. From here we use the conclusion, not the derivation.

Why You Can Usually Just Plug In

Apply the laws to any polynomial and they unwind to the same thing: substitute. The same works for a quotient of two polynomials, as long as the bottom one does not vanish.

Theorem — The Direct Substitution Property

If $\displaystyle f$ is a polynomial or a rational function, and $\displaystyle a$ is in the domain of $\displaystyle f$, then

$$\lim_{x \to a} f(x) \;=\; f(a).$$

Why only those two? Because those are exactly the functions the laws can reach. Start from $\displaystyle \lim x = a$ and $\displaystyle \lim c = c$, and the sum, product and quotient laws build every polynomial and every rational function — and nothing else. $\displaystyle \sin x$ and $\displaystyle e^{x}$ are not built that way, so the laws say nothing about them. Yet.

The hypothesis is the domain, not the formula. $\displaystyle \frac{t^{2}-t-12}{t-4}$ is a rational function and the Property still does not apply at $\displaystyle t=4$ — $\displaystyle 4$ is not in its domain, which is exactly what $\displaystyle 0/0$ was telling you.

Your turn

Exam 1 asks this one almost word for word, and it says justify your steps. Evaluate $\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 3} \left( 2x^2 - 5x + 1 \right)$ — and write the justification you would hand in.

$\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 3}\left(2x^{2} - 5x + 1\right)$$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle 2(3)^{2} - 5(3) + 1$Direct Substitution — $\displaystyle 3$ is in the domain
$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle 4$arithmetic

That right-hand column is what "justify your steps" means. One clause, and it is not a law name — name the property and say why it applies: "Direct Substitution, and $\displaystyle 3$ is in the domain."

When Substitution Fails

Example

$\displaystyle \lim_{t \to 4} \frac{t^2 - t - 12}{t - 4}$

Substituting gives $\displaystyle \dfrac{0}{0}$.

$\displaystyle \dfrac{0}{0}$ is not an answer and it is not zero. It means the laws do not apply yet — the quotient law needs a nonzero denominator, and you do not have one.

Show: what $\displaystyle 0/0$ is telling you

It is a signal, and it always says the same thing: rewrite the function first.

Cancelling Is Division

Put limits aside for one minute. This is an algebra question.

Your turn

Solve $\displaystyle x^{2} = 3x$. Cancel the $\displaystyle x$ from both sides and you get $\displaystyle x = 3$. Now check — is that every solution?

Show: what the cancellation cost

$\displaystyle x = 0$ solves it too: $\displaystyle 0^{2} = 3(0)$. Cancelling the $\displaystyle x$ threw that solution away, because when $\displaystyle x = 0$ that cancellation was division by zero.

Cancelling is division. Every time you cancel something you are quietly claiming it is not zero. Nearly always that claim is true and costs nothing — which is exactly why it feels automatic. When it is false, it costs you an answer.

These Are Not the Same Function

Your turn

Let $\displaystyle f(x) = \dfrac{x^{2}-9}{x-3}$ and $\displaystyle g(x) = x+3$. What is $\displaystyle g(3)$? What is $\displaystyle f(3)$?

Show: the two answers, and what they tell you

$\displaystyle g(3) = 6$. But $\displaystyle f(3) = \dfrac{0}{0}$, which is undefined — $\displaystyle 3$ is not in the domain of $\displaystyle f$ at all.

Two functions that disagree about whether $\displaystyle 3$ is even a legal input are not the same function. So the line you have been writing since algebra,

$$\frac{x^{2}-9}{x-3} \;=\; x+3,$$

is not an identity. It is true at every $\displaystyle x \neq 3$, and at $\displaystyle x = 3$ the left-hand side does not exist.

One point. That is the entire difference between the two graphs — and it is the one point a limit at $\displaystyle 3$ refuses to look at.

Factor and Cancel

The numerator and denominator share a factor. Cancel it — legally, because $\displaystyle x \neq a$ in a limit.

Your turn

Finish it: $\displaystyle \lim_{t \to 4} \frac{t^2 - t - 12}{t - 4}$ — and justify every line, the way Exam 1 will ask.

$\displaystyle \lim_{t \to 4} \frac{t^{2} - t - 12}{t - 4}$$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle \lim_{t \to 4} \frac{(t-4)(t+3)}{t-4}$substituting gave $\displaystyle 0/0$, so factor
$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle \lim_{t \to 4} (t + 3)$$\displaystyle t \neq 4$ in a limit, so cancelling is legal
$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle 7$Direct Substitution — $\displaystyle 4$ is in the domain now

The middle line is the one that earns the point, and you can now say why it is more than a slogan. Cancelling $\displaystyle t-4$ claims $\displaystyle t \neq 4$. In an equation that claim can be false and lose you an answer. Under $\displaystyle \lim_{t \to 4}$ it is free: the limit never asks about $\displaystyle t = 4$, so the claim holds at every input it does ask about.

Said the other way: $\displaystyle \frac{(t-4)(t+3)}{t-4}$ and $\displaystyle t+3$ are different functions, agreeing everywhere except $\displaystyle t = 4$. A limit at $\displaystyle 4$ cannot tell them apart — so it is allowed to use whichever one you can substitute into.

Two More Ways to Rewrite

Factoring is the common one. Two others turn up often enough to know on sight.

A root in the way? Multiply top and bottom by the conjugate — the same two terms with the sign flipped. It is multiplying by $\displaystyle 1$, so nothing changes; what it buys is $\displaystyle (a-b)(a+b) = a^{2}-b^{2}$, and squaring is what kills a square root.

A fraction inside a fraction? Combine the top into a single fraction first.

Your turn

$\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sqrt{x + 9} - 3}{x}$ — same rule: a justification on every line.

$\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sqrt{x + 9} - 3}{x}$$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sqrt{x+9} - 3}{x} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{x+9} + 3}{\sqrt{x+9} + 3}$$\displaystyle 0/0$, so multiply by $\displaystyle 1$
$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\left(\sqrt{x+9}\right)^{2} - 3^{2}}{x\left(\sqrt{x+9} + 3\right)}$$\displaystyle (a-b)(a+b) = a^{2} - b^{2}$
$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{(x+9) - 9}{x\left(\sqrt{x+9} + 3\right)}$the root is gone: $\displaystyle \left(\sqrt{x+9}\right)^{2} = x+9$
$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{x}{x\left(\sqrt{x+9} + 3\right)}$tidy the top
$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1}{\sqrt{x+9} + 3}$$\displaystyle x \neq 0$ in a limit, so cancelling is legal
$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle \frac{1}{6}$root law, and $\displaystyle 6 \neq 0$ — substitute

Why not just write "Direct Substitution" on that last line? Because the Property was stated for polynomials and rational functions, and $\displaystyle \sqrt{x+9}+3$ is neither. It is the root law doing the work — which is what the laws are there for. In 2.5 this stops being a special case.

When Nothing Rewrites: The Squeeze Theorem

Some limits cannot be rewritten into anything you can substitute into. Here is the one we are going to do:

Example
$$\lim_{x \to 0} x^{2} \sin\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)$$

Every tool from today fails on it. Substituting needs $\displaystyle \sin(1/0)$, which is not a number. There is nothing to factor and nothing to cancel, and no root to rationalise. As $\displaystyle x \to 0$ the function oscillates infinitely often.

But it is trapped.

Theorem — The Squeeze Theorem

If $\displaystyle f(x) \le g(x) \le h(x)$ near $\displaystyle a$ (except possibly at $\displaystyle a$), and $\displaystyle \lim_{x \to a} f(x) = \lim_{x \to a} h(x) = L$, then $\displaystyle \lim_{x \to a} g(x) = L$.

Three hypotheses, and students drop the third: the two bounds must agree in the limit. Trapped is not enough.

Closing the Trap

Back to the limit we are after:

$$\lim_{x \to 0} x^{2} \sin\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)$$

Since $\displaystyle -1 \le \sin(1/x) \le 1$ for every $\displaystyle x \neq 0$, multiplying by $\displaystyle x^2 \ge 0$ gives

$$-x^2 \;\le\; x^2 \sin(1/x) \;\le\; x^2 .$$
Your turn

Both outer functions are polynomials. Take their limits as $\displaystyle x \to 0$, and then say what the Squeeze Theorem lets you conclude.

Show: both bounds, and the conclusion

Justify it the way Exam 1 wants — one line per hypothesis:

  1. $\displaystyle -x^2 \le x^2\sin(1/x) \le x^2$ for every $\displaystyle x \neq 0$   (trapped near $\displaystyle 0$)
  2. $\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 0} (-x^2) = 0$ and $\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 0} x^2 = 0$   (both bounds have limits)
  3. the two limits agree   (the third hypothesis, and the one students drop)

Only then: $\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 0} x^2 \sin(1/x) = 0$ by the Squeeze Theorem.

Notice what never happened: nobody evaluated $\displaystyle g$ at $\displaystyle 0$. The theorem does not ask you to, and $\displaystyle g(0)$ does not exist. That is the whole move.

Trapped Is Not Enough

Example

$\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 0} \sin(1/x)$, which is trapped between $\displaystyle -1$ and $\displaystyle 1$.

Your turn

The bounds are $\displaystyle f(x) = -1$ and $\displaystyle h(x) = 1$. Does the Squeeze Theorem apply?

Show: why the theorem says nothing here

No. $\displaystyle \lim_{x\to 0}(-1) = -1$ and $\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 0} 1 = 1$. The bounds do not agree, so the theorem concludes nothing — and in fact the limit does not exist.

The gap has to close. A trap that stays two units wide traps nothing.

The Whole Strategy

1. Try substituting

Polynomials and rational functions with a nonzero denominator: you are already done.

2. Got $\displaystyle 0/0$?

Rewrite. Factor and cancel, multiply by the conjugate, or combine fractions — then substitute.

3. Cannot rewrite?

Look for a trap. Bound it above and below by things you can take limits of.

4. Still stuck?

The limit may not exist. Check the one-sided limits from 2.2.

In that order, every time. Most of the marks lost on this material come from starting at step 2 without trying step 1.

Where This Goes

The Direct Substitution Property covered two kinds of function — polynomials and rational functions — because those are the only two the laws can build.

But we substituted into $\displaystyle \sqrt{x+9}+3$ on Wednesday, and the laws only just reached it. You will want to do the same to $\displaystyle \sin x$, $\displaystyle e^{x}$ and $\displaystyle \ln x$, and for those the laws have nothing to say at all.

Every one of those functions turns out to have the same property anyway: $\displaystyle \lim_{x \to a} f(x) = f(a)$ at every point of its domain. Thursday we stop earning that one function at a time and name the property itself — and then it covers all of them at once. That is Section 2.5.

What You Should Be Able to Do

  • I can evaluate a limit by taking it apart with the limit laws.
  • I can check that a limit law applies before using it, including the nonzero-denominator condition.
  • I can evaluate a $\displaystyle 0/0$ limit by factoring, rationalising, or combining fractions first.
  • I can use the Squeeze Theorem and state the hypotheses it needs.

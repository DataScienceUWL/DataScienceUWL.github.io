3.2  The Product and Quotient Rules

MTH 207 Calculus I — Differentiation Rules

The Guess You Already Disproved

Five rules so far, and not one of them differentiates a product. Before Exam 1 you showed the obvious guess is false. Do it once more, because the wreckage tells us what the real rule has to look like.

Your turn

Let $\displaystyle f(x)=x^{2}$ and $\displaystyle g(x)=x^{3}$. Work out both of these.

$$\text{(i)}\quad \frac{d}{dx}\left[f(x)g(x)\right] \qquad\qquad \text{(ii)}\quad f'(x)\,g'(x)$$

No new rules — multiply first for (i).

Show: (i) f(x)g(x)=x^ 5 , so the derivative…

(i) $\displaystyle f(x)g(x)=x^{5}$, so the derivative is $\displaystyle 5x^{4}$. (ii) $\displaystyle f'(x)g'(x)=(2x)(3x^{2})=6x^{3}$. Not equal, and not even the same degree.

So $\displaystyle (fg)' \neq f'g'$. But look at what the right answer is made of: $\displaystyle 5x^{4}$, from pieces $\displaystyle x^{2}$, $\displaystyle x^{3}$, $\displaystyle 2x$, $\displaystyle 3x^{2}$. The honest question is not “what is the rule” — it is what combination of those four pieces gives $\displaystyle 5x^{4}$.

Your turn

Try $\displaystyle f'(x)\,g(x) + f(x)\,g'(x)$ on these two functions. What do you get?

Show: the answer

$\displaystyle (2x)(x^{3}) + (x^{2})(3x^{2}) = 2x^{4} + 3x^{4} = 5x^{4}$. It works.

Where the Product Rule Comes From

Theorem — The Product Rule

If $\displaystyle f$ and $\displaystyle g$ are both differentiable, then

$$\frac{d}{dx}\left[f(x)g(x)\right] \;=\; f'(x)\,g(x) \;+\; f(x)\,g'(x)$$

Learn the shape, not the letters. It is a sum of two products, and each product carries a derivative on exactly one factor — first one gets primed, then the other. Two terms, two factors apiece, one prime each.

That last part is the whole content of the previous frame: a term with two primes in it, $\displaystyle f'g'$, is the guess you already killed.

My turn

Here is why the rule looks like that. One fact first, and it is not a new one — it is the difference quotient with the $\displaystyle h$ moved across:

$$\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h} \approx f'(x) \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad f(x+h)-f(x) \approx f'(x)\,h$$

In words: when $\displaystyle x$ grows by $\displaystyle h$, $\displaystyle f$ grows by about $\displaystyle f'(x)h$.

Now picture $\displaystyle f(x)\,g(x)$ as the area of a rectangle — width $\displaystyle f(x)$, height $\displaystyle g(x)$. Let $\displaystyle x$ grow by $\displaystyle h$. The width grows by about $\displaystyle f'(x)h$, and the height by about $\displaystyle g'(x)h$.

Your turn

The rectangle gets wider and taller, so new area appears around two sides of it. How many separate pieces of new area are there, and which piece is the smallest?

Show: Three:

Three: a strip up the side, a strip along the top, and the small corner square where the two strips meet. The corner is the smallest.

Show: The two strips

The two strips. Each one is a whole side of the rectangle times the amount the other side grew:

$$\text{side strip} \;=\; f'(x)h \cdot g(x) \qquad\qquad \text{top strip} \;=\; f(x)\cdot g'(x)h$$
Show: The corner

The corner is the only piece that is small in both directions at once, so it picks up two factors of $\displaystyle h$:

$$\text{corner} \;=\; f'(x)h \cdot g'(x)h \;=\; f'(x)\,g'(x)\,h^{2}$$
Show: Add the three pieces and divide by $h$

Add the three pieces and divide by $\displaystyle h$ — which is exactly what taking a derivative does:

$$\frac{\text{new area}}{h} \;\approx\; f'(x)\,g(x) \;+\; f(x)\,g'(x) \;+\; f'(x)\,g'(x)\,h$$

Now let $\displaystyle h \to 0$. The first two terms have no $\displaystyle h$ left in them, so they survive exactly as they are — and they are the Product Rule. The third term still carries an $\displaystyle h$, so it goes to $\displaystyle 0$.

So $\displaystyle f'g'$ was never missing. It is the corner — the one piece that is small in both directions at once. It picks up two factors of $\displaystyle h$, and dividing by $\displaystyle h$ only cancels one of them. That is the real answer to the last frame: $\displaystyle (fg)' \neq f'g'$ because $\displaystyle f'g'$ is a second-order piece, not because it is nonsense.

Show: the same picture with the approximations made exact — for the curious, not for today

The argument above uses $\displaystyle \approx$, so it shows why the rule looks the way it does rather than proving it. The exact version is the same three pieces, with the approximations replaced by the definition. Start from the definition and subtract and add $\displaystyle f(x+h)g(x)$ — that single step is what cuts the growth into the side strip and the top strip:

$$\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(x+h)g(x+h)-f(x+h)g(x)+f(x+h)g(x)-f(x)g(x)}{h}$$

Group the first two terms and the last two, then factor:

$$\lim_{h\to 0}\left[f(x+h)\,\frac{g(x+h)-g(x)}{h} \;+\; g(x)\,\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}\right]$$

Both fractions are difference quotients, and $\displaystyle \lim_{h\to 0} f(x+h) = f(x)$ because $\displaystyle f$ is continuous — which we know only because $\displaystyle f$ is differentiable, from §2.8. That theorem is not decoration; it is what makes this line work. The result is $\displaystyle f(x)g'(x) + g(x)f'(x)$.

Now You: Products

Your turn

Differentiate each. Say which rule you are using before you use it.

$$\text{(a)}\;\; y = 3e^{x} \qquad \text{(b)}\;\; y = x\,e^{x} \qquad \text{(c)}\;\; y = \left(x^{2}+3x\right)e^{x}$$
Show: the first one is a trap, and the trap is the lesson

(a) $\displaystyle 3$ is a constant, not a function of $\displaystyle x$. This is the Constant Multiple Rule: $\displaystyle y' = 3e^{x}$. The Product Rule also works — it gives $\displaystyle 0\cdot e^{x} + 3e^{x}$ — but you did not need it.

(b) Now both factors move, so the Product Rule is required:

$$y' = (1)e^{x} + x\,e^{x} = e^{x}(1+x)$$

(c) Same shape, bigger first factor:

$$y' = (2x+3)e^{x} + \left(x^{2}+3x\right)e^{x} = e^{x}\left(x^{2}+5x+3\right)$$

Factoring $\displaystyle e^{x}$ out at the end is not required, but do it anyway: every one of these answers has $\displaystyle e^{x}$ in both terms, and the factored form is what you will need the moment anyone asks where $\displaystyle y'=0$.

Your turn

Now a tangent line. For $\displaystyle y = x\,e^{x}$, find the equation of the tangent at $\displaystyle x=0$.

Show: y(0) = 0 e^ 0 = 0…

$\displaystyle y(0) = 0\cdot e^{0} = 0$, and from (b), $\displaystyle y'(0) = e^{0}(1+0) = 1$. So the tangent is $\displaystyle y - 0 = 1(x-0)$, that is, $\displaystyle \mathbf{y = x}$.

Think Before You Differentiate

The Product and Quotient Rules are always correct. They are not always the fastest, and on a timed paper that difference is points.

Your turn

Differentiate these. Look hard at each one before you reach for a rule.

$$\text{(a)}\;\; H(u) = \left(u - 3\sqrt{u}\right)\left(u + 3\sqrt{u}\right) \qquad \text{(b)}\;\; f(x) = \frac{x^{3}+2x}{x}$$
Show: both collapse before any rule is needed

(a) That is a difference of squares:

$$H(u) = u^{2} - \left(3\sqrt{u}\right)^{2} = u^{2} - 9u \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad H'(u) = 2u - 9$$

(b) Divide term by term, which is legal for every $\displaystyle x\neq0$:

$$f(x) = x^{2} + 2 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad f'(x) = 2x$$

Both are Power Rule problems wearing a disguise. The Product and Quotient Rules get the same answers and take three times as long.

Before any rule: can I multiply it out, divide it through, or factor it? Algebra you already have beats a rule you just learned, and this is the same pitch as the whole course — calculus is algebra and trig plus one new idea.

The Quotient Rule, and the Order That Ruins It

Theorem — The Quotient Rule

If $\displaystyle f$ and $\displaystyle g$ are differentiable and $\displaystyle g(x)\neq 0$, then

$$\frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}\right] \;=\; \frac{f'(x)\,g(x) \;-\; f(x)\,g'(x)}{\left[g(x)\right]^{2}}$$

Say it this way and the order stays put. Write $\displaystyle \text{hi}$ for the top, $\displaystyle \text{lo}$ for the bottom, and $\displaystyle D$ for “the derivative of”:

$$\left(\frac{\text{hi}}{\text{lo}}\right)' \;=\; \frac{\text{lo}\,D\,\text{hi} \;-\; \text{hi}\,D\,\text{lo}}{\text{lo}^{2}}$$

“Lo D hi, minus hi D lo, over lo squared.” It is the same rule as above, and it is worth saying out loud until it is automatic — notice that it starts with the bottom, which is precisely the thing people get backwards.

It is proved from the Product Rule — write $\displaystyle f = (f/g)\cdot g$ and solve. We are not doing that today; it is in §3.2 of the book if you want it.

Two ways to get this wrong, and both are in tonight's homework. First: it is not $\displaystyle f'/g'$. Second, and far more common: the numerator is subtracted in one order only. $\displaystyle f'g - fg'$ is right; $\displaystyle fg' - f'g$ is its negative, and a sign error on every single problem.

Your turn

Even with lo D hi, you will doubt yourself mid-exam. So learn to check. Take $\displaystyle f(x)=x^{3}$ and $\displaystyle g(x)=x$, where you already know the answer, and run the Quotient Rule both ways. Which order survives?

Show: the check, in ten seconds

You know the answer without any rule: $\displaystyle \frac{x^{3}}{x} = x^{2}$, so the derivative is $\displaystyle 2x$.

Right order, $\displaystyle f'g$ first:

$$\frac{(3x^{2})(x) - (x^{3})(1)}{x^{2}} = \frac{3x^{3}-x^{3}}{x^{2}} = \frac{2x^{3}}{x^{2}} = 2x$$

That is the $\displaystyle 2x$ you already knew, so this order is the right one.

Wrong order, $\displaystyle fg'$ first:

$$\frac{(x^{3})(1) - (3x^{2})(x)}{x^{2}} = \frac{x^{3}-3x^{3}}{x^{2}} = \frac{-2x^{3}}{x^{2}} = -2x$$

Off by a sign, and it would be off by a sign on every problem you did.

That is a ten-second test you can run on the exam, on a function simple enough to differentiate another way. You do not have to remember the order — you have to remember that you can check it.

Now You: Quotients

Your turn

Differentiate.

$$\text{(a)}\;\; g(x) = \frac{2+5x}{3-4x} \qquad\qquad \text{(b)}\;\; y = \frac{e^{x}}{x}$$
Show: (a) — and the numerator does not simplify as much as you hope

$\displaystyle f = 2+5x$ so $\displaystyle f' = 5$;   $\displaystyle g = 3-4x$ so $\displaystyle g' = -4$.

$$y' = \frac{5(3-4x) - (2+5x)(-4)}{(3-4x)^{2}} = \frac{15-20x + 8+20x}{(3-4x)^{2}} = \frac{23}{(3-4x)^{2}}$$

The $\displaystyle x$ terms cancel. That is a feature of this problem, not a rule — but it is a good sign you got the order right, because the wrong order gives $\displaystyle -23$.

Show: (b) — and then look at it again
$$y' = \frac{e^{x}\cdot x - e^{x}\cdot 1}{x^{2}} = \frac{e^{x}(x-1)}{x^{2}}$$

In (b), factoring $\displaystyle e^{x}$ out of the numerator is what turns an answer into something you can read: it is zero exactly at $\displaystyle x=1$, and that is the question §4.1 will ask about this function.

What You Should Be Able to Do

  • State the Product Rule and the Quotient Rule, and say why $\displaystyle (fg)' \neq f'g'$.
  • Differentiate a product or a quotient, including ones with $\displaystyle e^{x}$ in them.
  • Simplify first when you can — multiply out, divide through, factor — and recognize when a rule is not needed at all.
  • Check the Quotient Rule's order on a function you already know, instead of hoping you remembered it.
  • Find a tangent line to a curve whose slope needs one of these rules.

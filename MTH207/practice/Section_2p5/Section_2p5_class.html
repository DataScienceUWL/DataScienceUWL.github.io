What 2.3 Kept Saying

Every time a limit came out easily in 2.3, the reason was the same: we could substitute.

$$\lim_{x\to 3}\left(2x^2-5x+1\right) \;=\; 2(3)^2-5(3)+1$$

And every time it did not, the reason was also the same — substituting gave $\displaystyle \tfrac00$, and the function had a hole or a break at exactly the point we cared about.

"You can just plug in" is not a lucky accident. It has a name, it has a definition, and today is that definition.

What Continuity Says

Definition — Continuous at a point

$\displaystyle f$ is continuous at $\displaystyle a$ if

$$\lim_{x\to a} f(x) \;=\; f(a)$$

That one line is quietly asking for three things at once.

Your turn

What are the three? Two of them are about the two sides of that equation existing at all.

Show: the three requirements

  1. $\displaystyle f(a)$ is defined — $\displaystyle a$ is in the domain.
  2. $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to a}f(x)$ exists — so both one-sided limits exist and agree.
  3. The two are equal.

Section 2.2 spent a day insisting $\displaystyle f(a)$ and $\displaystyle \lim_{x\to a}f(x)$ are answers to different questions. Continuity is the name for the case where they happen to agree.

Three Ways to Fail

Three requirements, so three ways to break — and each picture breaks a different one.

Your turn

For each picture, say which of the three requirements fails. Two of them fail the same one — which two, and why?

Show: which requirement each one breaks
  • Removable: the limit exists and $\displaystyle f(1)$ exists, but they disagree — requirement 3.
  • Jump: the one-sided limits disagree, so the limit does not exist — requirement 2.
  • Infinite: the limit does not exist either, and $\displaystyle f(1)$ is not defined — requirement 2 again, and 1 as well.
Jump and infinite both fail requirement 2. Only the removable one could be fixed by redefining a single value — which is where the name comes from.

Which Functions Are Continuous?

Almost every function we use, wherever it is defined:

  • polynomials — everywhere
  • rational functions — everywhere except where the denominator is zero
  • roots, and $\displaystyle \sin$, $\displaystyle \cos$, $\displaystyle \exp$, $\displaystyle \log$ — everywhere in their domains
  • sums, differences, products, quotients and compositions of continuous functions

The first two lines are old news — the limit laws gave you exactly those in 2.3, and that is why Direct Substitution was stated for polynomials and rational functions and nothing else. The third line is the new part, and it is why this section exists: the laws could never have reached $\displaystyle \sin x$ or $\displaystyle e^{x}$, because those are not built out of $\displaystyle x$ and constants by $\displaystyle +$, $\displaystyle -$, $\displaystyle \times$, $\displaystyle \div$.

Continuous Where?

My turn

Is $\displaystyle \tan x$ continuous?

The honest answer is that the question is missing a word. $\displaystyle \tan x$ is continuous at every point of its domain. But $\displaystyle x = \pi/2$ is not in its domain — there is nothing there to be continuous or discontinuous.

Compare: $\displaystyle \dfrac{1}{x}$ is continuous at every point of its domain too, and its domain does not include $\displaystyle 0$.

And $\displaystyle \sqrt{x}$? Its domain is $\displaystyle [0,\infty)$, so $\displaystyle 0$ is in the domain — but there is no left side to check. At an endpoint we ask only for the side that exists:

$$\lim_{x \to 0^{+}}\sqrt{x} \;=\; 0 \;=\; \sqrt{0},$$

which is what continuous from the right means. That is exactly why 2.3 stated the Direct Substitution Property for polynomials and rational functions only: their domains have no endpoints, so the question could not come up.

"Continuous" is always at a point, or on an interval. A function is not continuous or discontinuous in the abstract, and the domain decides which points are even candidates.

Making It Continuous On Purpose

Example 1

Find the value of $\displaystyle c$ that makes $\displaystyle g$ continuous at $\displaystyle x=2$:

$$g(x) \;=\; \begin{cases} x^2 + c, & x < 2\\[2pt] 3x - 1, & x \ge 2\end{cases}$$
Your turn

Work it. The three requirements tell you exactly what to write down.

$\displaystyle g(2) = 3(2)-1 = 5$, so requirement 1 is fine for any $\displaystyle c$.

The limit has to exist, so the two sides must agree:

$$\lim_{x\to2^-}g(x) = 4 + c \qquad \lim_{x\to2^+}g(x) = 5$$

Setting them equal: $\displaystyle 4 + c = 5$, so $\displaystyle \mathbf{c = 1}$.

With $\displaystyle c=1$ the limit is $\displaystyle 5$ and $\displaystyle g(2)=5$, so requirement 3 holds too.

Nothing was guessed. Each requirement became one line, and the only unknown fell out of requirement 2.

You Cannot Cross Without Landing

Theorem — Intermediate Value Theorem

Suppose $\displaystyle f$ is continuous on $\displaystyle [a,b]$, and $\displaystyle N$ is any number between $\displaystyle f(a)$ and $\displaystyle f(b)$. Then there is at least one $\displaystyle c$ in $\displaystyle (a,b)$ with

$$f(c) = N$$

Read it as a sentence about drawing: if you start below $\displaystyle N$ and finish above it without lifting your pen, you crossed $\displaystyle N$ somewhere. At least one $\displaystyle c$ — the theorem promises a crossing, not a single crossing.

Why "Continuous" Is the Hypothesis

Your turn

Sketch a function on $\displaystyle [0,2]$ with $\displaystyle f(0)=-1$ and $\displaystyle f(2)=1$ that never equals $\displaystyle 0$. What must your sketch have?

Show: what your sketch needs

A break — a jump straight over $\displaystyle 0$. Any such function is discontinuous somewhere in $\displaystyle [0,2]$, and that is exactly what the theorem rules out.

Drop "continuous" and the theorem is simply false. It is not decoration on the statement; it is the statement.

Using the IVT: Showing a Root Exists

Example 2

Show that $\displaystyle x^3 - x - 1 = 0$ has a solution between $\displaystyle 1$ and $\displaystyle 2$.

Your turn

You are not being asked to solve it. What are you being asked for, and what would you have to check?

Let $\displaystyle f(x) = x^3 - x - 1$. It is a polynomial, so it is continuous on $\displaystyle [1,2]$ — the hypothesis is satisfied.

$$f(1) = 1 - 1 - 1 = -1 \qquad f(2) = 8 - 2 - 1 = 5$$

$\displaystyle N = 0$ lies between $\displaystyle -1$ and $\displaystyle 5$.

By the IVT there is a $\displaystyle c$ in $\displaystyle (1,2)$ with $\displaystyle f(c)=0$. That $\displaystyle c$ solves the equation.

Three lines: continuous, the two values straddle $\displaystyle N$, cite the theorem. The IVT tells you a root exists — it never tells you where.

Where This Goes

Continuity is the hypothesis on nearly every theorem left in this course — the Extreme Value Theorem, the Mean Value Theorem, and the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus all open with "suppose $\displaystyle f$ is continuous".

Next: what happens to $\displaystyle f(x)$ as $\displaystyle x$ runs off to infinity, rather than toward a point. Same limit idea, different question.

  • State the IVT with its hypothesis, and use it to show a root exists.
  • Say why "continuous at every point of its domain" is the careful phrasing.

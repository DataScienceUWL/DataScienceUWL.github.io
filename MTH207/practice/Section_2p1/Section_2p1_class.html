First: What the Parentheses Mean in $f(x)$

$\displaystyle f$ is not a number multiplying $\displaystyle x$. It is a machine: whatever goes in the parentheses gets used everywhere $\displaystyle x$ appears in the formula.

Show: what $\displaystyle f(3)$ means

If $\displaystyle f(x) = x^2 + 1$, then $\displaystyle f(3) = 3^2 + 1 = 10$. Whatever sits inside the parentheses goes everywhere $\displaystyle x$ was — the $\displaystyle x$ is a placeholder, not a quantity.

Show: why $\displaystyle f(t)$ is the same function

So $\displaystyle f(t) = t^2 + 1$ is the same function: the letter names the slot, not the machine.

Your turn

With $\displaystyle f(x) = x^2 + 1$, write $\displaystyle f(5)$, then $\displaystyle f(a)$, then $\displaystyle f(x+h)$.

Show: the three values

$\displaystyle f(5) = 26$;   $\displaystyle f(a) = a^2 + 1$;   $\displaystyle f(x+h) = (x+h)^2 + 1$.

Your turn

Same function or different?   $\displaystyle g(x) = 3x - 4$   and   $\displaystyle g(s) = 3s - 4$.

Show: whether they differ

The same. Only the name of the slot changed. (Different would mean a different rule — $\displaystyle 3x-4$ against $\displaystyle 4x-3$, say.)

Two Questions

A geometry question.

What is the slope of the line that just touches a curve at one point?

A physics question.

A speedometer reads $\displaystyle 58$ mph right now. What does "velocity at an instant" even mean?

These look like different questions. By the end of today they will be the same question — and we will not be able to answer either one yet.

First, Lines

Every answer today is the equation of a line, so let us agree on the two forms.

Suppose you know one point on the line, $\displaystyle (x_0, y_0)$, and its slope $\displaystyle m$. Today you always will.

Show: point–slope form

Point–slope: $\displaystyle y - y_0 = m(x - x_0)$

The subscripts are doing real work. $\displaystyle x_0$ and $\displaystyle y_0$ are numbers you already know — the coordinates of your point. $\displaystyle x$ and $\displaystyle y$ have no subscript because they stay variables: they range over every other point on the line.

Show: slope–intercept form

Slope–intercept: $\displaystyle y = mx + b$, where $\displaystyle b$ is the $\displaystyle y$-value where the line crosses the $\displaystyle y$-axis.

There are two ways to get slope–intercept from a point and a slope, and both are worth having.

  • Write point–slope, then expand and simplify.
  • Start from $\displaystyle y = mx + b$, put $\displaystyle m$ in, then substitute the point and solve for $\displaystyle b$.
My turn

The line through $\displaystyle (1,2)$ with slope $\displaystyle 3$ — let me do this one both ways.

Show: using point–slope

Point–slope, then expand.

\[ y - y_0 = m(x - x_0) \]
\[ y - 2 = 3(x - 1) \]
\[ y = 3x - 3 + 2 = 3x - 1 \]
Show: or solving for $\displaystyle b$

Or solve for $\displaystyle b$.

\[ y = mx + b \]
\[ 2 = 3(1) + b \quad\Longrightarrow\quad b = -1 \]
\[ y = 3x - 1 \]

Same line, both ways.

\[ y - y_0 = m(x - x_0) \qquad\qquad y = mx + b \]
Your turn

Find the line through $\displaystyle (3, 9)$ with slope $\displaystyle 6$ — once each way. Do you get the same line?

Show: point–slope

Point–slope.

\[ y - 9 = 6(x - 3) \]
\[ y = 6x - 18 + 9 = 6x - 9 \]
Show: solving for $\displaystyle b$

Solve for $\displaystyle b$.

\[ 9 = 6(3) + b \quad\Longrightarrow\quad b = -9 \]
\[ y = 6x - 9 \]

Same line, both ways.

What Is a Tangent Line?

"Tangent" comes from the Latin tangens — touching.

A tangent should head in the same direction as the curve where it touches.

Euclid's definition works for a circle: a line meeting the curve exactly once.

For other curves it breaks down:

  • line $\displaystyle \ell$ meets the curve once but is clearly not tangent
  • line $\displaystyle t$ does look tangent — and meets the curve twice

Counting intersections is the wrong idea. We need to talk about direction.

The Tangent Problem

Example 1

Find the slope of the tangent line to $\displaystyle y = x^2$ at the point $\displaystyle P(3, 9)$.

The difficulty. Slope needs two points. We have only $\displaystyle P$.

Show: the first table

The workaround. Pick a second point $\displaystyle Q(x, x^2)$ on the curve and compute the slope of the secant line $\displaystyle PQ$ — from the Latin secans, "cutting":

\[ m_{PQ} = \frac{f(x) - f(a)}{x - a} = \frac{x^2 - 9}{x - 3} \]
Show: the second table

Then slide $\displaystyle Q$ toward $\displaystyle P$ and watch what the secant slopes do.

Sliding $Q$ Toward $P$

My turn

One of these first, to set the pattern — the secant from $\displaystyle P = (3,9)$ to $\displaystyle Q = (6, 36)$.

Show: the secant slope

$\displaystyle P = (3, 9)$ and $\displaystyle Q = (x, x^2)$, so the secant slope is

\[ m_{PQ} = \frac{x^2 - 9}{x - 3} \]

and at $\displaystyle x = 6$:

\[ m_{PQ} = \frac{36 - 9}{6 - 3} = \frac{27}{3} = 9 \]

Slide $\displaystyle Q$ in yourself — $\displaystyle y = x^2$ with $\displaystyle P$ at $\displaystyle (3,9)$.

The Secants Rotate into the Tangent

As $\displaystyle Q$ slides toward $\displaystyle P$, the secant lines pivot about $\displaystyle P$ and settle onto one line — the tangent.

We say the slope of the tangent is the limit of the slopes of the secants:

\[ m = \lim_{Q \to P} m_{PQ} = \lim_{x \to 3} \frac{x^2 - 9}{x - 3} \]

Evaluating the Limit

My turn

Why we are allowed to cancel $\displaystyle (x-3)$ is the whole point, so let me do this one.

Show: the limit to evaluate
$$m = \lim_{x \to 3} \frac{x^2 - 9}{x - 3}$$
Show: why substitution fails

Substituting $\displaystyle x = 3$ gives $\displaystyle \dfrac{0}{0}$ — nonsense. Factor instead.

$$= \lim_{x \to 3} \frac{(x-3)(x+3)}{x - 3}$$
Show: the limit
$$= \lim_{x \to 3} (x+3) \;=\; 6$$

Read that cancellation again. We divided by $\displaystyle x - 3$, and that is only legal because $\displaystyle x \neq 3$. The limit lets $\displaystyle x$ approach $\displaystyle 3$ without ever being $\displaystyle 3$ — so $\displaystyle x - 3$ is never zero, and dividing by it is fine. This one distinction is the whole content of Section 2.2.

Show: the table of slopes

So the tangent at $\displaystyle P(3,9)$ has slope $\displaystyle 6$. Using point–slope, exactly as in the warm-up:

\[ y - 9 = 6(x - 3) \qquad\text{or}\qquad y = 6x - 9 \]

The Velocity Problem

The same ball as day one, written two ways:

\[ s(t) = 64 - 16(t-1)^2 \;=\; -16t^2 + 32t + 48, \qquad 0 \le t \le 3. \]

Same function, two shapes. The left one shows the peak at a glance; the right one is easier to compute with. Choosing the convenient form is part of the work.

The difficulty. Velocity is (change in position)/(time elapsed) — but an instant is not an interval. There is nothing to divide by.

The same workaround. Average over a short interval and then shrink it.

Average Velocity over Shrinking Intervals

Example 2

Estimate the ball's velocity at the instant $\displaystyle t = 0.5$.

Compute the average velocity on $\displaystyle [0.5,\,1]$ first.

Show: the average velocities
\[ AV_{[0.5,\,1]} = \frac{s(1) - s(0.5)}{1 - 0.5} = \frac{64 - 60}{0.5} = 8\text{ ft/sec} \]
Show: the pattern they settle on

Shrinking the interval: $\displaystyle [0.5,\,0.75] \to 12$,   $\displaystyle [0.5,\,0.6] \to 14.4$,   $\displaystyle [0.5,\,0.51] \to 15.84$.

Your turn

Do not compute another one. Say what number these are heading for, and say how confident you are.

Show: the instantaneous velocity

$\displaystyle \mathbf{16}$ ft/sec — the instantaneous velocity at $\displaystyle t = 0.5$. Confidence is the honest issue: nothing here proves it is exactly $\displaystyle 16$. That is what Section 2.2 is for.

They Are the Same Problem

On the graph of $\displaystyle s(t)$, take $\displaystyle P(a, s(a))$ and $\displaystyle Q(a+h, s(a+h))$. The slope of the secant $\displaystyle PQ$ is

\[ m_{PQ} = \frac{s(a+h) - s(a)}{h} \]

which is exactly the average velocity on $\displaystyle [a,\,a+h]$.

So shrinking the interval is sliding $\displaystyle Q$ toward $\displaystyle P$.

Your turn

In the picture, one line's slope is the average velocity over $\displaystyle [a,\,a+h]$ and the other's is the instantaneous velocity at $\displaystyle t = a$. Which is which? Then say what happens to the secant as $\displaystyle h$ shrinks toward $\displaystyle 0$.

Show: which line is which

The secant through $\displaystyle P$ and $\displaystyle Q$ gives the average; the line touching only at $\displaystyle P$ gives the instantaneous. As $\displaystyle h \to 0$, $\displaystyle Q$ slides into $\displaystyle P$ and the secant turns into the tangent.

Slope of the secant $\displaystyle =$ average rate of change. Slope of the tangent $\displaystyle =$ instantaneous rate of change. One idea, two vocabularies.

What We Still Owe

Both problems ended in the same place: a quantity we could only approach, never evaluate directly.

Tangent

Slope $\displaystyle = \lim_{x \to 3} \dfrac{x^2-9}{x-3}$ — substituting gives $\displaystyle 0/0$.

Velocity

Speed $\displaystyle = \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{s(a+h)-s(a)}{h}$ — substituting gives $\displaystyle 0/0$.

We have no rules yet for computing a limit. That is Sections 2.2 through 2.6 — and we return to tangents and velocities in Section 2.7 with the derivative.

What You Should Be Able to Do

By the end of this section — and on the quiz and the exam:

  • I can compute the average rate of change of a function on an interval and explain why it is the slope of a secant line.
  • I can estimate the slope of a tangent line by computing average rates of change over shrinking intervals.
  • I can explain the difference between average and instantaneous velocity, and say which one a secant slope gives.

If you cannot honestly tick one of these off, that is the one to work on — not the one that felt hardest in class.

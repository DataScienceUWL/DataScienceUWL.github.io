Horizontal Asymptotes: What Is Even Being Asked

On Quiz 3, nine of you scored zero on a two-point horizontal asymptote question — and only one of those was an arithmetic slip. The rest solved $\displaystyle x^{2}+4=0$, or set the numerator to zero, or substituted a number, or wrote a difference quotient.

Every one of those is a real procedure, correctly done. It is just answering a different question. A horizontal asymptote is a statement about $\displaystyle x\to\pm\infty$, so the only move that finds one is a limit at infinity. Nothing is ever substituted for $\displaystyle x$.

Your turn

Find all horizontal asymptotes of

$$f(x) \;=\; \frac{2x-7}{\sqrt{9x^{2}+4}}$$

There is more than one. Do each direction separately.

Show: both directions, and they differ

Divide top and bottom by $\displaystyle x$. Going right, $\displaystyle x>0$ so $\displaystyle x=\sqrt{x^{2}}$:

$$\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{2-7/x}{\sqrt{9+4/x^{2}}} \;=\; \frac{2}{3}$$

Going left, $\displaystyle x<0$, so $\displaystyle x=-\sqrt{x^{2}}$ and the root picks up a minus:

$$\lim_{x\to-\infty}\frac{2-7/x}{-\sqrt{9+4/x^{2}}} \;=\; -\frac{2}{3}$$

Two asymptotes: $\displaystyle y=\frac23$ and $\displaystyle y=-\frac23$. Both are lines, so both answers are equations.

This one problem is the two things Quiz 3 caught, together: a function really can have two different horizontal asymptotes, and the sign of $\displaystyle x$ is what makes them different. If you can do this one you can do the exam's.

The Other Kind of Asymptote

“Asymptote” on its own does not say which kind. Exam 1 asks about both, and a single function can carry one of each.

Your turn

For $\displaystyle f(x)=\frac{x-5}{x+2}$, find

$$\lim_{x\to-2^{+}}f(x) \qquad\text{and}\qquad \lim_{x\to-2^{-}}f(x)$$

and then say what your answers tell you about the graph. No calculator — name each factor.

Show: name the pieces, then read the sign off

At $\displaystyle x=-2$ the numerator is $\displaystyle -7$, nowhere near zero; only the denominator is small. So nothing gets evaluated — each piece gets named:

$$\frac{\text{about } -7}{(\text{small, positive})} = -\infty \qquad\qquad \frac{\text{about } -7}{(\text{small, negative})} = +\infty$$

The two sides disagree, so $\displaystyle \lim_{x\to-2}f(x)$ does not exist — not even as $\displaystyle \infty$. What that says about the graph: $\displaystyle x=-2$ is a vertical asymptote, the curve dropping on the right of it and rising on the left.

Same $\displaystyle f$, other question. Divide by $\displaystyle x$: $\displaystyle \lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\frac{1-5/x}{1+2/x}=1$, so $\displaystyle y=1$ is a horizontal asymptote too. One function, two asymptotes, and the two calculations have nothing in common. Vertical comes from $\displaystyle f\to\pm\infty$ at a finite $\displaystyle a$; horizontal comes from $\displaystyle x\to\pm\infty$. Writing $\displaystyle \infty$ in your answer is not what makes one vertical — where $\displaystyle x$ is headed is.

A Radical at Infinity

Your turn
$$\lim_{x\to-\infty}\left(\sqrt{x^{2}+5x} \;+\; x\right)$$

Substituting gives $\displaystyle \infty - \infty$, which is not a number. Multiply by the conjugate.

$$\sqrt{x^{2}+5x}+x \;=\; \frac{\left(\sqrt{x^{2}+5x}+x\right)\left(\sqrt{x^{2}+5x}-x\right)}{\sqrt{x^{2}+5x}-x} \;=\; \frac{5x}{\sqrt{x^{2}+5x}-x}$$

Now divide top and bottom by $\displaystyle x$ — and out at $\displaystyle -\infty$, $\displaystyle x=-\sqrt{x^{2}}$:

$$\lim_{x\to-\infty}\frac{5}{-\sqrt{1+5/x}-1} \;=\; \frac{5}{-1-1} \;=\; -\frac52$$

Same sign step as the last frame, and as Quiz 3 problem 3. Three different-looking problems, one hypothesis: out at $\displaystyle -\infty$ the variable is negative, so $\displaystyle \sqrt{x^{2}}$ is $\displaystyle -x$.

Seven Points for Writing a Result Down

The paper asks you to state one of these. It does not say which, so you need all five cold.

limits equal $\displaystyle L$, THEN $\displaystyle \lim_{x\to a}g(x)=L$

$\displaystyle f(a)$ and $\displaystyle f(b)$, THEN $\displaystyle f(c)=N$ for some $\displaystyle c$ in $\displaystyle (a,b)$

Your turn

Close everything. Write the Squeeze Theorem — hypothesis and conclusion, in full sentences. Then do the same for one more from the list, without looking.

Show: what a complete statement contains

If $\displaystyle f(x) \le g(x) \le h(x)$ near $\displaystyle a$ (except possibly at $\displaystyle a$), and $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to a}f(x) = \lim_{x\to a}h(x) = L$, then $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to a}g(x) = L$.

Four pieces, and each one is worth marks: the inequality with $\displaystyle g$ in the middle, that it holds near $\displaystyle a$, that the two outer limits are equal, and that the conclusion is about $\displaystyle g$.

The two outer limits agreeing is the whole engine. If they disagree the theorem says nothing at all.

This is the one question on the paper where knowing it and writing it are the same act. Seven points, no computation — and the cheapest seven points on the paper if you have actually memorized all five.

``Give an Example of a Function Such That ...''

Seven points of the exam ask you to produce a function, not analyze one. A good answer is short and checkable.

Your turn

Give a function $\displaystyle f$ with $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to2}f(x)=5$ but $\displaystyle f(2)\neq 5$. Then one with $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to2}f(x)$ not existing at all.

Show: two answers, both one line

First: $\displaystyle f(x)=5$ for $\displaystyle x\neq 2$ and $\displaystyle f(2)=0$. The limit ignores the value at the point, so it is $\displaystyle 5$; the value is $\displaystyle 0$.

Second: $\displaystyle f(x)=\frac{|x-2|}{x-2}$. From the right it is $\displaystyle 1$, from the left $\displaystyle -1$, so the limit does not exist.

Both are pieces you have already met. You are not being asked to invent anything — you are being asked to remember one.

Build a small stock before Tuesday: $\displaystyle |x|$ for a corner, $\displaystyle \frac1x$ for a blow-up, $\displaystyle x^{3}$ for a horizontal tangent that is not a turning point, a piecewise function for anything about values versus limits. Four functions answer most of these.

One Trap, and It Is Worth 15 Points

You learned the Power Rule yesterday. One question on Exam 1 hands you a function and says, in bold, to use the definition of the derivative — and that using shortcut rules will earn no points. That is every rule from §3.1, not just the Power Rule.

Your turn

Practice the shape. $\displaystyle f(x)=2-\frac{4}{x}$. Find $\displaystyle f'(1)$ from the definition, keeping $\displaystyle \lim$ on every line until you take it.

$$f'(1)=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(1+h)-f(1)}{h} =\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\left(2-\frac{4}{1+h}\right)-(-2)}{h} =\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{4-\frac{4}{1+h}}{h}$$

Common denominator on top:

$$=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\frac{4(1+h)-4}{1+h}}{h} =\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{4h}{h(1+h)} =\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{4}{1+h}=4$$

Check it with the Power Rule afterwards — $\displaystyle f'(x)=4x^{-2}$, so $\displaystyle f'(1)=4$. Use it to check, never to answer, on that question.

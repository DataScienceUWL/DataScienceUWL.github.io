What Section 2.1 Owed Us

Two problems, one answer. The tangent slope and the instantaneous velocity both came out as the same move: compute something you can on an interval, then shrink the interval.

Both times we said the numbers "approach" a value and moved on.

Today we say what "approach" actually means. That is the one new idea the whole course rests on.

Estimating a Limit from a Table

Consider $\displaystyle f(x) = \dfrac{x^2-1}{x-1}$.

Estimate

$$\lim_{x \to 1} \frac{x^2-1}{x-1}$$

from values of $\displaystyle f(x)$ on either side of $\displaystyle 1$.

Values of f(x) as x approaches 1 from both sides
$\displaystyle x$$\displaystyle 0.9$$\displaystyle 0.99$$\displaystyle 0.999$$\displaystyle 1.001$$\displaystyle 1.01$$\displaystyle 1.1$
$\displaystyle f(x)$$\displaystyle 1.9$$\displaystyle 1.99$$\displaystyle 1.999$$\displaystyle 2.001$$\displaystyle 2.01$$\displaystyle 2.1$
Your turn

Two different questions. What is $\displaystyle f(1)$? And what is this table heading for? Answer both — then say which of the two the limit is asking.

Show: the two answers, and which one is the limit

$\displaystyle f(1)$ does not exist. The table heads for $\displaystyle \mathbf{2}$.

The limit asks only the second question.

We write

$$\lim_{x \to 1} f(x) = 2$$

even though $\displaystyle f(1)$ does not exist. The limit never asks about the point itself.

What a Limit Means

Definition — Limit of a function

We write

$$\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L$$

if we can make the values of $\displaystyle f(x)$ as close to $\displaystyle L$ as we like by taking $\displaystyle x$ close enough to $\displaystyle a$, but not equal to $\displaystyle a$.

Say it out loud like this: "the limit, as $\displaystyle x$ approaches $\displaystyle a$, of $\displaystyle f$ of $\displaystyle x$, equals $\displaystyle L$."

And in your head, say what it means:

"As $\displaystyle x$ gets close to $\displaystyle a$, $\displaystyle f(x)$ gets close to $\displaystyle L$."

Read the last four words of the definition again. They are doing all the work.

  • The limit does not care whether $\displaystyle f(a)$ exists.
  • If $\displaystyle f(a)$ does exist, the limit does not care what it equals.

$\displaystyle f(a)$ and $\displaystyle \lim_{x\to a} f(x)$ are answers to two different questions. Getting them confused is the most expensive mistake in this chapter.

Reading Limits off a Graph

Your turn

From the graph: $\displaystyle \lim_{x\to1}f(x)$,   $\displaystyle f(1)$,   $\displaystyle \lim_{x\to3}f(x)$,   $\displaystyle \lim_{x\to5}f(x)$,   $\displaystyle f(5)$. Say if one does not exist.

Show: what happens at $\displaystyle x=1$
$$\lim_{x\to1}f(x) = 2, \qquad f(1) = 4$$
Show: what happens at $\displaystyle x=3$
$$\lim_{x\to3}f(x) \ \textbf{does not exist}$$
Show: what happens at $\displaystyle x=5$
$$\lim_{x\to5}f(x) = 3 = f(5)$$

Three different things happen at $\displaystyle x=1$, $\displaystyle x=3$ and $\displaystyle x=5$. Only one of them is a function behaving itself.

When a Table Lies to You

A table is evidence, not proof. Consider $\displaystyle f(x) = \sin(\pi/x)$ near $\displaystyle 0$.

Your turn

Compute $\displaystyle f(x)$ at $\displaystyle x = 1$, $\displaystyle \tfrac12$, $\displaystyle \tfrac13$, $\displaystyle \tfrac14$, $\displaystyle \tfrac15$. What would you conclude?

Show: what the table says

Every one gives $\displaystyle \sin(n\pi) = 0$. A table says the limit is $\displaystyle 0$.

Show: what the graph shows

The limit does not exist. The function swings between $\displaystyle -1$ and $\displaystyle 1$ infinitely often, and your table happened to sample the zeros every time.

Approaching from One Side

At $\displaystyle x=3$ the limit failed — but not because nothing happened. Two different things happened, one from each side.

Definition — One-sided limits

The left-hand limit — approach $\displaystyle a$ through values less than $\displaystyle a$:

$$\lim_{x \to a^-} f(x) = L$$

The right-hand limit — approach $\displaystyle a$ through values greater than $\displaystyle a$:

$$\lim_{x \to a^+} f(x) = L$$
Your turn

Go back to the graph. What are $\displaystyle \lim_{x\to3^-}f(x)$ and $\displaystyle \lim_{x\to3^+}f(x)$?

Show: the two one-sided limits

$\displaystyle 3$ from the left, $\displaystyle 1$ from the right.

$$\lim_{x \to 3^-} f(x) = 3$$
$$\lim_{x \to 3^+} f(x) = 1$$

$\displaystyle \lim_{x\to a}f(x) = L$ exactly when both one-sided limits exist and both equal $\displaystyle L$. At $\displaystyle x=3$ they exist and disagree, so the two-sided limit does not exist.

Limits That Grow Without Bound

My turn

Look at $\displaystyle g(x) = \dfrac{1}{x^2}$ as $\displaystyle x \to 0$. As $\displaystyle x$ gets small, $\displaystyle x^2$ gets very small and positive, so $\displaystyle 1/x^2$ gets enormous — from both sides.

% no-reveal — states its own answer

We write $\displaystyle \lim_{x\to0} \dfrac{1}{x^2} = \infty$.

This is not a number. Writing $\displaystyle \infty$ is shorthand for "the values grow past any bound you name" — it is a description of how the limit fails, not a value it reaches.

Your turn

Now try $\displaystyle h(x) = \dfrac{1}{x}$ as $\displaystyle x \to 0$. Check both sides separately. Is the answer the same?

Show: approaching from the right
$$\text{From the right:}\quad \frac1x \to \infty$$
Show: approaching from the left
$$\text{From the left:}\quad \frac1x \to -\infty$$
Show: what that means for the limit

The two sides disagree, so $\displaystyle \lim_{x\to0}\frac1x$ does not exist — not even as $\displaystyle \infty$.

Vertical Asymptotes

Definition — Vertical asymptote

The line $\displaystyle x = a$ is a vertical asymptote of $\displaystyle y=f(x)$ if at least one of the one-sided limits at $\displaystyle a$ is $\displaystyle \infty$ or $\displaystyle -\infty$.

Note what this does not say. It says nothing about whether $\displaystyle f(a)$ exists — only about how $\displaystyle f$ behaves nearby.

Your turn

Find the vertical asymptotes of $\displaystyle f(x) = \dfrac{x+1}{(x-2)(x+3)}$. Then decide the sign of $\displaystyle f$ just to the right of $\displaystyle x=2$.

Show: where the denominator vanishes
$$\text{Denominator} = 0 \text{ at } x = 2 \text{ and } x = -3$$
Show: the sign of each factor
$$\text{Just right of } 2:\quad x+1 \approx 3 > 0, \quad x-2 = \text{small} > 0, \quad x+3 \approx 5 > 0$$
Show: the sign of $\displaystyle f$
$$\text{All three positive, so } f \to +\infty$$

Finding where an asymptote is takes algebra. Finding which way the graph goes takes a sign check on each factor.

Where This Goes

We can now estimate a limit from a table or a graph, and say precisely when one fails to exist.

What we can do

Read a limit off a picture. Recognise a jump, a hole, an oscillation, an asymptote.

What we cannot do yet

Compute a limit exactly, from a formula, without guessing from numbers.

What You Should Be Able to Do

By the end of this section — and on the quiz and the exam:

  • I can estimate a limit from a table or a graph, and explain why the value of the function at the point does not affect it.
  • I can decide whether a two-sided limit exists by comparing the one-sided limits.
  • I can recognise an infinite limit and locate the vertical asymptotes of a function.
  • I can explain how a table of values or a calculator can mislead me about a limit.

If you cannot honestly tick one of these off, that is the one to work on — not the one that felt hardest in class.

MTH 207 Calculus I — Limits and Derivatives

One Letter Changes Everything

Last section we computed $\displaystyle f'(2)$, $\displaystyle f'(3)$, $\displaystyle s'(0.5)$ — a number each time, the slope at one point. Doing that once per point is honest work and it does not scale.

Your turn

$\displaystyle f'(a)$ and $\displaystyle f'(x)$ are written almost identically. What is the difference, and why is it worth a section of its own?

In $\displaystyle f'(a)$ the point $\displaystyle a$ is a constant you chose, so $\displaystyle f'(a)$ is a number. In $\displaystyle f'(x)$ the point is the variable $\displaystyle x$, so $\displaystyle f'$ is a function — one output for every input where the limit exists.

Compute it once, with the letter left in, and you have every $\displaystyle f'(a)$ at the same time.

$\displaystyle f'(a)$ is a number. $\displaystyle f'(x)$ is a function. The section is the move from one to the other, and the move costs nothing — it is the same limit with $\displaystyle a$ not yet chosen.

The Derivative Function

Definition — The derivative of $\displaystyle f$
$$f'(x) \;=\; \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}$$

for every $\displaystyle x$ at which this limit exists. Where it exists, $\displaystyle f$ is differentiable.

My turn

$\displaystyle f(x) = x^{2} - 3x$ — the same function as last time. Find $\displaystyle f'(x)$.

$\displaystyle f'(x)$$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}$the definition, with $\displaystyle x$ left alone
$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\left[(x+h)^{2} - 3(x+h)\right] - \left[x^{2} - 3x\right]}{h}$both brackets on the page — this is where the sign slips
$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{x^{2} + 2xh + h^{2} - 3x - 3h - x^{2} + 3x}{h}$multiply out, and distribute the minus
$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{2xh + h^{2} - 3h}{h}$collect — every surviving term has an $\displaystyle h$
$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{h(2x + h - 3)}{h}$factor $\displaystyle h$ out of the numerator
$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle \lim_{h\to 0}\,(2x + h - 3)$cancel $\displaystyle h$: the limit never uses $\displaystyle h = 0$
$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle 2x - 3$now substitute $\displaystyle h = 0$

Check it against last time: $\displaystyle f'(2) = 2(2) - 3 = 1$, which is the number we worked for a whole frame on Thursday. One computation now covers every point at once.

Nothing in that chain is new. The only difference from Thursday is that $\displaystyle x$ stayed a letter — and the payoff is a formula instead of a number.

Now You

Your turn

$\displaystyle f(x) = 3x^{2} - x$. Find $\displaystyle f'(x)$ from the definition:

$$f'(x) \;=\; \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}$$

Then use it to write down $\displaystyle f'(0)$ and $\displaystyle f'(2)$ without doing any more limits.

$\displaystyle f'(x)$$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\left[3(x+h)^{2} - (x+h)\right] - \left[3x^{2} - x\right]}{h}$the definition
$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{3x^{2} + 6xh + 3h^{2} - x - h - 3x^{2} + x}{h}$multiply out, and distribute the minus
$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{6xh + 3h^{2} - h}{h}$collect
$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{h(6x + 3h - 1)}{h}$factor $\displaystyle h$ out
$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle \lim_{h\to 0}\,(6x + 3h - 1)$cancel $\displaystyle h$
$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle 6x - 1$substitute $\displaystyle h = 0$

$\displaystyle f'(0) = -1$ and $\displaystyle f'(2) = 11$ — substitution into the formula, no limits required. That is what the section bought.

Four Ways to Write the Same Thing

$$f'(x) \;=\; y' \;=\; \frac{dy}{dx} \;=\; \frac{d}{dx}f(x)$$

$\displaystyle \frac{dy}{dx}$ is Leibniz notation. It is not a fraction — it is one symbol — but it is built to look like the quotient it came from, and it carries the variables with it, which is why every later chapter uses it.

To say “the derivative at a point” in Leibniz notation you have to say where:

$$f'(2) \;=\; \left.\frac{dy}{dx}\right|_{x=2}$$

$\displaystyle \frac{dy}{dx}$ tells you what is changing and with respect to what. In a problem with more than one letter in it, that is not decoration — it is the only notation that stays unambiguous.

Differentiable at a Point

Definition — Differentiable at a point

$\displaystyle f$ is differentiable at $\displaystyle a$ when

$$f'(a) \;=\; \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}$$

exists. $\displaystyle f$ is differentiable on an interval when it is differentiable at every point of that interval.

Nothing new is being defined — that is the same limit you have been computing all period. What is new is the question: does it exist? So far it always has. Here is one where it does not.

My turn

$\displaystyle f(x) = |x|$ at $\displaystyle a = 0$. Compute $\displaystyle f'(0)$ — or show it does not exist.

$$f'(0) \;=\; \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{|0+h| - |0|}{h} \;=\; \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{|h|}{h}$$

There is nothing left to simplify, and no $\displaystyle h$ to cancel. But $\displaystyle |h|$ means two different things depending on the sign of $\displaystyle h$, so take the two sides separately.

From the right, $\displaystyle h > 0$, so $\displaystyle |h| = h$:

$$\lim_{h\to 0^{+}}\frac{|h|}{h} \;=\; \lim_{h\to 0^{+}}\frac{h}{h} \;=\; \lim_{h\to 0^{+}} 1 \;=\; 1$$

From the left, $\displaystyle h < 0$, so $\displaystyle |h| = -h$:

$$\lim_{h\to 0^{-}}\frac{|h|}{h} \;=\; \lim_{h\to 0^{-}}\frac{-h}{h} \;=\; \lim_{h\to 0^{-}} (-1) \;=\; -1$$

The one-sided limits are $\displaystyle 1$ and $\displaystyle -1$. They disagree, so the limit does not exist — and $\displaystyle f'(0)$ is that limit.

So $\displaystyle f(x) = |x|$ is not differentiable at $\displaystyle 0$. Everywhere else it is: near any $\displaystyle a > 0$ the graph is the line $\displaystyle y = x$, and near any $\displaystyle a < 0$ it is the line $\displaystyle y = -x$. No computation needed — look at it in a moment and you will see why.

$$f(x) = |x| \text{ is differentiable on } (-\infty, 0) \cup (0, \infty).$$

“Does not exist” is a real answer here, and it is the first time in this section that it is. Notice what did the work: not the algebra — there was none — but the two sides disagreeing. That is a limit failing in exactly the way §2.2 taught you limits fail.

Differentiable Is Where There Is a Tangent Line

Start with a curve where nothing goes wrong.

Pick any point on that curve and there is exactly one line through it that matches the curve's direction. Three are drawn, and nothing makes those three special — you could draw one at every point, and the slope you would get is $\displaystyle f'(a)$.

So this $\displaystyle f$ is differentiable on $\displaystyle (-\infty, \infty)$. Most functions you have met are like this.

Now the one from a moment ago.

Away from $\displaystyle 0$ there is no ambiguity either: the graph is already a straight line, so the tangent line is that line, slope $\displaystyle -1$ on the left and $\displaystyle +1$ on the right. At the corner, each side hands you a different line and there is no way to pick one — which is the picture of the two one-sided limits you computed disagreeing.

$\displaystyle f$ is differentiable at $\displaystyle a$ exactly where the graph has one clear tangent line at $\displaystyle a$ — one line, with a slope you could write down. The cubic has one everywhere. $\displaystyle |x|$ has one everywhere except a single point.

(1) Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to-\infty}\frac{\sqrt{9x^{2}+1}}{2x-3}$. Show the step where the sign of $\displaystyle x$ matters.

$\displaystyle \sqrt{9x^{2}+1} = \sqrt{x^{2}}\sqrt{9 + 1/x^{2}} = |x|\sqrt{9+1/x^{2}}$, and on this side $\displaystyle x < 0$, so $\displaystyle |x| = -x$:

Your turn

(2) Show that $\displaystyle x^{5} + 2x = 5$ has a solution between $\displaystyle 1$ and $\displaystyle 2$. Name the theorem and check its hypothesis.

Let $\displaystyle f(x) = x^{5} + 2x - 5$, so a solution is a zero of $\displaystyle f$.

$\displaystyle f$ is a polynomial, so it is continuous on $\displaystyle [1,2]$ — that is the hypothesis, and it is the part people skip.

$\displaystyle f(1) = -2 < 0$ and $\displaystyle f(2) = 31 > 0$, so $\displaystyle 0$ lies between them.

By the Intermediate Value Theorem there is a $\displaystyle c$ in $\displaystyle (1,2)$ with $\displaystyle f(c) = 0$.

How to Read a Theorem

Day 13 — Tue Sep 29, before the quiz

Every theorem in this course has one shape:

$$\textbf{IF } \; \underbrace{A}_{\text{the hypothesis}} \;\textbf{, THEN }\; \underbrace{B}_{\text{the conclusion}}$$

The hypothesis is the price. The conclusion is what it buys. If the price is not paid, you do not get the conclusion — even in cases where the conclusion happens to be true anyway.

Two you already own, written in that shape:

Theorem — Intermediate Value Theorem

IF $\displaystyle f$ is continuous on $\displaystyle [a,b]$, and $\displaystyle N$ is between $\displaystyle f(a)$ and $\displaystyle f(b)$,  THEN there is a $\displaystyle c$ in $\displaystyle (a,b)$ with $\displaystyle f(c)=N$.

Theorem — Continuity, from §2.5

IF $\displaystyle f$ is continuous at $\displaystyle a$,  THEN $\displaystyle \lim_{x\to a}f(x) = f(a)$.

A True/False question is almost never asking whether you remember the theorem. It is asking whether the statement in front of you actually pays the price the theorem charges.

Answering a True/False, in Two Moves

If you think it is TRUE: name the theorem, and show its hypotheses are met. “True by the IVT” is half an answer — the other half is why the IVT applies here.

If you think it is FALSE: give one counterexample. It has to do two things at once — satisfy the hypothesis exactly as the statement gives it, and break the conclusion. One is enough. You do not owe an explanation of why the statement is generally wrong.

Your turn

Two statements. True or false, and say why in one sentence.

  1. If $\displaystyle f$ is continuous at $\displaystyle 3$, then $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to3}f(x) = f(3)$.
  2. If $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to3}f(x) = 5$, then $\displaystyle f(3) = 5$.

1. True. That is the theorem, and its hypothesis — $\displaystyle f$ continuous at $\displaystyle 3$ — is handed to you in the statement. Naming it is the entire answer.

2. False. Take

$$f(x) = \begin{cases} 5 & x \neq 3\\ 0 & x = 3\end{cases}$$

The limit as $\displaystyle x\to3$ is $\displaystyle 5$, and $\displaystyle f(3) = 0$. One counterexample, done — and it is the first thing you learned about limits in §2.2: a limit never looks at the point.

Now look at what happened. Statement 2 is statement 1 read backwards, with the word continuous quietly missing. Reading a theorem backwards is the single most common way to get one of these wrong. The converse of a theorem is not the theorem.

Two habits. Read what you are given, not what the example in your notes was given — a statement that hands you numbers but never says continuous or differentiable has left something out on purpose. And when a statement looks like a theorem you know, check whether it is that theorem or its converse.

Four Ways to Have No Tangent Line

Your turn

A corner is one way. Think in pictures, not formulas: what else could a graph do at a point that would leave you with no tangent line?

A break. $\displaystyle f$ is not even continuous at $\displaystyle a$. You can rule this one out without writing a difference quotient at all.

A corner. The two one-sided slopes are different numbers — $\displaystyle -1$ and $\displaystyle +1$ for $\displaystyle |x|$.

A cusp. The two one-sided slopes run off in opposite directions, one to $\displaystyle +\infty$ and one to $\displaystyle -\infty$, so the curve comes to a point.

A vertical tangent. The slopes run to $\displaystyle +\infty$ from both sides. There genuinely is a tangent line here — it is vertical — but a vertical line has no slope, so $\displaystyle f'(a)$ still does not exist.

Name which one. “Not differentiable” on its own does not earn the point; a cusp at $\displaystyle x=2$ does.

Differentiable Means Continuous. Not the Reverse.

Theorem — Differentiability implies continuity

If $\displaystyle f$ is differentiable at $\displaystyle a$, then $\displaystyle f$ is continuous at $\displaystyle a$.

You have already used this, backwards. “A break means no derivative” was the first item on the list you just made — and that is this theorem read in reverse: no continuity, so no differentiability.

Your turn

Is the converse true? If $\displaystyle f$ is continuous at $\displaystyle a$, must $\displaystyle f$ be differentiable at $\displaystyle a$?

No. $\displaystyle f(x) = |x|$ is continuous at $\displaystyle 0$ — you can draw it without lifting the pencil — and we computed $\displaystyle f'(0)$ and found it does not exist. One counterexample is all a converse needs.

Continuity says the graph does not break. Differentiability says it does not break and has no corner, no cusp and no vertical tangent. It is the stronger condition, so it implies the weaker one and not the other way round.

Differentiable $\displaystyle \Rightarrow$ continuous. Continuous $\displaystyle \not\Rightarrow$ differentiable. Reading that arrow backwards is the single most common error in this chapter, and it is worth three points on the final.

From a Graph of $f$ to a Graph of $f'$

You have a sheet with a curve on it. You are not given its formula and you do not need it. A derivative is a slope, and a slope is something you can measure with a straight edge.

My turn

One point together. Estimate the slope of $\displaystyle f$ at $\displaystyle x=-1$, write it in the table, and plot it on the lower grid.

Lay the straight edge so it just grazes the curve at $\displaystyle x=-1$. Go one unit right — then the rise is the slope, with no dividing to do. Here the rise is $\displaystyle 1.5$, so $\displaystyle f'(-1) = 1.5$. Write $\displaystyle 1.5$ in the table, and plot the single point $\displaystyle (-1,\,1.5)$ on the lower grid.

Your turn

Now the other six points. Fill in the table, plot every one, then connect them with a smooth curve.

A parabola — and you never had a formula for anything.

Two readings to check against the top graph. Your curve crosses zero at $\displaystyle x=\pm2$, which is exactly where $\displaystyle f$ levels off. And your curve is highest at $\displaystyle x=0$, which is where $\displaystyle f$ climbs most steeply.

Watch it draw itself in CalcLens — the same curve and the same window. Drag the point across and each tangent slope drops onto the lower axis; Sweep and trace does continuously what you just did at seven points. Show the true $\displaystyle f'$ is there when you want to check yourself, so leave it alone until your own curve is drawn.

You just built a whole function out of slopes, one point at a time. That is all $\displaystyle f'$ is — and it is why $\displaystyle f'$ deserves to be called a function rather than a number.

The Picture: $f$ Above, $f'$ Below

Three readings, and they are the whole skill: $\displaystyle f'>0$ where $\displaystyle f$ rises, $\displaystyle f'<0$ where $\displaystyle f$ falls, and $\displaystyle f'=0$ exactly where $\displaystyle f$ levels off.

Doing It Again

$\displaystyle f'$ is a function, so it has a derivative of its own:

$$f''(x) \;=\; \left(f'\right)'(x), \qquad f'''(x), \qquad f^{(4)}(x), \ \ldots$$

If $\displaystyle f(x) = x^{2} - 3x$ then $\displaystyle f'(x) = 2x - 3$, and differentiating again gives $\displaystyle f''(x) = 2$. One more gives $\displaystyle f'''(x) = 0$.

Your turn

You are a passenger in a car with every one of your five senses switched off. What can you still feel?

Almost everyone says speed. You cannot feel speed — sitting still in a parked car and sitting in a smooth plane at $\displaystyle 500$ mph feel identical, which is the whole content of the answer.

What you feel is acceleration, which is $\displaystyle s''(t)$ — you feel it as a force, which is why $\displaystyle F = ma$. And you feel jerk, $\displaystyle s'''(t)$, the rate at which the acceleration itself changes: it is what makes a sudden stop unpleasant, and what a good driver is smoothing out.

$\displaystyle s'$ is velocity. $\displaystyle s''$ is acceleration. $\displaystyle s'''$ is jerk. Your body is a second- and third-derivative detector and a very poor first-derivative one.

  • Name the three ways differentiability fails, and identify which one a given graph shows.
  • Say why differentiable implies continuous, and give a function that is continuous but not differentiable.
  • Compute a second derivative and say what it means about the original.

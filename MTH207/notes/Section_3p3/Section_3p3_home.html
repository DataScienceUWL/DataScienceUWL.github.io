What Does the Derivative of Sine Look Like?

None of the six rules you have touches $\displaystyle \sin x$. But you do not need a rule to see what its derivative is — you have been reading $\displaystyle f'$ off the graph of $\displaystyle f$ since §2.8.

Your turn

Sketch $\displaystyle y'$ on the lower axes. Three questions to guide it:

  1. Where is the slope of $\displaystyle \sin x$ zero?
  2. Where is it steepest, and is it positive or negative there?
  3. What is the largest the slope ever gets?
Then look at your sketch: what familiar function is it?

At every peak and trough of $\displaystyle \sin x$ the tangent is horizontal, so $\displaystyle y'$ must be zero there. Between those places $\displaystyle \sin x$ rises and falls, so $\displaystyle y'$ swings positive and negative. The steepest point is $\displaystyle x=0$, and there the slope is exactly $\displaystyle 1$.

That is $\displaystyle \cos x$.

So $\displaystyle \frac{d}{dx}\sin x$ looks like $\displaystyle \cos x$. A sketch is not a proof — it cannot tell you the peak is exactly $\displaystyle 1$ rather than $\displaystyle 0.98$. The rest of today is earning that.

Two Limits You Cannot Substitute Into

You think it is $\displaystyle \cos x$. To prove it we go where every rule in this course has come from — the definition — and the definition leaves behind two limits that are new.

Both are $\displaystyle \frac{0}{0}$, so substituting tells you nothing. Here they are, in whichever letter you prefer:

$$\lim_{\theta \to 0}\frac{\sin\theta}{\theta} \quad\text{or}\quad \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{\sin h}{h} \qquad\qquad \lim_{\theta \to 0}\frac{\cos\theta - 1}{\theta} \quad\text{or}\quad \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{\cos h - 1}{h}$$

Neither can be evaluated by any algebra you have. Both can be read straight off a graph:

⚠ Every line of this section assumes RADIANS

$$\lim_{\theta \to 0}\frac{\sin\theta}{\theta} = 1 \quad\text{in radians} \qquad\qquad \lim_{\theta \to 0}\frac{\sin\theta}{\theta} = \frac{\pi}{180} \quad\text{in degrees}$$

Every derivative formula in this section is only true in RADIANS.

Side note — and this is the one to carry with you. Saying $\displaystyle \lim_{\theta \to 0}\frac{\sin\theta}{\theta} = 1$ is the same as saying that for $\displaystyle \theta$ near $\displaystyle 0$ (in radians),

$$\sin\theta \;\approx\; \theta$$

Near the origin, $\displaystyle \sin\theta$ and the line $\displaystyle y=\theta$ are the same curve.

We come back to this in the last section today. Once you know $\displaystyle \sin\theta \approx \theta$ for small $\displaystyle \theta$, you can often see what a trig limit is going to be before you do any algebra on it.

Where the Sine Formula Comes From

From precalculus — you are given these. Nobody is expected to produce them from memory, and they stay on the board all period:

$$\sin(A+B) = \sin A\,\cos B + \cos A\,\sin B$$
$$\cos(A+B) = \cos A\,\cos B - \sin A\,\sin B$$
My turn

Now the definition, straight through. The only new ingredients are the two limits from the last slide.

$\displaystyle \frac{d}{dx}\left[\sin x\right]$$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{\sin(x+h)-\sin x}{h}$the definition of the derivative
$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{\sin x \cos h + \cos x \sin h - \sin x}{h}$the addition formula, with $\displaystyle A=x$ and $\displaystyle B=h$
$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{\sin x\left(\cos h - 1\right) + \cos x \sin h}{h}$group the two terms carrying $\displaystyle \sin x$
$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle \lim_{h \to 0}\left[\sin x \cdot \frac{\cos h - 1}{h} \;+\; \cos x \cdot \frac{\sin h}{h}\right]$split, and pull out what has no $\displaystyle h$ in it
$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle \sin x \cdot 0 \;+\; \cos x \cdot 1$the two limits
$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle \cos x$

⚠ And there is the radians assumption, cashed in. The second-to-last line used $\displaystyle \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{\sin h}{h} = 1$, which is only $\displaystyle 1$ because $\displaystyle h$ is in radians. So $\displaystyle \frac{d}{dx}\sin x = \cos x$ inherits that hypothesis: in degrees it is simply false. Same for every formula below it.

Cosine is the same four lines, using the other addition formula. We are not redoing them; the result is the one you have to know:

Theorem — The Two Formulas
$$\frac{d}{dx}\left[\sin x\right] = \cos x \qquad\qquad \frac{d}{dx}\left[\cos x\right] = -\sin x$$

⚠ The minus on cosine is not optional, and dropping it is the most common error in this section. A $\displaystyle 30$-second check: $\displaystyle \cos x$ is decreasing just to the right of $\displaystyle x=0$, so its derivative must be negative there. $\displaystyle -\sin x$ is; $\displaystyle +\sin x$ is not.

Differentiate It Four Times

Your turn

Let $\displaystyle f(x) = \sin x$. Compute, in order:

$$f'(x), \qquad f''(x), \qquad f'''(x), \qquad f^{(4)}(x)$$

Each is the derivative of the line above it. No new rules.

$$f'(x) = \cos x \qquad f''(x) = -\sin x \qquad f'''(x) = -\cos x \qquad f^{(4)}(x) = \sin x$$

Back where we started. The derivatives of sine cycle with period four, and so do cosine's.

So $\displaystyle f^{(100)}(x) = \sin x$, because $\displaystyle 100$ is a multiple of $\displaystyle 4$ — and you never have to compute more than four derivatives of a sine or a cosine. Divide the order by $\displaystyle 4$ and keep the remainder.

Your turn

Nothing new is needed for these — they are yesterday's rules with today's functions in the slots.

$$\text{(a)}\;\; y = x^{2}\sin x \qquad \text{(b)}\;\; y = \frac{\sin x}{e^{x}} \qquad \text{(c)}\;\; y = 3\cos x + x^{2}\sin x$$

$\displaystyle f = x^{2}$ so $\displaystyle f' = 2x$;   $\displaystyle g = \sin x$ so $\displaystyle g' = \cos x$.

$$y' = 2x\sin x + x^{2}\cos x$$
$$y' = \frac{e^{x}\cos x - \sin x \cdot e^{x}}{\left(e^{x}\right)^{2}} = \frac{e^{x}\left(\cos x - \sin x\right)}{\left(e^{x}\right)^{2}} = \frac{\cos x - \sin x}{e^{x}}$$

Factor $\displaystyle e^{x}$ out of the numerator and one copy cancels. Always look for that here — $\displaystyle e^{x}$ is never zero, so the cancellation is always legal.

$$y' = -3\sin x + 2x\sin x + x^{2}\cos x$$

The $\displaystyle -3\sin x$ is where cosine's minus sign shows up. A $\displaystyle +3\sin x$ means it was dropped.

The Magnificent Six

You know two derivatives. The other four trigonometric functions are all quotients of sine and cosine, so §3.2 already gives you everything you need:

$$\tan x = \frac{\sin x}{\cos x} \qquad \sec x = \frac{1}{\cos x} \qquad \cot x = \frac{\cos x}{\sin x} \qquad \csc x = \frac{1}{\sin x}$$
My turn

Watch $\displaystyle \tan x$ once. Quotient Rule, then the Pythagorean identity $\displaystyle \sin^{2}x + \cos^{2}x = 1$ does the simplifying.

$\displaystyle \frac{d}{dx}\tan x$$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle \frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{\sin x}{\cos x}\right]$it is a quotient
$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle \frac{\cos x \cdot \cos x - \sin x \cdot \left(-\sin x\right)}{\cos^{2}x}$lo D hi $\displaystyle -$ hi D lo, over lo squared
$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle \frac{\cos^{2}x + \sin^{2}x}{\cos^{2}x}$two minus signs make a plus
$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle \frac{1}{\cos^{2}x}$the Pythagorean identity
$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle \sec^{2}x$
Your turn

Now the other three, the same way.

  1. $\displaystyle \sec x$ — start by writing $\displaystyle \sec x = \dfrac{1}{\cos x}$, and write your final answer using $\displaystyle \sec x$ and $\displaystyle \tan x$.
  2. $\displaystyle \cot x$
  3. $\displaystyle \csc x$

$$\frac{d}{dx}\sec x = \frac{\cos x \cdot 0 - 1 \cdot \left(-\sin x\right)}{\cos^{2}x} = \frac{\sin x}{\cos^{2}x} = \frac{1}{\cos x}\cdot\frac{\sin x}{\cos x} = \sec x \tan x$$

Splitting $\displaystyle \frac{\sin x}{\cos^{2}x}$ into $\displaystyle \frac{1}{\cos x}\cdot\frac{\sin x}{\cos x}$ is the step that gets it into the requested form.

$$\frac{d}{dx}\cot x = \frac{\sin x\left(-\sin x\right) - \cos x \cos x}{\sin^{2}x} = \frac{-\left(\sin^{2}x+\cos^{2}x\right)}{\sin^{2}x} = -\csc^{2}x$$
$$\frac{d}{dx}\csc x = \frac{\sin x \cdot 0 - 1 \cdot \cos x}{\sin^{2}x} = -\frac{\cos x}{\sin^{2}x} = -\csc x \cot x$$

Every one of the four whose name starts with “c” carries a minus sign — $\displaystyle \cos$, $\displaystyle \cot$, $\displaystyle \csc$. That is not a coincidence worth explaining, but it is a check worth running.

Now You: All Six, and Three Factors

Your turn

Differentiate.

$$\text{(a)}\;\; y = 4\sec x - 3\csc x \qquad \text{(b)}\;\; g(t) = e^{t}\left(3\tan t - 2t\right) \qquad \text{(c)}\;\; y = x\,e^{x}\sin x$$
$$y' = 4\sec x \tan x + 3\csc x \cot x$$

The second term is $\displaystyle -3 \cdot \left(-\csc x \cot x\right)$, so it comes out positive. Two minus signs, one answer.

$$g'(t) = e^{t}\left(3\tan t - 2t\right) + e^{t}\left(3\sec^{2}t - 2\right) = e^{t}\left(3\tan t - 2t + 3\sec^{2}t - 2\right)$$

Group it as $\displaystyle \left(x e^{x}\right)\cdot \sin x$ and apply the Product Rule to the group:

$$y' = \left(e^{x} + x e^{x}\right)\sin x + x e^{x}\cos x = e^{x}\left(\sin x + x\sin x + x\cos x\right)$$

A product of three factors differentiates one factor at a time: the answer has three terms, each with exactly one factor primed. Think of the rectangle from §3.2 grown into a box — three faces slide out, so three terms.

Two That Look Like Something Else

Your turn

(a) Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{h \to 0}\frac{\tan\left(\frac{\pi}{4}+h\right)-\tan\frac{\pi}{4}}{h}$. You should not need to compute a limit.

That is the definition of the derivative of $\displaystyle \tan x$ at $\displaystyle x=\frac{\pi}{4}$.

$$\frac{d}{dx}\tan x = \sec^{2}x \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \sec^{2}\frac{\pi}{4} = 2$$

using $\displaystyle \cos\frac{\pi}{4} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$, so $\displaystyle \sec\frac{\pi}{4} = \frac{1}{\cos(\pi/4)} = \sqrt{2}$ and $\displaystyle \left(\sqrt{2}\right)^{2} = 2$.

Your turn

(b) Let $\displaystyle g(x) = \tan x + 2\sin x$. Find the tangent line to $\displaystyle y=g(x)$ at $\displaystyle x=0$. Then: is there a tangent line at $\displaystyle x=\frac{\pi}{2}$?

$\displaystyle g'(x) = \sec^{2}x + 2\cos x$, so $\displaystyle g(0) = 0$ and $\displaystyle g'(0) = 1 + 2 = 3$:

$$y = 3x$$

At $\displaystyle x=\frac{\pi}{2}$ there is no tangent line, because there is no point — $\displaystyle \tan x$ has a vertical asymptote there and $\displaystyle g\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right)$ does not exist. A derivative formula you can write down does not promise the function is there.

$\displaystyle \sec^{2}\frac{\pi}{2}$ is undefined, so the formula warns you too — but only if you check it rather than trusting it. Domain first, derivative second.

Back to the Two Limits

Those two limits from Thursday are not only scaffolding for the derivation — they are the only tool you have for a trig limit that comes out $\displaystyle \frac{0}{0}$. The whole move is to make the inside of the sine match what is underneath it.

My turn

One first. $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0}\frac{\sin 3x}{x}$.

The rule needs $\displaystyle \sin(\text{something})$ over that same something. Here the inside is $\displaystyle 3x$ but the bottom is $\displaystyle x$, so manufacture the $\displaystyle 3x$:

$$\lim_{x \to 0}\frac{\sin 3x}{x} = \lim_{x \to 0} 3\cdot\frac{\sin 3x}{3x} = 3 \cdot 1 = 3$$

As $\displaystyle x \to 0$ so does $\displaystyle 3x$, which is why $\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 0}\frac{\sin 3x}{3x} = 1$.

Your turn

Now these.

$$\text{(a)}\;\; \lim_{x \to 0}\frac{\sin 7x}{4x} \qquad\qquad \text{(b)}\;\; \lim_{x \to 0} x\cot x$$
$$\lim_{x \to 0}\frac{\sin 7x}{4x} = \lim_{x \to 0}\frac{7}{4}\cdot\frac{\sin 7x}{7x} = \frac{7}{4}\cdot 1 = \frac{7}{4}$$
$$\lim_{x \to 0} x\cot x = \lim_{x \to 0} x\cdot\frac{\cos x}{\sin x} = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{x}{\sin x}\cdot\cos x = \frac{1}{1}\cdot 1 = 1$$

$\displaystyle \frac{x}{\sin x}$ is the reciprocal of $\displaystyle \frac{\sin x}{x}$, so it goes to $\displaystyle \frac{1}{1}$.

Two moves, and that is the whole topic. Rewrite every $\displaystyle \tan$, $\displaystyle \cot$, $\displaystyle \sec$ and $\displaystyle \csc$ in terms of $\displaystyle \sin$ and $\displaystyle \cos$; then force the inside of each sine to match its denominator, paying for it with a constant out front.

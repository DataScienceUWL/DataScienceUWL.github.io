4.2  The Mean Value Theorem

MTH 207 Calculus I — Applications of Differentiation

You Were Going Exactly That Fast at Some Instant

Your turn

A car travels $\displaystyle 100$ miles in exactly $\displaystyle 2$ hours. Must its speedometer have read exactly $\displaystyle 50$ mph at some instant? Yes or no, and say why.

Show: yes — and the reason is worth saying carefully

The average speed is

$$\frac{100 \text{ miles}}{2 \text{ hours}} = 50 \text{ mph}.$$

If the speedometer had read under $\displaystyle 50$ the whole time, the car could not have covered $\displaystyle 100$ miles. If it had read over $\displaystyle 50$ the whole time, it would have gone further. So it was below at some moment and above at another — and speed changes continuously, so somewhere in between it was exactly $\displaystyle 50$.

That is the Mean Value Theorem, in English. The average rate of change over an interval is achieved exactly, as an instantaneous rate, at least once inside it.

Rolle's Theorem: the Flat Case

Start with the special case where the car ends where it started.

Theorem — Rolle's Theorem

Suppose

  1. $\displaystyle f$ is continuous on the closed interval $\displaystyle [a,b]$,
  2. $\displaystyle f$ is differentiable on the open interval $\displaystyle (a,b)$, and
  3. $\displaystyle f(a) = f(b)$.
Then there is a number $\displaystyle c$ in $\displaystyle (a,b)$ with $\displaystyle \;f'(c) = 0$.

Show: why it is true, using what we proved yesterday

$\displaystyle f$ is continuous on a closed interval, so by the Extreme Value Theorem it attains an absolute max and an absolute min. If both are at the endpoints, then since $\displaystyle f(a)=f(b)$ the max and the min are equal — so $\displaystyle f$ is constant, and $\displaystyle f'(c)=0$ everywhere inside. Otherwise one of them is at an interior point $\displaystyle c$. That is a local extremum, $\displaystyle f$ is differentiable there, so Fermat gives $\displaystyle f'(c)=0$.

Rolle is EVT and Fermat put together — which is why §4.1 came first. Nothing new was needed.

Your turn

If $\displaystyle f$ is differentiable everywhere and $\displaystyle f'(x)$ is never zero, can $\displaystyle f(0)$ and $\displaystyle f(1)$ be equal?

Show: no — this is Rolle read backwards

If $\displaystyle f(0) = f(1)$, Rolle would hand us a $\displaystyle c$ in $\displaystyle (0,1)$ with $\displaystyle f'(c)=0$. There is no such $\displaystyle c$. So $\displaystyle f(0) \neq f(1)$.

The Mean Value Theorem

Now tilt the picture. Everything is the same except the two ends are at different heights.

Theorem — The Mean Value Theorem

Suppose

  1. $\displaystyle f$ is continuous on the closed interval $\displaystyle [a,b]$, and
  2. $\displaystyle f$ is differentiable on the open interval $\displaystyle (a,b)$.
Then there is a number $\displaystyle c$ in $\displaystyle (a,b)$ with

$$f'(c) = \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}.$$

The right-hand side is the slope of the secant through the two endpoints — the average rate of change. The theorem says some tangent is parallel to it. Rolle is the case where that secant happens to be horizontal.

Check the Hypotheses, Then Find $c$

My turn

Verify that $\displaystyle f(x) = x^{2} - 2x$ satisfies the hypotheses of the MVT on $\displaystyle [0,3]$, and find all numbers $\displaystyle c$ the theorem guarantees. Watch the shape of this one. You get the next.

Show: hypothesis 1 — continuity, said out loud

$\displaystyle f$ is a polynomial. Polynomials are continuous everywhere, so $\displaystyle f$ is continuous on $\displaystyle [0,3]$. ✓

Show: hypothesis 2 — differentiability, said out loud

Polynomials are differentiable everywhere, so $\displaystyle f$ is differentiable on $\displaystyle (0,3)$. ✓ Both hypotheses hold, so the MVT applies and such a $\displaystyle c$ exists.

Show: now compute the secant slope
$$f(3) = 9 - 6 = 3, \qquad f(0) = 0 - 0 = 0$$
$$\frac{f(3)-f(0)}{3-0} = \frac{3 - 0}{3} = 1$$
Show: set $\displaystyle f'(c)$ equal to it and solve
$$f'(x) = 2x - 2$$
$$2c - 2 = 1$$
$$2c = 3$$
$$c = \tfrac{3}{2}$$
Show: last step: is it actually in the interval?

$\displaystyle \tfrac{3}{2}$ is in $\displaystyle (0,3)$. ✓   So $\displaystyle c = \tfrac{3}{2}$, and it is the only one.

Now You

Your turn

Verify the hypotheses of the MVT for $\displaystyle f(x) = x^{3} - 3x$ on $\displaystyle \left[-1, 2\right]$, and find all $\displaystyle c$. Write the hypothesis check as a sentence, not as a tick.

Show: the hypotheses

$\displaystyle f$ is a polynomial, so it is continuous on $\displaystyle [-1,2]$ and differentiable on $\displaystyle (-1,2)$. Both hypotheses hold.

Show: the secant slope
$$f(2) = 8 - 6 = 2, \qquad f(-1) = -1 + 3 = 2$$
$$\frac{f(2)-f(-1)}{2-\left(-1\right)} = \frac{2 - 2}{3} = 0$$

⚠ The endpoints have equal values, so this is really Rolle — the secant is horizontal.

Show: solve $\displaystyle f'(c) = 0$
$$f'(x) = 3x^{2} - 3$$
$$3c^{2} - 3 = 0$$
$$c^{2} = 1$$
$$c = -1 \quad\text{and}\quad c = 1$$
Show: which ones are allowed?

The theorem promises $\displaystyle c$ strictly inside $\displaystyle (-1,2)$. So $\displaystyle c=-1$ is an endpoint and does not count:

$$c = 1 \quad\text{only.}$$

Discarding $\displaystyle c=-1$ is the step that separates a correct answer from a nearly correct one. The MVT's $\displaystyle c$ lives in the open interval, always.

What the MVT Proves

The MVT is mostly not used to find $\displaystyle c$. It is used to prove things that have nothing to do with $\displaystyle c$ at all.

Your turn

If $\displaystyle f'(x) = 0$ for every $\displaystyle x$ in an interval, must $\displaystyle f$ be constant there?

Show: yes, and the MVT is the only honest proof

Take any two points $\displaystyle x_{1} < x_{2}$ in the interval. The MVT applies, so there is a $\displaystyle c$ between them with

$$f'(c) = \frac{f(x_{2}) - f(x_{1})}{x_{2} - x_{1}}.$$

But $\displaystyle f'(c) = 0$, so the fraction is $\displaystyle 0$, so $\displaystyle f(x_{2}) = f(x_{1})$. Any two values of $\displaystyle f$ are equal — $\displaystyle f$ is constant.

The corollary that Chapter 5 is built on. If $\displaystyle f'(x) = g'(x)$ on an interval, apply the above to $\displaystyle f - g$: its derivative is $\displaystyle 0$, so $\displaystyle f - g$ is constant, and

$$f(x) = g(x) + C.$$

Not $\displaystyle f = g$ — the constant need not be zero. That $\displaystyle C$ is exactly the $\displaystyle +\,C$ you will write on every antiderivative from §4.9 onward, and this is the theorem that earns it.

Your turn

Is there a function with $\displaystyle f(1) = -2$, $\displaystyle f(3) = 0$, and $\displaystyle f'(x) > 1$ for every $\displaystyle x$?

Show: no — and the MVT is what rules it out

If such an $\displaystyle f$ existed, the MVT on $\displaystyle [1,3]$ would give a $\displaystyle c$ with

$$f'(c) = \frac{f(3)-f(1)}{3-1} = \frac{0 - \left(-2\right)}{2} = 1.$$

That contradicts $\displaystyle f'(x) > 1$ for every $\displaystyle x$. So no such function exists.

What You Should Be Able to Do

  • State the MVT, including both hypotheses and which interval each one is about — closed for continuity, open for differentiability.
  • Verify the hypotheses in writing before computing anything. “$\displaystyle f$ is a polynomial, so it is continuous on $\displaystyle [a,b]$ and differentiable on $\displaystyle (a,b)$” earns marks.
  • Find every $\displaystyle c$ by solving $\displaystyle f'(c) = \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}$, then discard any root outside the open interval.
  • Recognise Rolle as the case $\displaystyle f(a)=f(b)$, and use it backwards to show a function cannot repeat a value.
  • Explain why $\displaystyle f' = g'$ on an interval forces $\displaystyle f = g + C$.

Up to now a theorem answered a question you already had. The MVT answers questions you would not have thought to ask — that is what makes it feel harder than its arithmetic.

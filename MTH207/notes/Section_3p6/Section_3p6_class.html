3.6  Logarithmic and Inverse Trig Derivatives

MTH 207 Calculus I — Differentiation Rules

Three More for the Catalog

This section adds the last three kinds of function to your catalog of derivatives:

  • exponentials of any base — $\displaystyle 2^{x}$, $\displaystyle 5^{x}$, not just $\displaystyle e^{x}$
  • logarithms — $\displaystyle \ln x$ and $\displaystyle \log_a x$
  • inverse trig functions — $\displaystyle \arcsin$, $\displaystyle \arctan$ and the rest

After today every standard function you know has a derivative rule.

Your turn

Differentiate each, or get as far as you can.

$$\text{(a)}\;\; y = e^{3x} \qquad\qquad \text{(b)}\;\; y = 2^{x}$$
Show: (a) you already own this

Outside $\displaystyle e^{(\;)}$, inside $\displaystyle 3x$. The Chain Rule:

$$y' = e^{3x}\cdot 3 = 3e^{3x}$$
Show: (b) looks the same and is not

In $\displaystyle e^{3x}$ the variable is in the exponent but the base is $\displaystyle e$, which is its own derivative. In $\displaystyle 2^{x}$ the base is $\displaystyle 2$. The Power Rule cannot touch it — that rule is for $\displaystyle x^{n}$, a variable base and constant exponent. This is the other way round.

$\displaystyle x^{2}$ and $\displaystyle 2^{x}$ are not the same kind of object and do not get the same rule. Say which is which before you differentiate either.

Where $\frac1x$ Comes From

$\displaystyle \ln x$ is the inverse of $\displaystyle e^{x}$, and you have no rule for inverses. But you have one for equations you cannot solve — yesterday.

My turn

Derive $\displaystyle \frac{d}{dx}\ln x$ using implicit differentiation.

rewrite, differentiate both sides, substitute back

$\displaystyle y = \ln x$the thing we want the derivative of
$\displaystyle e^{y} = x$rewrite without the logarithm
$\displaystyle \frac{d}{dx}\left[\,e^{y}\,\right] = \frac{d}{dx}\left[\,x\,\right]$apply $\displaystyle \frac{d}{dx}$ to both sides — §3.5
$\displaystyle e^{y}\,\frac{dy}{dx} = 1$$\displaystyle y$ is a function of $\displaystyle x$, so $\displaystyle e^{y}$ carries a $\displaystyle \frac{dy}{dx}$
$\displaystyle \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{1}{e^{y}}$solve
$\displaystyle \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{1}{x}$and $\displaystyle e^{y}$ is $\displaystyle x$ — line two
Theorem — Derivative of the Natural Logarithm
$$\frac{d}{dx}\,\ln x \;=\; \frac1x \qquad (x>0)$$

Yesterday's method, one day later, on something you will use for the rest of the course. Implicit differentiation was not a detour.

One Rewrite, Both Rules

You now have $\displaystyle e^{x}$ and $\displaystyle \ln x$. Every other base is one rewrite away from them — so there is nothing new to learn, only something to notice.

Any base, written naturally. Since $\displaystyle a = e^{\ln a}$ and $\displaystyle \log_a x$ is just $\displaystyle \ln x$ rescaled,

$$a^{x} = e^{\,x\ln a} \qquad\qquad \log_a x = \frac{\ln x}{\ln a}$$

and in both, $\displaystyle \ln a$ is a number.

My turn

Differentiate each by rewriting. No new rule.

the exponential

$\displaystyle y = a^{x} = e^{\,x\ln a}$rewrite with base $\displaystyle e$
$\displaystyle y' = e^{\,x\ln a}\cdot\frac{d}{dx}\left(x\ln a\right) = e^{\,x\ln a}\cdot\ln a$Chain Rule; the inside is a number times $\displaystyle x$
$\displaystyle y' = a^{x}\ln a$and $\displaystyle e^{\,x\ln a}$ is where we started

the logarithm

$\displaystyle y = \log_a x = \frac{1}{\ln a}\cdot\ln x$change of base — $\displaystyle \frac{1}{\ln a}$ is a number out front
$\displaystyle y' = \frac{1}{\ln a}\cdot\frac{1}{x} = \frac{1}{x\ln a}$constant multiple; no Quotient Rule
Theorem — Two consequences, not two new rules
$$\frac{d}{dx}\,a^{x} = a^{x}\ln a \qquad\qquad \frac{d}{dx}\,\log_a x = \frac{1}{x\ln a}$$

Both carry $\displaystyle \ln a$ — exponentials multiply by it, logarithms divide by it. Base $\displaystyle e$ makes $\displaystyle \ln a = 1$ and both collapse to what you already knew, which is why $\displaystyle e$ is the convenient base.

Keep the shortcut, keep the rewrite. After about five of these you will stop rewriting and just use the rules — that is fine and expected. What matters is that you can rebuild them in two lines, because the final asks you to do exactly that for $\displaystyle a^{x}$, and because the next frame is the case where neither rule applies.

Expand Before You Differentiate

The Chain and Quotient Rules will grind through a messy logarithm. The log laws make most of that work disappear before you start.

Inside a logarithm, products become sums, quotients become differences, and exponents come out front:

$$\ln(AB) = \ln A + \ln B \qquad \ln\!\frac{A}{B} = \ln A - \ln B \qquad \ln\!\left(A^{n}\right) = n\ln A$$
My turn

Watch how much work that saves.

expand first, then differentiate term by term

$\displaystyle y = \ln\!\left(\frac{\left(2x+3\right)^{5}}{\sqrt{x^{4}+1}}\right)$as given — a quotient, a power and a root, all inside one log
$\displaystyle y = \ln\!\left(\frac{\left(2x+3\right)^{5}}{\left(x^{4}+1\right)^{1/2}}\right)$before any law: a square root is the power $\displaystyle \tfrac12$
$\displaystyle y = \ln\!\left(\left(2x+3\right)^{5}\right) - \ln\!\left(\left(x^{4}+1\right)^{1/2}\right)$$\displaystyle \ln\!\frac{A}{B} = \ln A - \ln B$, with $\displaystyle A = \left(2x+3\right)^{5}$ and $\displaystyle B = \left(x^{4}+1\right)^{1/2}$
$\displaystyle y = 5\ln\left(2x+3\right) - \tfrac12\ln\left(x^{4}+1\right)$$\displaystyle \ln\!\left(A^{n}\right) = n\ln A$ on each term — the $\displaystyle 5$ and the $\displaystyle \tfrac12$ come out front
$\displaystyle y' = 5\cdot\frac{2}{2x+3} - \tfrac12\cdot\frac{4x^{3}}{x^{4}+1}$now differentiate: two easy Chain Rules, no Quotient Rule anywhere
$\displaystyle y' = \frac{10}{2x+3} - \frac{2x^{3}}{x^{4}+1}$tidy

Expanding is not optional tidying — it is the method. Differentiating that quotient directly is a Quotient Rule with a Chain Rule in each piece, for the same answer and five times the algebra.

Your turn

Differentiate $\displaystyle y = \ln\!\left(\ln x\right)$.

Show: no law applies — so it is just the Chain Rule

Outside $\displaystyle \ln(\;)$, inside $\displaystyle \ln x$:

$$y' = \frac{1}{\ln x}\cdot\frac{1}{x} = \frac{1}{x\ln x}$$

The laws expand a log of a product, quotient or power. A log of a log is none of those — there is nothing to expand, so it is an ordinary composite.

Take the Log of Both Sides

If the logs make a messy function easy, put one there on purpose.

Logarithmic differentiation. Take $\displaystyle \ln$ of both sides, expand with the laws, differentiate implicitly, then solve for $\displaystyle y'$ and put $\displaystyle y$ back.

My turn

A product of three awkward factors.

log both sides, expand one law at a time, differentiate, solve

$\displaystyle y = \frac{x^{3}\sqrt{x^{2}+1}}{\left(3x+2\right)^{5}}$Quotient, Product and Chain all at once — or
$\displaystyle \ln y = \ln\!\left(\frac{x^{3}\sqrt{x^{2}+1}}{\left(3x+2\right)^{5}}\right)$take $\displaystyle \ln$ of both sides — nothing is expanded yet
$\displaystyle \ln y = \ln\!\left(x^{3}\sqrt{x^{2}+1}\right) - \ln\!\left(\left(3x+2\right)^{5}\right)$$\displaystyle \ln\!\frac{A}{B} = \ln A - \ln B$: top over bottom becomes a difference
$\displaystyle \ln y = \ln\!\left(x^{3}\right) + \ln\!\left(\sqrt{x^{2}+1}\right) - \ln\!\left(\left(3x+2\right)^{5}\right)$$\displaystyle \ln(AB) = \ln A + \ln B$ on the top, which was a product
$\displaystyle \ln y = \ln\!\left(x^{3}\right) + \ln\!\left(\left(x^{2}+1\right)^{1/2}\right) - \ln\!\left(\left(3x+2\right)^{5}\right)$the square root is the power $\displaystyle \tfrac12$ — rewrite it before using any law on it
$\displaystyle \ln y = 3\ln x + \tfrac12\ln\left(x^{2}+1\right) - 5\ln\left(3x+2\right)$$\displaystyle \ln\!\left(A^{n}\right) = n\ln A$ on all three terms — every exponent comes out front
$\displaystyle \frac{1}{y}\,\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{3}{x} + \frac{x}{x^{2}+1} - \frac{15}{3x+2}$differentiate both sides — the left needs the Chain Rule, and that is where $\displaystyle \frac1y$ comes from
$\displaystyle \frac{dy}{dx} = y\left(\frac{3}{x} + \frac{x}{x^{2}+1} - \frac{15}{3x+2}\right)$multiply both sides by $\displaystyle y$
$\displaystyle \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{x^{3}\sqrt{x^{2}+1}}{\left(3x+2\right)^{5}}\left(\frac{3}{x} + \frac{x}{x^{2}+1} - \frac{15}{3x+2}\right)$put the original $\displaystyle y$ back — the answer may not contain $\displaystyle y$
Your turn

Now one the old rules cannot touch: $\displaystyle y = x^{x}$, for $\displaystyle x>0$.

Show: neither a power nor an exponential — both move

$\displaystyle x^{n}$ has a constant exponent and $\displaystyle a^{x}$ a constant base. Here neither is constant, so no rule you own applies. Take logs:

$$\ln y = \ln\left(x^{x}\right) = x\ln x$$

Now differentiate both sides. The right is a product, so it needs the Product Rule:

$$\frac{1}{y}\,\frac{dy}{dx} = \underbrace{\left(1\right)\ln x}_{\text{first}'\;\cdot\;\text{second}} + \underbrace{x\cdot\frac{1}{x}}_{\text{first}\;\cdot\;\text{second}'} = \ln x + 1$$
$$\frac{dy}{dx} = y\left(\ln x + 1\right) = x^{x}\left(\ln x + 1\right)$$

Inverse Trig Functions

A reminder, not a re-teach. Each one takes a number and hands back an angle.

$\displaystyle \theta = \arcsin t$

means $\displaystyle t = \sin\theta$, with $\displaystyle -\frac{\pi}{2}\le\theta\le\frac{\pi}{2}$

$\displaystyle \theta = \arccos t$

means $\displaystyle t = \cos\theta$, with $\displaystyle 0\le\theta\le\pi$

$\displaystyle \theta = \arctan t$

means $\displaystyle t = \tan\theta$, with $\displaystyle -\frac{\pi}{2} \lt \theta \lt \frac{\pi}{2}$

$\displaystyle \arctan t$ is the angle whose tangent is $\displaystyle t$, and the other two read the same way. The range says which such angle, because there are infinitely many — and each one answers from half the circle.

Reading it backwards is what we need next: $\displaystyle \theta = \arctan t$ means $\displaystyle \tan\theta = t$. If any of this is shaky, the Trig Review page in Canvas has the whole thing with practice.

The Derivative of $\arctan$

This is how the inverse trig derivatives are found. Undo the inverse first.

My turn

Derive $\displaystyle \frac{d}{dx}\arctan x$.

rewrite, differentiate, convert

$\displaystyle y = \arctan x$the thing we want
$\displaystyle \tan y = x$rewrite — no inverse left in it
$\displaystyle \sec^{2}y\;\frac{dy}{dx} = 1$differentiate both sides
$\displaystyle \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{1}{\sec^{2}y}$solve — but this is still in $\displaystyle y$, so it is not an answer

Draw the triangle. $\displaystyle \tan y = x$ means $\displaystyle \frac{\text{opp}}{\text{adj}} = \frac{x}{1}$ — so label the opposite side $\displaystyle x$ and the adjacent side $\displaystyle 1$, and Pythagoras fills in the hypotenuse. Now read whatever you need straight off it. No identity to remember.

and now finish it

$\displaystyle \sec y = \frac{\text{hyp}}{\text{adj}} = \frac{\sqrt{1+x^{2}}}{1}$read off the left-hand triangle
$\displaystyle \sec^{2}y = 1+x^{2}$square it
$\displaystyle \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{1}{1+x^{2}}$substitute — and there is no $\displaystyle y$ left
Theorem — Derivative of the arctangent
$$\frac{d}{dx}\arctan x = \frac{1}{1+x^{2}}$$
Your turn

Now derive $\displaystyle \frac{d}{dx}\arcsin x$ the same way. Draw your own triangle for $\displaystyle \sin y = x$ first — which side is $\displaystyle x$, and which side is $\displaystyle 1$?

Show: the triangle you should have drawn

$\displaystyle \sin y = \frac{\text{opp}}{\text{hyp}} = \frac{x}{1}$ — so this time $\displaystyle x$ is the opposite side and $\displaystyle 1$ is the hypotenuse, and Pythagoras fills in the adjacent side.

rewrite, differentiate, read it off the triangle

$\displaystyle y = \arcsin x$the thing we want
$\displaystyle \sin y = x = \frac{x}{1}$rewrite — opposite $\displaystyle x$, hypotenuse $\displaystyle 1$
$\displaystyle \cos y\;\frac{dy}{dx} = 1$differentiate both sides — the left carries a $\displaystyle \frac{dy}{dx}$
$\displaystyle \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{1}{\cos y}$solve — but this is still in $\displaystyle y$, so it is not an answer yet
$\displaystyle \cos y = \frac{\text{adj}}{\text{hyp}} = \frac{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}{1}$Pythagoras gave the adjacent side — read it off
$\displaystyle \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}$substitute

Rewrite, differentiate, convert. The last one gets skipped — an answer still in $\displaystyle y$ is not finished, and the triangle is the fastest way to finish it.

You have now derived two of the six. Here is the whole set — look at how it is arranged before you reach for a pen.

$$\begin{array}{ll|l} \frac{d}{dx}\arcsin x = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}} & \frac{d}{dx}\arctan x = \dfrac{1}{1+x^{2}} & \text{memorize these two} \\[16pt] \frac{d}{dx}\arccos x = -\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}} & \frac{d}{dx}\mathrm{arccot}\,x = -\dfrac{1}{1+x^{2}} & \text{the one above, with a minus} \\[16pt] \frac{d}{dx}\mathrm{arcsec}\,x = \dfrac{1}{|x|\sqrt{x^{2}-1}} & \frac{d}{dx}\mathrm{arccsc}\,x = -\dfrac{1}{|x|\sqrt{x^{2}-1}} & \text{reference only} \end{array}$$

Memorize two. $\displaystyle \arcsin$ and $\displaystyle \arctan$ — the top row. That is the entire memorization load for this section.

The middle row is free, and here is why. The “co” functions are complementary:

$$\arcsin x + \arccos x = \frac{\pi}{2} \qquad\qquad \arctan x + \mathrm{arccot}\, x = \frac{\pi}{2}$$

Differentiate both sides of either one. The right side is a constant, so its derivative is $\displaystyle 0$:

$$\frac{d}{dx}\arcsin x + \frac{d}{dx}\arccos x = 0 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{d}{dx}\arccos x = -\frac{d}{dx}\arcsin x$$

That is the whole derivation. You do not learn the “co” derivatives — you flip the sign of the one above it.

The bottom row you do not learn at all. $\displaystyle \mathrm{arcsec}$ and $\displaystyle \mathrm{arccsc}$ are in Stewart and on Look It Up; this course never asks for them. The triangle still builds them if you ever need one.

Your turn

Now use them. Differentiate.

$$\text{(a)}\;\; y = \arcsin\!\left(x^{3}\right) \qquad\qquad \text{(b)}\;\; y = x\arctan x$$

(a) a chain — outside first, then the inside

$\displaystyle y = \arcsin\!\left(x^{3}\right)$outside is $\displaystyle \arcsin$, inside is $\displaystyle x^{3}$
$\displaystyle y' = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\left(x^{3}\right)^{2}}}\cdot\frac{d}{dx}\left[\,x^{3}\,\right]$the $\displaystyle \arcsin$ rule evaluated at the inside, times the inside's derivative
$\displaystyle y' = \frac{3x^{2}}{\sqrt{1-x^{6}}}$tidy — $\displaystyle \left(x^{3}\right)^{2} = x^{6}$

(b) a product, with $\displaystyle \arctan$ as one factor

$\displaystyle y = x\arctan x$$\displaystyle f = x$, $\displaystyle g = \arctan x$
$\displaystyle y' = \frac{d}{dx}\left[\,x\,\right]\arctan x + x\,\frac{d}{dx}\left[\,\arctan x\,\right]$Product Rule written out first
$\displaystyle y' = \arctan x + \frac{x}{1+x^{2}}$and $\displaystyle \frac{d}{dx}\arctan x = \frac{1}{1+x^{2}}$

Nothing new — that is §3.2 and §3.4. The only thing inverse trig adds is which formula goes on the outside. Notice (b) does not simplify: $\displaystyle \arctan x$ and a rational function do not combine.

What You Should Be Able to Do

  • Say whether something is $\displaystyle x^{n}$ or $\displaystyle a^{x}$ — which letter is moving — before choosing a rule.
  • Differentiate $\displaystyle a^{x}$ and $\displaystyle \log_a x$, keeping the $\displaystyle \ln a$: exponentials multiply by it, logarithms divide by it.
  • Derive $\displaystyle \frac{d}{dx}a^{x}$ by rewriting $\displaystyle a^{x}=e^{\,x\ln a}$, and $\displaystyle \frac{d}{dx}\ln x$ by differentiating $\displaystyle e^{y}=x$ implicitly.
  • Expand first: inside a log, products, quotients and exponents all come apart before you differentiate.
  • Use logarithmic differentiation on an ugly product or quotient, and on a variable power like $\displaystyle x^{x}$.
  • Say what $\displaystyle \arcsin$, $\displaystyle \arccos$ and $\displaystyle \arctan$ return — an angle, from half the circle.
  • Derive $\displaystyle \frac{d}{dx}\arctan x$ and $\displaystyle \frac{d}{dx}\arcsin x$ by rewriting, differentiating and reading the triangle.
  • Differentiate $\displaystyle \arcsin$ and $\displaystyle \arctan$ inside a chain or a product — memorizing only those two.

Every elementary function now has a derivative rule. §3.9 stops asking for new rules and starts asking what they are for.

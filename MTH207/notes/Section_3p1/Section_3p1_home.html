That guess is correct, it is called the Power Rule, and the rest of this section is (a) checking that it is actually true and (b) finding out exactly where it stops working.

Where the Rule Comes From

A guessed pattern is not a theorem. Check one case honestly.

My turn

$\displaystyle 3x^{2}$, exactly as guessed. The same expansion works for any whole number $\displaystyle n$: everything but one term carries a spare $\displaystyle h$ and dies, and the survivor is $\displaystyle nx^{n-1}$.

Five Rules, and Then Almost Everything

Theorem — The Power Rule
$$\frac{d}{dx}\left(x^{n}\right) \;=\; nx^{n-1}$$

That holds for every real number $\displaystyle n$ — not just whole numbers. $\displaystyle n$ may be negative, or a fraction, or $\displaystyle \pi$.

Two cases are worth writing down on their own, because you will use them constantly and neither looks like a power at first glance:

The rules so far — one more arrives by the end of today
rulewhat it says

power$\displaystyle \frac{d}{dx}\left(x^{n}\right) = nx^{n-1}$
constant multiple$\displaystyle \frac{d}{dx}\left[c\,f(x)\right] = c\,f'(x)$
sum and difference$\displaystyle \frac{d}{dx}\left[f(x) \pm g(x)\right] = f'(x) \pm g'(x)$

The first two are the Power Rule in disguise: $\displaystyle c = cx^{0}$ gives $\displaystyle 0$, and $\displaystyle x = x^{1}$ gives $\displaystyle 1x^{0} = 1$. Write them down anyway — a horizontal line has slope $\displaystyle 0$ and the line $\displaystyle y=x$ has slope $\displaystyle 1$, and you should not have to derive either one mid-problem.

Between them these handle every polynomial, every root and every $\displaystyle 1/x^{n}$ — provided you first rewrite it as a power. $\displaystyle \sqrt{x}$ is $\displaystyle x^{1/2}$; $\displaystyle \frac{1}{x^{3}}$ is $\displaystyle x^{-3}$. The rewriting is the work.

Now You

Your turn

Differentiate each. Two of them are traps.

  1. $\displaystyle f(x) = 3x^{4} - 2x + 7$
  2. $\displaystyle g(x) = \sqrt{x}$
  3. $\displaystyle h(x) = \frac{1}{x^{3}}$
  4. $\displaystyle p(x) = \pi^{2}$
  5. $\displaystyle q(x) = x^{e}$

1. $\displaystyle f'(x) = 12x^{3} - 2$. The $\displaystyle 7$ contributes nothing.

2. Rewrite first: $\displaystyle g(x) = x^{1/2}$, so $\displaystyle g'(x) = \frac12 x^{-1/2} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}$.

3. Rewrite first: $\displaystyle h(x) = x^{-3}$, so $\displaystyle h'(x) = -3x^{-4} = -\frac{3}{x^{4}}$.

4. $\displaystyle p'(x) = 0$. $\displaystyle \pi^{2}$ is a number — roughly $\displaystyle 9.87$ — and there is no $\displaystyle x$ in it. The trap is that $\displaystyle \pi$ looks like a variable.

5. $\displaystyle q'(x) = e\,x^{e-1}$. Here $\displaystyle e$ is the exponent and it is a constant, so the Power Rule applies exactly as written. The trap is that $\displaystyle e$ looks like it must involve $\displaystyle e^{x}$.

Before differentiating, ask one question: is the variable in the base or in the exponent? Base means Power Rule. Exponent means something else entirely — the next frame.

Two Rules That Do Not Exist

Your turn

Both of these are false. Find a single example that kills each.

$$\text{(a)}\quad \frac{d}{dx}\left[f(x)g(x)\right] = f'(x)\,g'(x) \qquad\qquad \text{(b)}\quad \frac{d}{dx}\left(e^{x}\right) = x\,e^{x-1}$$

Take $\displaystyle f(x) = x^{2}$ and $\displaystyle g(x) = x^{3}$.

The claim says the answer is $\displaystyle 2x \cdot 3x^{2} = 6x^{3}$.

But $\displaystyle f(x)g(x) = x^{5}$, whose derivative we know is $\displaystyle 5x^{4}$.

$\displaystyle 6x^{3} \neq 5x^{4}$, so the claim is false. The real rule for a product needs something more careful — that is 3.2.

The Power Rule is about $\displaystyle x^{n}$: variable in the base, constant in the exponent. In $\displaystyle e^{x}$ it is the other way round — the base $\displaystyle e$ is a constant and the variable is upstairs. The rule simply does not reach it.

The truth is remarkable:

$$\frac{d}{dx}\left(e^{x}\right) \;=\; e^{x}$$

A function that is its own derivative. The next two frames earn it, starting from a general base $\displaystyle a^{x}$.

Neither of these is a slip in the algebra. Both are a correct rule used outside the hypothesis it came with — the same failure as the Quotient Law question on Quiz 2, where the law was quoted without checking the denominator.

The One the Power Rule Cannot Reach

Not one of the five rules touches $\displaystyle 2^{x}$. The variable is in the exponent, so there is no $\displaystyle n$ to bring down and nothing to subtract from. Back to the definition then — and for every base at once: $\displaystyle f(x) = a^{x}$, with $\displaystyle a > 0$.

Your turn

No limits yet. Just algebra. Take the difference quotient

$$\frac{a^{x+h} - a^{x}}{h}$$

and factor $\displaystyle a^{x}$ out of the numerator. Which exponent law do you need?

$\displaystyle a^{x+h} = a^{x}a^{h}$ — adding exponents means multiplying. The numerator is then $\displaystyle a^{x}a^{h} - a^{x} = a^{x}\left(a^{h}-1\right)$, so the whole quotient is $\displaystyle \frac{a^{x}\left(a^{h}-1\right)}{h}$.

That line is the entire derivation. Everything after it is bookkeeping on top of the algebra you just did.

$\displaystyle f'(x)$$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{a^{x+h}-a^{x}}{h}$the definition of the derivative
$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{a^{x}a^{h}-a^{x}}{h}$$\displaystyle a^{x+h}=a^{x}a^{h}$ — the exponent law, and nothing else
$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{a^{x}\left(a^{h}-1\right)}{h}$factor $\displaystyle a^{x}$ out of the numerator
$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle a^{x}\,\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{a^{h}-1}{h}$$\displaystyle h$ is what moves. $\displaystyle a^{x}$ has no $\displaystyle h$ in it, so it is a constant here and comes out front

Now stop and read what is left: $\displaystyle a^{x}$, times a limit with no $\displaystyle x$ anywhere inside it. Choose a base and that limit is one fixed number — the same number at every $\displaystyle x$.

So which number is it? Put $\displaystyle x = 0$ into the line we just finished and see.

Because $\displaystyle a^{0}=1$, the $\displaystyle a^{x}$ out front is just $\displaystyle 1$:

$$f'(0) \;=\; a^{0}\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{a^{h}-1}{h} \;=\; \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{a^{h}-1}{h}$$

So the leftover limit is $\displaystyle f'(0)$ — the slope of $\displaystyle y = a^{x}$ at the point $\displaystyle (0,1)$. It was never an anonymous number; it is the one piece of information about $\displaystyle a^{x}$ that the definition could not hand us for free.

Which is a rule for every exponential there is:

$$\frac{d}{dx}\left(a^{x}\right) \;=\; a^{x}\cdot f'(0) \;=\; a^{x}\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{a^{h}-1}{h}$$

An exponential's derivative is itself, times its own slope at $\displaystyle (0,1)$. One unknown number is the only thing standing between that and a clean rule.

Why That Number

So every exponential's derivative is itself times one number, and that number is its slope at $\displaystyle (0,1)$. All of them pass through $\displaystyle (0,1)$. The slope there is the only thing that differs:

Your turn

The slope at $\displaystyle (0,1)$ is $\displaystyle 0.69$ when $\displaystyle a = 2$ and $\displaystyle 1.10$ when $\displaystyle a = 3$. What does that guarantee about some base in between?

The slope climbs continuously from $\displaystyle 0.69$ to $\displaystyle 1.10$ as $\displaystyle a$ goes from $\displaystyle 2$ to $\displaystyle 3$, so somewhere between them is a base whose tangent at $\displaystyle (0,1)$ has slope exactly $\displaystyle 1$. (That is the Intermediate Value Theorem, from §2.5.)

For that base, and only that base, the leftover limit is $\displaystyle 1$ and the rule collapses to something perfect. So we give it a name.

Definition — The number e

$\displaystyle e$ is the base whose exponential has slope exactly $\displaystyle 1$ at $\displaystyle (0,1)$ — equivalently, the one positive number for which

$$\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{e^{h}-1}{h} \;=\; 1$$

To four places, $\displaystyle e \approx 2.7183$. It is irrational, like $\displaystyle \pi$.

Every other row is the Power Rule or a consequence of it. This one is not, which is why the variable being in the exponent is the thing to check first.

Tangent, and the Line Across It

Definition — The normal line

The normal line to a curve at a point is the line through that point perpendicular to the tangent. Its slope is $\displaystyle -1/m$, where $\displaystyle m$ is the slope of the tangent.

Your turn

$\displaystyle y = 4 - x^{2}$. Find the equations of the tangent line and the normal line at $\displaystyle x = 1$.

$\displaystyle y' = -2x$, so at $\displaystyle x = 1$ the slope is $\displaystyle m = -2$, and the point is $\displaystyle (1,3)$.

Tangent: $\displaystyle y - 3 = -2(x-1)$, so $\displaystyle y = -2x + 5$.

Normal: slope $\displaystyle -1/(-2) = \frac12$, so $\displaystyle y - 3 = \frac12(x-1)$, giving $\displaystyle y = \frac12 x + \frac52$.

Check the slopes multiply to $\displaystyle -1$: $\displaystyle (-2)\left(\frac12\right) = -1$. If they do not, one of the two lines is wrong.

Your turn

Close the notes. Write the Intermediate Value Theorem, hypothesis and conclusion, in full sentences.

Four pieces, and dropping any one of them costs marks:

1. $\displaystyle f$ is continuous on a closed interval $\displaystyle [a,b]$. 2. $\displaystyle N$ is a number between $\displaystyle f(a)$ and $\displaystyle f(b)$. 3. Then there is at least one $\displaystyle c$ — not exactly one. 4. That $\displaystyle c$ lies in the open interval $\displaystyle (a,b)$, and $\displaystyle f(c)=N$.

The theorem promises that such a $\displaystyle c$ exists. It never tells you where it is, and it does not claim there is only one.

“Know the theorem” means two separate skills. Writing it down is the first. Looking at a statement and deciding whether its hypothesis is actually met is the second, and that is the one that shows up as True/False.

What You Should Be Able to Do

  • Differentiate any polynomial, root or $\displaystyle 1/x^{n}$ — rewriting it as a power first.
  • Say why the Power Rule follows from the definition, at least for $\displaystyle x^{3}$.
  • Recognise when the variable is in the exponent instead of the base, and know the Power Rule does not apply there.
  • State $\displaystyle \frac{d}{dx}\left(e^{x}\right) = e^{x}$ and say what is special about $\displaystyle e$.
  • Write the equation of the tangent line and the normal line at a point, and check they are perpendicular.
  • Give an example showing $\displaystyle \frac{d}{dx}(fg) \neq f'g'$.

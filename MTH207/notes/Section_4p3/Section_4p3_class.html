4.3  Derivatives and the Shape of a Graph

MTH 207 Calculus I — Applications of Differentiation

What Section 4.1 Left Unfinished

Fermat's Theorem told us where to look: a local maximum or minimum can only happen at a critical number. It did not tell us which critical numbers are actually extrema — and we already know some are not.

$\displaystyle f(x) = x^{3}$ has $\displaystyle f'(0) = 0$, so $\displaystyle 0$ is a critical number. But look at it: the curve flattens for an instant and carries straight on. There is no hill and no valley at the origin. A critical number is a candidate, nothing more.

So we need a test that looks at a critical number and says: maximum, minimum, or neither. Every such test is going to be phrased in terms of whether $\displaystyle f$ is rising or falling on each side of that candidate — so before we can prove anything, those two words have to mean something exact.

What Increasing Really Means

Definition — Increasing and Decreasing

Let $\displaystyle x_{1}$ and $\displaystyle x_{2}$ be any two points of an interval $\displaystyle I$ with $\displaystyle x_{1} < x_{2}$.

  • $\displaystyle f$ is increasing on $\displaystyle I$ if $\displaystyle f(x_{1}) < f(x_{2})$.
  • $\displaystyle f$ is decreasing on $\displaystyle I$ if $\displaystyle f(x_{1}) > f(x_{2})$.

Two things to carry, and they are both about the words rather than the pictures. “On an interval” — increasing is never a property of a single point, always of a stretch, which is why every answer in this section is an interval. And “any two points” — it has to hold for every pair in that stretch, not just the pair you happened to look at.

Why $f' > 0$ Means Increasing

Take a rising function, pick any two points, and draw the secant between them.

That picture is the whole argument. If $\displaystyle f' > 0$ everywhere on the interval then every tangent slopes up — and the Mean Value Theorem says the secant between any two points is parallel to one of those tangents. So the secant slopes up too, which says exactly that $\displaystyle f(x_{2}) > f(x_{1})$. Flip every “up” to “down” and you have the other half.

Theorem — The Increasing/Decreasing Test

If $\displaystyle f'(x) > 0$ on an interval, then $\displaystyle f$ is increasing on that interval. If $\displaystyle f'(x) < 0$ on an interval, then $\displaystyle f$ is decreasing there.

Worth noticing what the MVT just bought. We knew nothing about $\displaystyle f$ except the sign of its derivative, and concluded something about the values of $\displaystyle f$ at two points we never computed. That is what the MVT is for — it is the bridge from derivative information back to function information. Without it, “$\displaystyle f' > 0$ means rising” is a picture you believe; with it, it is a theorem.

Show: if you want the algebra behind it

The MVT, as we stated it in §4.2 with $\displaystyle a = x_{1}$ and $\displaystyle b = x_{2}$:

$$f'(c) = \frac{f(x_{2}) - f(x_{1})}{x_{2} - x_{1}}$$

Multiply both sides by $\displaystyle x_{2}-x_{1}$, which is positive because we chose $\displaystyle x_{1} < x_{2}$:

$$f(x_{2}) - f(x_{1}) = f'(c)\left(x_{2} - x_{1}\right)$$

Now the difference sits alone on the left and the right is a product whose second factor is positive — so the sign of $\displaystyle f(x_{2}) - f(x_{1})$ is the sign of $\displaystyle f'(c)$, and nothing else.

$$f'(c) > 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; f(x_{2}) > f(x_{1}), \qquad f'(c) < 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; f(x_{2}) < f(x_{1})$$

And $\displaystyle x_{1} < x_{2}$ were any two points of the interval, so each line is a statement about every pair at once — which is the definition.

The Sign Chart

The test is about intervals, so the work is to split the number line into intervals on which $\displaystyle f'$ cannot change sign. Those breaks happen only at critical numbers — and at points where $\displaystyle f$ is undefined.

  1. Mark every place $\displaystyle f'$ could change sign — that is $\displaystyle f'(c)=0$ or $\displaystyle f'(c)$ does not exist. They cut the line into intervals.
  2. Determine the sign of $\displaystyle f'$ on each interval: pick a point inside it and evaluate $\displaystyle f'$. You need the sign, not the value.
  3. Put the signs on the chart and interpret them — rising where $\displaystyle f' > 0$, falling where $\displaystyle f' < 0$.

Work it on $\displaystyle f(x) = x^{3}-12x-5$. ⚠ Every chart in this section starts the same way, so watch the shape of the work, not the numbers.

step 1 — differentiate, then FACTOR

$\displaystyle f(x) = x^{3}-12x-5$the function we were handed
$\displaystyle f'(x) = 3x^{2}-12$Power Rule, term by term
$\displaystyle f'(x) = 3\left(x^{2}-4\right)$pull the common factor of $\displaystyle 3$ out — always do this before anything else
$\displaystyle f'(x) = 3\left(x-2\right)\left(x+2\right)$difference of squares; now the roots can be read off

Factoring is not tidying, it is the whole step. $\displaystyle 3x^{2}-12$ and $\displaystyle 3(x-2)(x+2)$ are the same function, but only the second one tells you where it is zero without solving anything.

step 2 — the critical numbers, and there are two kinds

$\displaystyle f'(x) = 3\left(x-2\right)\left(x+2\right) = 0$set the factored form equal to zero
$\displaystyle x = -2 \quad\text{and}\quad x = 2$a product is zero exactly when a factor is
$\displaystyle \text{$f'$ exists for every $x$}$$\displaystyle f'$ is a polynomial — so the second kind of critical number, where $\displaystyle f'$ fails to exist, contributes none here

Those two numbers cut the line into three intervals: $\displaystyle (-\infty,-2)$, $\displaystyle (-2,2)$ and $\displaystyle (2,\infty)$.

step 3 — one test point inside each interval

$\displaystyle f'(-3) = 3(-3-2)(-3+2) = 3(-5)(-1) = 15 > 0$any point of $\displaystyle (-\infty,-2)$ would do; $\displaystyle -3$ is just the easiest
$\displaystyle f'(0) = 3(0-2)(0+2) = 3(-2)(2) = -12 < 0$$\displaystyle 0$ is almost always the easiest point in a middle interval
$\displaystyle f'(3) = 3(3-2)(3+2) = 3(1)(5) = 15 > 0$and one from $\displaystyle (2,\infty)$

Only the sign was ever needed. The $\displaystyle 15$, $\displaystyle -12$ and $\displaystyle 15$ did no work that $\displaystyle +$, $\displaystyle -$ and $\displaystyle +$ would not have done. Here they were free, so we wrote them — but substitute into the factored form and you can stop as soon as you know whether each factor is positive or negative. On a messier $\displaystyle f'$ that is the difference between a line of work and a page of it, and no calculator is ever needed for any of it.

Now put those three signs on the chart:

Two number lines, and the second one is the point. The top line is bookkeeping: the signs of $\displaystyle f'$ and, at each marked number, whether it got there because $\displaystyle f'=0$ or because $\displaystyle f'$ DNE. The bottom line turns that into a picture — up, down, up. Read the arrows and you can already see the shape of the graph, with a peak at $\displaystyle x=-2$ and a valley at $\displaystyle x=2$, before computing a single $\displaystyle y$-value.

The arrows were right. The curve climbs to the peak at $\displaystyle x=-2$, falls to the valley at $\displaystyle x=2$, and climbs again — and the only thing the graph adds is the two $\displaystyle y$-values, $\displaystyle 11$ and $\displaystyle -21$. Everything about the shape was already on the second number line.

A number lands on the chart for one of two reasons, and the chart records which. Here both came from $\displaystyle f'=0$. They do not have to — so run the same three steps on $\displaystyle f(x) = x^{2/3}$ and watch the second kind appear:

steps 1 and 2 — differentiate, and look for BOTH kinds

$\displaystyle f(x) = x^{2/3}$the function
$\displaystyle f'(x) = \tfrac{2}{3}x^{-1/3}$Power Rule — subtract one from the exponent
$\displaystyle f'(x) = \frac{2}{3\sqrt[3]{x}}$a negative exponent is a denominator; now both kinds are visible at a glance
$\displaystyle \text{$f'$ is never $0$}$the numerator is the constant $\displaystyle 2$, and $\displaystyle 2 \neq 0$ — so the FIRST kind contributes nothing
$\displaystyle \text{$f'(0)$ does not exist}$the denominator is $\displaystyle 0$ at $\displaystyle x=0$, and $\displaystyle f(0)=0$ is defined — so $\displaystyle x=0$ IS a critical number, of the second kind

step 3 — one test point on each side of $\displaystyle 0$

$\displaystyle f'(-1) = \frac{2}{3\sqrt[3]{-1}} = \frac{2}{3(-1)} = -\tfrac{2}{3} < 0$the cube root of a negative is negative — $\displaystyle \sqrt[3]{-1} = -1$
$\displaystyle f'(1) = \frac{2}{3\sqrt[3]{1}} = \frac{2}{3} > 0$and on the right

A minimum where the derivative does not exist. The curve comes to a point rather than levelling off — the tangent there is vertical, so $\displaystyle f'(0)$ cannot be a number. Fermat's Theorem never promised $\displaystyle f'(c)=0$ at an extremum; it promised $\displaystyle c$ is a critical number, and “$\displaystyle f'$ does not exist” is the other half of that definition earning its place.

Testing one point per interval is enough, and that is not a shortcut — it is the Intermediate Value Theorem. If $\displaystyle f'$ were positive somewhere in an interval and negative somewhere else in it, $\displaystyle f'$ would have to be zero in between, and that zero would be a critical number we had already marked.

Show: step 1 — the critical numbers
$$f'(x) = 6x^{2} - 18x + 12 = 6\left(x^{2}-3x+2\right) = 6\left(x-1\right)\left(x-2\right)$$

$\displaystyle f'$ is a polynomial, so it exists everywhere. The critical numbers are $\displaystyle x=1$ and $\displaystyle x=2$.

Test $\displaystyle x=0$: $\displaystyle 6(-1)(-2) > 0$. Test $\displaystyle x=1.5$: $\displaystyle 6(0.5)(-0.5) < 0$. Test $\displaystyle x=3$: $\displaystyle 6(2)(1) > 0$. Only the sign matters, so pick the easiest number in each interval — then record the three answers on one line:

Show: step 3 — say it

$\displaystyle f$ is increasing on $\displaystyle (-\infty,1)$ and on $\displaystyle (2,\infty)$, and decreasing on $\displaystyle (1,2)$. The First Derivative Test

Theorem — The First Derivative Test

Suppose $\displaystyle c$ is a critical number of a continuous function $\displaystyle f$.

  • If $\displaystyle f'$ changes from positive to negative at $\displaystyle c$, then $\displaystyle f$ has a local maximum at $\displaystyle c$.
  • If $\displaystyle f'$ changes from negative to positive at $\displaystyle c$, then $\displaystyle f$ has a local minimum at $\displaystyle c$.
  • If $\displaystyle f'$ does not change sign at $\displaystyle c$, then $\displaystyle f$ has neither at $\displaystyle c$.

The third bullet is the one that matters, and it is the reason $\displaystyle x^{3}$ was the first thing on the board today. A critical number is not a promise. The test is about the change in sign, never about the value of $\displaystyle f'$ at $\displaystyle c$ — which is zero, and tells you nothing by itself.

Your Turn: A Critical Number That Is Neither

Your turn

Find the critical numbers of $\displaystyle f(x) = x^{3} - 3x^{2} + 3x$ and classify each one. Draw both number lines — the sign chart, and the arrows underneath it.

Show: there is one critical number, and it is not an extremum
$$f'(x) = 3x^{2} - 6x + 3 = 3\left(x^{2}-2x+1\right) = 3\left(x-1\right)^{2}$$

So $\displaystyle x=1$ is the only critical number. Now test either side:

$$f'(0) = 3(1) > 0 \qquad f'(2) = 3(1) > 0$$

$\displaystyle f'$ is positive on both sides — in fact $\displaystyle f'\geq 0$ everywhere, since it is $\displaystyle 3$ times a square.

There is no sign change, so by the First Derivative Test $\displaystyle f$ has neither a maximum nor a minimum at $\displaystyle x=1$. The curve flattens for an instant and carries on rising.

This is $\displaystyle x^{3}$ wearing a disguise — and you could not have spotted it from $\displaystyle f'(1) = 0$ alone. The squared factor is the tell: a factor of $\displaystyle f'$ raised to an even power touches zero without crossing it, so the sign on the two sides is the same.

One That Is Not a Polynomial

My turn

Find the intervals of increase and decrease of $\displaystyle f(x) = x e^{-x}$, and classify any local extrema. Watch this one — it is about which factors you are allowed to ignore.

Show: product rule first, then factor
$$f'(x) = (1)e^{-x} + x\left(-e^{-x}\right) = e^{-x}\left(1 - x\right)$$
Show: the sign is decided by one factor only

$\displaystyle e^{-x}$ is always positive, so it can never change the sign of $\displaystyle f'$ — the whole sign question is the sign of $\displaystyle 1-x$.

$$f'(x) > 0 \iff x < 1 \qquad f'(x) < 0 \iff x > 1$$

So $\displaystyle f$ is increasing on $\displaystyle (-\infty,1)$ and decreasing on $\displaystyle (1,\infty)$, and by the First Derivative Test there is a local maximum at $\displaystyle x=1$, of value $\displaystyle f(1) = e^{-1} = \frac{1}{e}$.

Factor $\displaystyle f'$ before you test anything, then throw away every factor that cannot change sign. An exponential is never zero and never negative; a square is never negative; a sum of squares plus one is never zero. Each one you discard is an interval you do not have to test.

What You Should Be Able to Do

  • Use the sign of $\displaystyle f'$ to find the intervals where $\displaystyle f$ increases and decreases, and say why one test point per interval is enough.
  • Apply the First Derivative Test to classify a critical number as a local maximum, a local minimum, or neither.
  • Recognize when a critical number is neither, and explain it from the sign of $\displaystyle f'$ on the two sides.
  • Factor $\displaystyle f'$ and discard the factors that cannot change sign.

Concavity: Which Way It Bends

Increasing and decreasing is about where the curve goes. Concavity is about which way it bends, and it is the second derivative that knows.

Definition — Concave Up and Concave Down

$\displaystyle f$ is concave up on an interval if $\displaystyle f'' > 0$ there, and concave down if $\displaystyle f'' < 0$ there.

Why that is the right definition: $\displaystyle f''$ is the derivative of $\displaystyle f'$, so $\displaystyle f'' > 0$ says the slope is increasing. A curve whose slope keeps growing has to curl upward — it holds its tangent line below itself.

This is the question §3.10 could not answer. When we estimated $\displaystyle \sqrt{9.2}$ with a tangent line and asked whether the estimate was too big or too small, the honest answer was “look at the picture” — the reason is concavity, and we did not have it yet. Now we do: the square root is concave down, so its tangent line lies above it, so a linear approximation to a square root overestimates. Every time.

Inflection Points, and the Trap

Definition — Inflection Point

A point on the curve where $\displaystyle f$ is continuous and the concavity changes — from up to down, or down to up.

Read that again: it is about a CHANGE. Solving $\displaystyle f''=0$ gives you candidates, exactly the way solving $\displaystyle f'=0$ gave you candidates in §4.1. It does not give you inflection points, and the test is the same shape as the First Derivative Test: check the sign of $\displaystyle f''$ on both sides.

Both have $\displaystyle f''(0) = 0$. Only one has an inflection point there. So “$\displaystyle f''(c) = 0$, therefore $\displaystyle (c,f(c))$ is an inflection point” is false, and it is false for exactly the reason “$\displaystyle f'(c)=0$, therefore $\displaystyle f$ has an extremum at $\displaystyle c$” was false in §4.1. Same mistake, one derivative up.

Your Turn: Concavity and Inflection

Your turn

For $\displaystyle f(x) = x^{3} - 3x^{2} + 3x$ — yesterday's function — find the intervals of concavity and any inflection points. Draw both number lines again, but now the top one is the sign chart for $\displaystyle f''$, and underneath it draw the bend rather than arrows.

Show: differentiate twice, then check for a CHANGE
$$f'(x) = 3\left(x-1\right)^{2} \qquad f''(x) = 6\left(x-1\right)$$

$\displaystyle f''=0$ at $\displaystyle x=1$. Test either side:

$$f''(0) = -6 < 0 \quad\text{concave down} \qquad f''(2) = 6 > 0 \quad\text{concave up}$$

The concavity does change, so there is an inflection point at $\displaystyle x=1$, namely the point $\displaystyle (1,1)$.

Hold yesterday's result next to this one. At $\displaystyle x=1$ this function has a critical number that is not an extremum, and an inflection point. Those are not a coincidence: the curve flattens out and keeps going precisely because it is switching which way it bends.

The Second Derivative Test

Theorem — The Second Derivative Test

Suppose $\displaystyle f''$ is continuous near $\displaystyle c$ and $\displaystyle f'(c) = 0$.

  • If $\displaystyle f''(c) > 0$, then $\displaystyle f$ has a local minimum at $\displaystyle c$.
  • If $\displaystyle f''(c) < 0$, then $\displaystyle f$ has a local maximum at $\displaystyle c$.
  • If $\displaystyle f''(c) = 0$, the test says nothing. Go back to the first derivative test.

It is a picture, not a rule to memorize. A horizontal tangent on a curve that is bending upward sits at the bottom of a bowl; a horizontal tangent on a curve bending downward sits at the top of a hill. And notice what is cheaper here: this test evaluates $\displaystyle f''$ at one point, where the first derivative test needs the sign of $\displaystyle f'$ on both sides.

Your Turn: One Function, Three Warnings

Your turn

Let $\displaystyle f(x) = x^{4} - 4x^{3}$. Find all critical numbers, classify each, and find the intervals of concavity and any inflection points. Four number lines this time: a sign chart for $\displaystyle f'$ with its arrows, and a sign chart for $\displaystyle f''$ with its bends.

Show: step 1 — critical numbers
$$f'(x) = 4x^{3} - 12x^{2} = 4x^{2}\left(x-3\right)$$

So $\displaystyle x=0$ and $\displaystyle x=3$.

Show: step 2 — try the second derivative test, and watch it fail
$$f''(x) = 12x^{2} - 24x = 12x\left(x-2\right)$$

At $\displaystyle x=3$: $\displaystyle f''(3) = 36 > 0$, so a local minimum, of value $\displaystyle f(3) = 81 - 108 = -27$. At $\displaystyle x=0$: $\displaystyle f''(0) = 0$. The test says nothing. Fall back to the sign of $\displaystyle f'$: the factor $\displaystyle 4x^{2}$ is never negative, so the sign of $\displaystyle f'$ is the sign of $\displaystyle x-3$, which is negative on both sides of $\displaystyle 0$. No sign change, so $\displaystyle x=0$ is neither a maximum nor a minimum.

Show: the two charts side by side, which is the whole analysis

Read them together: $\displaystyle x=0$ appears on both charts, and it is an inflection point on the lower one while being a non-extremum on the upper one.

Show: step 3 — concavity

$\displaystyle f''=12x(x-2)$ is zero at $\displaystyle x=0$ and $\displaystyle x=2$, and it is an upward parabola, so it is positive outside $\displaystyle [0,2]$ and negative inside.

$$\text{concave up on } (-\infty,0) \text{ and } (2,\infty); \quad \text{concave down on } (0,2)$$

Concavity changes at both, so there are inflection points at $\displaystyle x=0$ and $\displaystyle x=2$.

Three warnings in one function. $\displaystyle x=0$ is a critical number that is not an extremum; the second derivative test is silent there; and $\displaystyle x=0$ is an inflection point. A critical number and an inflection point can sit at the same place, and neither one implies the other.

Reading the Shape from the Signs Alone

Everything so far started from a formula for $\displaystyle f$. The harder question — and the one worth the most on Exam 3 — hands you $\displaystyle f'$ and $\displaystyle f''$ and never tells you what $\displaystyle f$ is.

Your turn

A function $\displaystyle f$ has

$$f'(x) = \left(x+1\right)\left(x-2\right) \qquad f''(x) = 2x-1$$

Find the intervals of increase and decrease, classify the critical numbers, find the intervals of concavity, and locate any inflection points. Build all four number lines. You are never given $\displaystyle f$, so the charts are the only picture you get — and they are enough.

Show: from $\displaystyle f'$ — where it rises and falls

$\displaystyle f'=0$ at $\displaystyle x=-1$ and $\displaystyle x=2$. It is an upward parabola, so it is positive outside $\displaystyle [-1,2]$ and negative inside:

$$\text{increasing on } (-\infty,-1) \text{ and } (2,\infty); \quad \text{decreasing on } (-1,2)$$

So $\displaystyle f'$ goes $\displaystyle +$ to $\displaystyle -$ at $\displaystyle x=-1$: local maximum. And $\displaystyle -$ to $\displaystyle +$ at $\displaystyle x=2$: local minimum.

Show: from $\displaystyle f''$ — which way it bends

$\displaystyle f''=0$ at $\displaystyle x=\frac{1}{2}$, negative to its left and positive to its right:

$$\text{concave down on } \left(-\infty,\tfrac{1}{2}\right); \quad \text{concave up on } \left(\tfrac{1}{2},\infty\right)$$

and the concavity changes, so there is an inflection point at $\displaystyle x=\frac{1}{2}$.

Show: all four lines at once
Show: the cross-check that costs nothing

The two answers must agree. $\displaystyle f''(-1) = -3 < 0$: concave down at the local maximum, which is what a hilltop should be. $\displaystyle f''(2) = 3 > 0$: concave up at the local minimum. If those had disagreed, one of the sign charts was wrong.

Your turn

Now the other direction, and no formula at all: sketch the graph of any function with $\displaystyle f'(x) > 0$ and $\displaystyle f''(x) < 0$ for every $\displaystyle x$.

Show: what the two conditions each demand

$\displaystyle f' > 0$ everywhere: always rising, never flat, never falling. $\displaystyle f'' < 0$ everywhere: always concave down, bending the same way the whole time. Together: a curve that climbs the entire way but climbs ever more slowly — $\displaystyle \sqrt{x}$, or $\displaystyle \ln x$, or the left half of a downward parabola. There is no critical number, no extremum and no inflection point anywhere, because nothing ever changes sign.

Notice you never needed $\displaystyle f$. Every question in this frame was answered from where $\displaystyle f'$ and $\displaystyle f''$ are positive and where they are negative — and that is all a sign chart ever carries. Exam 3 pays seventeen points for exactly this, and never once gives you the function.

  • Find inflection points — and explain why $\displaystyle f''(c)=0$ is not enough.
  • Apply the Second Derivative Test, and say what to do when it returns nothing.
  • Given only $\displaystyle f'$ and $\displaystyle f''$, describe the shape of $\displaystyle f$ completely; and given a description, sketch a curve that fits it.

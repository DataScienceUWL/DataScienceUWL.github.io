3.9  Related Rates

MTH 207 Calculus I — Differentiation Rules

A Rate You Cannot Measure

Some rates you can measure directly. Others you can only get from a rate you can measure, through an equation that ties them together.

My turn

A square's sides are growing at $\displaystyle 3$ cm/s. How fast is its area growing at the moment the side is $\displaystyle 10$ cm? Watch this one all the way through. You get the next one.

Show: first, estimate it — two pictures, a tenth of a second apart
$$\frac{\Delta A}{\Delta t} = \frac{10.3^{2} - 10^{2}}{0.1} = \frac{106.09 - 100}{0.1} = 60.9\ \text{cm}^{2}\text{/s}$$

That is an average rate over a tenth of a second — the shaded border divided by the time it took to appear.

That estimate is as far as arithmetic goes. To get the rate at the instant there are five steps, and the rest of this section is them:

  1. Draw it, and label what moves with letters — not numbers.
  2. Write what you know and what you want, as derivatives.
  3. Find the relating equation — one equation, in those letters.
  4. Differentiate both sides with respect to $\displaystyle t$.
  5. Now substitute the instant's values, and solve.
Here are all five, on the square you just estimated.

Show: Step 1 — draw it, and label what moves

$\displaystyle 10$, $\displaystyle 10.3$, $\displaystyle 100$ and $\displaystyle 106.09$ do not appear in a step-1 picture. They are where the square was at two instants, and a picture full of instants cannot be differentiated.

Show: Step 2 — what you know, and what you want

Know: $\displaystyle \frac{ds}{dt} = 3$ cm/s.   Want: $\displaystyle \frac{dA}{dt}$ at the instant $\displaystyle s = 10$. The $\displaystyle 10$ is not what the square is — it is where it is at one instant. A moment later the side is not $\displaystyle 10$ any more, which is why it is written here as a condition and not into the picture.

Show: Step 3 — the relating equation

One equation, in those letters, true at every instant:

$$A = s^{2}$$
Show: Step 4 — differentiate both sides with respect to $\displaystyle t$

$\displaystyle s$ moves, so it carries its own rate — this is the Chain Rule, and it is §3.5 with $\displaystyle t$ where $\displaystyle x$ used to be:

$$\frac{dA}{dt} = 2s\,\frac{ds}{dt}$$
Show: Step 5 — now substitute, and solve

Now the instant's values go in, and not one step sooner:

$$\frac{dA}{dt} = 2\left(10\right)\left(3\right) = 60\ \text{cm}^{2}\text{/s}$$

Compare that with the estimate: $\displaystyle 60.9$ over a tenth of a second, $\displaystyle 60$ exactly at the instant.

That is the whole method. You will do it on the next problem, and on every one after that. The only thing that changes is which equation goes in step 3.

Everything in this section is two quantities, an equation relating them, and time as the variable underneath both. You have done this before: it is §3.5, with $\displaystyle t$ where $\displaystyle x$ used to be.

Substitute Last

Step 5 said now substitute. That word is the whole section. There was another order available, and it is the one most people reach for first.

My turn

The same square, the other way round.

substitute first, then differentiate

$\displaystyle A = s^{2}$the same relating equation we just used
$\displaystyle A = \left(10\right)^{2} = 100$substitute $\displaystyle s = 10$ first
$\displaystyle \frac{dA}{dt} = 0$$\displaystyle 100$ is a constant — its derivative is zero

Substituting early freezes the picture. The instant you write $\displaystyle s=10$ you have replaced a moving square with a still photograph, and a photograph has no rates in it. Substitute at the last step, never the first.

A value that is constant for all time is different — that one you may substitute whenever you like. The test is not when you are told the number, it is whether the quantity moves.

The Method

The same five steps, in this order, every time.

  1. Draw it, and label what moves with letters — not numbers.
  2. Write what you know and what you want, as derivatives.
  3. Find the relating equation — one equation, in those letters.
  4. Differentiate both sides with respect to $\displaystyle t$.
  5. Now substitute the instant's values, and solve.
You watched them on the square. This one is yours.

Your turn

Two cars leave the same point. One drives south at $\displaystyle 60$ mi/h, the other west at $\displaystyle 25$ mi/h. How fast is the distance between them changing two hours later?

Show: Step 1 — draw it, and label what moves
Show: Step 2 — what you know, and what you want

Know: $\displaystyle \frac{dx}{dt} = 60$ and $\displaystyle \frac{dy}{dt} = 25$. Want: $\displaystyle \frac{dz}{dt}$ when $\displaystyle t = 2$.

Show: Step 3 — the relating equation

The roads meet at a right angle, so $\displaystyle x$ and $\displaystyle y$ are the legs and $\displaystyle z$ is the hypotenuse:

$$z^{2} = x^{2} + y^{2}$$
Show: Step 4 — differentiate both sides with respect to $\displaystyle t$
$$2z\,\frac{dz}{dt} = 2x\,\frac{dx}{dt} + 2y\,\frac{dy}{dt}$$

Every variable is changing, so every one carries its own rate.

Show: Step 5 — now substitute, and solve

Where are they at $\displaystyle t = 2$? $\displaystyle x = 120$ and $\displaystyle y = 50$, so $\displaystyle z = \sqrt{120^{2}+50^{2}} = 130$. $\displaystyle z$ is not given — it has to be computed.

$$2\left(130\right)\frac{dz}{dt} = 2\left(120\right)\left(60\right) + 2\left(50\right)\left(25\right)$$
$$130\,\frac{dz}{dt} = 7200 + 1250 = 8450$$

Every term carried a $\displaystyle 2$ — divide it out before anything else.

$$\frac{dz}{dt} = \frac{8450}{130} = 65\ \text{mi/h}$$

Solve, and say the units.

What Is Constant and What Is Not

Not every number in the story is a variable. Telling them apart decides what your equation even looks like.

Your turn

A cylindrical tank of radius $\displaystyle 2$ m is filled at $\displaystyle 3$ m$\displaystyle ^{3}$/min. How fast is the water level rising? Before computing: which of $\displaystyle r$, $\displaystyle h$, $\displaystyle V$ actually change?

Show: Step 1 — draw it, and label what moves
Show: Step 2 — what you know, and what you want

$\displaystyle V$ and $\displaystyle h$ change; $\displaystyle r = 2$ does not. Know: $\displaystyle \frac{dV}{dt} = 3$. Want: $\displaystyle \frac{dh}{dt}$.

Show: Step 3 — the relating equation, with the constant already in
$$V = \pi r^{2} h = \pi\left(2\right)^{2}h = 4\pi h$$

$\displaystyle r$ is constant, so this one you may substitute now — and it leaves a single moving letter on the right.

Show: Step 4 — differentiate both sides with respect to $\displaystyle t$
$$\frac{dV}{dt} = 4\pi\,\frac{dh}{dt}$$
Show: Step 5 — now substitute, and solve
$$3 = 4\pi\,\frac{dh}{dt} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{dh}{dt} = \frac{3}{4\pi}\ \text{m/min}$$

Leave it as $\displaystyle \frac{3}{4\pi}$. A decimal here hides that the answer is exactly the inflow divided by the surface area.

Constant for all time $\displaystyle \to$ substitute early. Changing $\displaystyle \to$ substitute last. Ask of every number: is this a fact about the container, or about this instant?

This is a rider on step 3. Before you differentiate, get the relating equation down to as few moving letters as you can — and a constant is not a moving letter.

Steady in, not steady up. Here the level does rise steadily — but only because the tank is a cylinder. Pour into a cone or a sphere at a constant rate and the level races and then crawls, because the area it is spreading over keeps changing.

When the Picture Gives the Equation

Yesterday the relating equation was handed to you. Today you have to find it — and it is almost always hiding in a pair of similar triangles.

Your turn

A street light sits on a $\displaystyle 15$ ft pole. A man $\displaystyle 6$ ft tall walks away from it at $\displaystyle 5$ ft/s. How fast is the tip of his shadow moving? Draw it first. Label the distance from the pole $\displaystyle x$ and the shadow length $\displaystyle s$.

Show: Step 1 — draw it, and label what moves
Show: Step 2 — what you know, and what you want

Know: $\displaystyle \frac{dx}{dt} = 5$. Want: how fast the tip moves, which is $\displaystyle \frac{d}{dt}\left(x+s\right)$ — not $\displaystyle \frac{ds}{dt}$.

Show: Step 3 — the relating equation, from similar triangles

The big triangle runs lamp to tip: height $\displaystyle 15$, base $\displaystyle x+s$. The small one is the man: height $\displaystyle 6$, base $\displaystyle s$. Same angles, so

$$\frac{x+s}{15} = \frac{s}{6} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 6\left(x+s\right) = 15s \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 6x = 9s \quad\Longrightarrow\quad s = \tfrac{2}{3}x$$

Simplify it before you differentiate — one moving letter on each side.

Show: Step 4 — differentiate both sides with respect to $\displaystyle t$
$$\frac{ds}{dt} = \tfrac{2}{3}\,\frac{dx}{dt}$$
Show: Step 5 — now substitute, and solve
$$\frac{ds}{dt} = \tfrac{2}{3}\left(5\right) = \tfrac{10}{3}\ \text{ft/s}$$

That is how fast the shadow lengthens. The question asked for the tip, which sits at $\displaystyle x+s$:

$$\frac{d}{dt}\left(x+s\right) = 5 + \tfrac{10}{3} = \tfrac{25}{3}\ \text{ft/s}$$

Read the question again before you answer it. The shadow's length and the shadow's tip are different quantities with different rates, and only one of them was asked for.

Notice what is not here: no value of $\displaystyle x$. The tip moves at $\displaystyle \frac{25}{3}$ ft/s no matter where he is — some related rates are constant, and the algebra tells you so.

The Trough

The hardest shape in the homework, and it is still the same five steps.

My turn

A trough $\displaystyle 10$ ft long has ends shaped like isosceles triangles, $\displaystyle 3$ ft across the top and $\displaystyle 1$ ft deep. Water pours in at $\displaystyle 12$ ft$\displaystyle ^{3}$/min. How fast is the depth rising when the water is $\displaystyle \tfrac12$ ft deep?

Show: Step 1 — draw it, and label what moves
Show: Step 2 — what you know, and what you want

Know: $\displaystyle \frac{dV}{dt} = 12$. Want: $\displaystyle \frac{dy}{dt}$ at the instant $\displaystyle y = \tfrac12$.

Show: Step 3 — the relating equation, reduced to ONE variable

Volume is cross-section times length, and the length $\displaystyle 10$ never changes:

$$V = A\cdot 10$$

The water's cross-section is a triangle that changes shape — deeper water is also wider — so similar triangles give the width in terms of the depth, and the second variable disappears:

$$\frac{w}{y} = \frac{3}{1} \;\Longrightarrow\; w = 3y \qquad A = \tfrac12\,w\,y = \tfrac12\left(3y\right)y = \tfrac32 y^{2}$$
$$V = 10\cdot\tfrac32 y^{2} = 15y^{2} \qquad\textbf{one variable left}$$
Show: Step 4 — differentiate both sides with respect to $\displaystyle t$
$$\frac{dV}{dt} = 30y\,\frac{dy}{dt}$$
Show: Step 5 — now substitute, and solve
$$12 = 30\left(\tfrac12\right)\frac{dy}{dt} = 15\,\frac{dy}{dt} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{dy}{dt} = \tfrac{4}{5}\ \text{ft/min}$$

Reduce to one variable before you differentiate. Two unknowns in the relating equation means two unknown rates and one equation — unsolvable. Similar triangles are what remove the second one.

Step 3 again. Yesterday the rider was substitute what is constant; today it is use the geometry to eliminate what is not. Same step, same goal — one moving letter before you differentiate.

Units Are Part of the Answer

A rate without units is not finished. Read them off the derivative itself: $\displaystyle \frac{dV}{dt}$ is volume per time — ft$\displaystyle ^{3}$/min — and $\displaystyle \frac{dy}{dt}$ is length per time, ft/min.

And the sign is information. A negative rate means the quantity is shrinking, and that is an answer, not an error. Two quantities can be related with one rising while the other falls — walk toward the lamp and the shadow shortens.

Your turn

A balloon is deflating. Which of these are negative?

$$\frac{dV}{dt} \qquad \frac{dr}{dt} \qquad \frac{dA}{dt}$$
Show: all three, and for the same reason

Shrinking volume forces shrinking radius, and shrinking radius forces shrinking surface area — they are tied by $\displaystyle V=\frac43\pi r^{3}$ and $\displaystyle A = 4\pi r^{2}$, both increasing in $\displaystyle r$. But none of the three rates equals another: they have different units and different sizes.

What You Should Be Able to Do

  • Draw the situation and label what moves with letters, never with the instant's numbers.
  • Write what you know and want as derivatives with respect to time.
  • Find the relating equation — a formula, Pythagoras, or similar triangles — and reduce it to one variable.
  • Differentiate first, substitute last. Every moving quantity carries its own rate.
  • Substitute a quantity early only when it is constant for all time.
  • State the answer with units, and read a negative rate as shrinking.

No new calculus here — this is §3.5's Chain Rule with $\displaystyle t$ underneath. What is new is that nobody hands you the equation.

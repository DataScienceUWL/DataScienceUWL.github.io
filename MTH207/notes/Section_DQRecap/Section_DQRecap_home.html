Two Questions, One Quotient

We met the same fraction twice, from two questions that look unrelated.

The tangent problem. How steep is the curve at a point?

We could only find the slope of a line through two points, so we took a second point $\displaystyle Q$ and computed the slope of the secant:

$$m_{PQ} \;=\; \frac{f(x)-f(a)}{x-a}$$

The velocity problem. How fast is the ball going at an instant?

We could only find speed over an interval, so we took a second time and computed the average velocity:

$$\text{avg vel} \;=\; \frac{s(x)-s(a)}{x-a}$$

Two questions, one fraction. In both, the obstacle was the same: what we wanted was about a single point, and the only tool we had needed two.

The Same Picture Both Times

The secant's slope is the average velocity — not an analogy, the same number. And the tangent's slope is the instantaneous velocity.

Your turn

So what has to happen to $\displaystyle Q$ for the secant to turn into the tangent?

$\displaystyle Q$ slides toward $\displaystyle P$. The secant pivots, and what it settles on is the tangent.

Both Answers Are Limits

Neither question could be answered directly. Both were answered the same way — compute the quotient on a shrinking gap and see what the numbers head for:

$$\text{tangent slope} \;=\; \lim_{x \to a} \frac{f(x)-f(a)}{x-a} \qquad \text{instantaneous velocity} \;=\; \lim_{x \to a} \frac{s(x)-s(a)}{x-a}$$

Same limit, two names. That is why one idea — the limit — answers both, and it is why we spent Thursday on what a limit actually means.

The Same Secant, Labelled Two Ways

You will see this quotient written another way, and it is worth five minutes because from Section 2.7 onward it is the only way you will see it.

Here is one secant line, drawn twice. Look at what is different.

Nothing moved. Same curve, same $\displaystyle P$, same $\displaystyle Q$, same line, same rise over the same run. The only thing that changed is what we call the second input — and therefore what we call the run.

Why the Two Names Agree

Call the gap $\displaystyle h$. Then the second input is $\displaystyle a+h$ rather than $\displaystyle x$:

$$h \;=\; x - a \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad x \;=\; a + h$$

Substituting that into the left-hand version turns it into the right-hand one:

$\displaystyle \frac{f(x)-f(a)}{x-a}$$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle \frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{(a+h)-a}$
$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle \frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}$

And $\displaystyle x \to a$ is exactly $\displaystyle h \to 0$. Closing the gap is the gap going to zero, so the two limits below are the same limit:

$$\lim_{x \to a}\frac{f(x)-f(a)}{x-a} \qquad=\qquad \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}$$

Does $Q$ Have to Be on the Right?

Nothing above said $\displaystyle Q$ has to come after $\displaystyle P$.

Your turn

Our ball intervals all started at $\displaystyle t = 0.5$. What is $\displaystyle h$ if the second time is $\displaystyle t = 0.4$ instead?

$\displaystyle h = 0.4 - 0.5 = \mathbf{-0.1}$. A point on the earlier side just makes $\displaystyle h$ negative:

Both the rise and the run flip sign, so the quotient is still the slope of that secant — nothing else changes.

Which is why approaching from the left is not a separate idea. It is the same quotient with $\displaystyle h < 0$ — exactly the one-sided limits we named on Thursday.

2.7  Derivatives and Rates of Change

MTH 207 Calculus I — Limits and Derivatives

Let me try the examples first →

You Have Already Computed These

In 2.1 we asked two questions that looked completely different.

  • Geometry: what is the slope of the tangent line at $\displaystyle P$?
  • Motion: how fast is the ball going at the instant $\displaystyle t = 0.5$?

Both came down to the same quotient, and both were finished by the same limit.

$$\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h}$$

That limit is used so often it gets a name and a symbol of its own. Today is the name.

The Definition

Definition — The derivative of $\displaystyle f$ at $\displaystyle a$
$$f'(a) \;=\; \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h}$$

provided this limit exists.

Setting $\displaystyle x = a + h$ gives the same number written a second way:

$$f'(a) \;=\; \lim_{x\to a}\frac{f(x) - f(a)}{x - a}$$
Your turn

Why are these the same? What happens to $\displaystyle x$ as $\displaystyle h\to 0$?

If $\displaystyle x = a+h$ then $\displaystyle h = x - a$, and $\displaystyle h\to 0$ says exactly $\displaystyle x\to a$. The numerator $\displaystyle f(a+h)-f(a)$ becomes $\displaystyle f(x)-f(a)$ and the denominator $\displaystyle h$ becomes $\displaystyle x-a$. Same quotient, same limit, different bookkeeping.

Use the $\displaystyle h$ form when you are handed a formula. Use the $\displaystyle x$ form when you are handed a point and want to keep the algebra in terms of $\displaystyle x$.

My turn

Two things that cost real marks. Never write $\displaystyle =\tfrac00$ — that is not a value the limit equals, it is a failed attempt, and it belongs in the margin. And $\displaystyle \lim$ rides every line until the last one.

From Slope to Line

A derivative is a slope. One slope plus one point is a line.

$$y - f(a) \;=\; f'(a)\,(x - a)$$
Your turn

You just found $\displaystyle f'(2) = 1$ for $\displaystyle f(x) = x^2 - 3x$. Write the equation of the tangent line to $\displaystyle y = f(x)$ at $\displaystyle x = 2$.

$\displaystyle f(2) = 4 - 6 = -2$, so the point is $\displaystyle (2,-2)$ and the slope is $\displaystyle 1$:

$$y + 2 = 1\,(x - 2) \qquad\text{or}\qquad y = x - 4$$

The derivative gives you the slope. It does not give you the point — you still have to evaluate $\displaystyle f(a)$, and forgetting that is the single most common way this problem is lost.

A Derivative Is a Rate, and Rates Have Units

The quotient $\displaystyle \dfrac{f(x) - f(a)}{x - a}$ is $\displaystyle \dfrac{\text{change in output}}{\text{change in input}}$, so:

$$\text{units of } f'(a) \;=\; \frac{\text{units of } f}{\text{units of } x}$$
  • $\displaystyle s(t)$ in feet, $\displaystyle t$ in seconds $\displaystyle \Rightarrow$ $\displaystyle s'(t)$ in $\displaystyle \text{ft}/\text{sec}$ — velocity.
  • $\displaystyle C(q)$ in dollars, $\displaystyle q$ in units $\displaystyle \Rightarrow$ $\displaystyle C'(q)$ in $\displaystyle \text{dollars}/\text{unit}$ — marginal cost.
  • $\displaystyle P(t)$ in bacteria, $\displaystyle t$ in hours $\displaystyle \Rightarrow$ $\displaystyle P'(t)$ in $\displaystyle \text{bacteria}/\text{hour}$ — growth rate.

An answer with no units is not an answer to a rate question. The units come straight off the definition — you never have to remember them.

The Ball From Day One

On day one we watched a ball's average velocity over shrinking intervals and said the numbers were heading for $\displaystyle 16$ ft/sec — then admitted that nothing there proved it. That debt is now payable.

$$s(t) = 64 - 16(t-1)^{2}\text{ feet},\qquad t\text{ in seconds}$$
Your turn

Find $\displaystyle s'(0.5)$ from the definition. Give the number and what it means, with units.

$\displaystyle s'(0.5)$$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{s(0.5+h) - s(0.5)}{h}$the definition, with $\displaystyle s(0.5) = 64 - 16(0.25) = 60$

$\displaystyle s'(0.5) = 16\text{ ft/sec}$ — at the instant $\displaystyle t = 0.5$ the ball is rising at $\displaystyle 16$ feet per second. Not over an interval. At that instant.

A company's cost to produce $\displaystyle q$ chairs is $\displaystyle C(q)$ dollars, and $\displaystyle C'(200) = 45$.

  2. Write one sentence a manager would understand.

When All You Have Is a Table

Real data does not come with a formula. It comes like this:

A bacteria population measured once an hour
$\displaystyle t$ (hours)01234
$\displaystyle P(t)$ (bacteria)100165271447738
Your turn

Estimate $\displaystyle P'(2)$. No calculator.

The natural move is the hour after $\displaystyle t=2$:

$$P'(2) \;\approx\; \frac{447 - 271}{3 - 2} \;=\; 176 \text{ bacteria per hour}$$

But the hour before is just as fair, and it gives something else:

$$P'(2) \;\approx\; \frac{271 - 165}{2 - 1} \;=\; 106 \text{ bacteria per hour}$$

Two estimates of one number, seventy apart, both reading the table correctly. So at least one of them is poor — and so far nothing says which.

Your turn

The tangent at $\displaystyle t=2$ sits between those two lines. With the same table and no new measurements, find an estimate that beats both.

Take the interval centered on $\displaystyle t=2$ — one hour each way:

$$P'(2) \;\approx\; \frac{447 - 165}{3 - 1} \;=\; \frac{282}{2} \;=\; 141 \text{ bacteria per hour}$$

For equally spaced data this is exactly the average of the two one-sided estimates: $\displaystyle (176 + 106)/2 = 141$. One of them is too steep and the other too shallow by roughly the same amount, so centering cancels most of the error instead of committing to a side.

This table is $\displaystyle P(t) = 100e^{t/2}$ rounded to whole bacteria, so the rate it was built from is $\displaystyle P'(2) = 50e = 135.9$:

With a table you cannot take a limit — so take the difference quotient over the interval centered on the point. The two one-sided estimates bracket the answer; the centered one splits the difference.

Order Them Without Computing Anything

Your turn

Put these five in order, largest first. No formula is given, and you do not need one: $\displaystyle f'(a)$, $\displaystyle f'(b)$, $\displaystyle f'(c)$, $\displaystyle m_{PQ}$, $\displaystyle m_{QR}$.

$$f'(b) \;>\; m_{PQ} \;>\; m_{QR} \;>\; f'(a) \;>\; f'(c)$$

The curve is steepest at $\displaystyle Q$, so $\displaystyle f'(b)$ wins. Each secant slope is an average of the steepnesses along its interval, so it lands between the ends. And the curve is flatter at $\displaystyle R$ than at $\displaystyle P$ — it has leveled off more on the right than it had risen on the left.

Two Errors Worth Recognizing

Work for the same $\displaystyle f(x) = x^{2} - 3x$, finding $\displaystyle f'(2)$. Every line follows from the one above by a move the student believed was legal. It comes out $\displaystyle -3$. It should be $\displaystyle 1$.

(1)

$$f'(2) \;=\; \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\left[(2+h)^{2} - 3(2+h)\right] - (-2)}{h}$$

(2)

$$=\; \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{4 + 4h + h^{2} - 6 - 3h - 2}{h}$$

(3)

$$=\; \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{h^{2} + h - 4}{h}$$

(4)

$$=\; \lim_{h\to 0}\,(h + 1 - 4) \;=\; -3$$
Your turn

Two of those lines are wrong. Which two, and what was the move each time?

Line 2 — the minus sign. Subtracting $\displaystyle f(2) = -2$ means $\displaystyle -(-2) = +2$, not $\displaystyle -2$. The bracket was dropped and the sign went with it, so $\displaystyle 4 - 6 + 2 = 0$ became $\displaystyle 4 - 6 - 2 = -4$. The habit that prevents it: write $\displaystyle \left[\,f(2+h)\,\right] - \left[\,f(2)\,\right]$ with both brackets, and keep them until the moment you multiply out.

Line 4 — cancelling something that is not a factor. The step $\displaystyle \frac{h^{2}+h-4}{h} = h + 1 - 4$ divided $\displaystyle h$ out of $\displaystyle h^{2}$, out of $\displaystyle h$, and then out of $\displaystyle -4$ — which has no $\displaystyle h$ in it. You can only cancel a common factor, so factor first and the question answers itself: $\displaystyle h^{2}+h-4$ has no $\displaystyle h$ to take out.

Done correctly line 3 reads $\displaystyle \frac{h^{2}+h}{h}$, which does factor: $\displaystyle \frac{h(h+1)}{h} = h+1$, and substituting $\displaystyle h = 0$ gives $\displaystyle 1$.

A check that costs nothing. In a correct difference quotient every term in the numerator contains an $\displaystyle h$ — it has to, because the numerator is $\displaystyle f(a+h) - f(a)$, which is $\displaystyle 0$ when $\displaystyle h$ is $\displaystyle 0$. So a leftover constant is a receipt for a mistake further up. Do not cancel your way past it; go back and find it.

What You Should Be Able to Do

  • State the definition of $\displaystyle f'(a)$ in both forms, and say when each is the convenient one.
  • Compute $\displaystyle f'(a)$ from the definition, writing only true equalities and keeping $\displaystyle \lim$ on every line until you take it.
  • Write the equation of the tangent line at $\displaystyle x = a$ — slope and point.
  • Give the units of a derivative, and say what it means in one sentence someone outside this room would understand.
  • Estimate a derivative from a table, using a centered interval, and call it an estimate.

So far $\displaystyle f'(a)$ has been a number at one point. Next: let $\displaystyle a$ move, and the derivative becomes a function in its own right.

Before You Start the Exercises

Read this at home, before the homework. Four moves account for most of the marks lost on difference quotients — and three of them are the same mistake wearing different clothes.

You may split a product. You may not split a sum. When you are not sure whether a step is legal, put a number in and check. It takes three seconds, it settles it completely, and it works on steps nobody ever showed you.

1. Cancelling in a fraction. Seeing an $\displaystyle h$ above and an $\displaystyle h$ below is not enough.

$$\frac{h+4}{h} \;\neq\; 4, \qquad\text{but}\qquad \frac{h\cdot 4}{h} = 4$$

Only a common factor cancels, and $\displaystyle h+4$ is a sum, not a product. Test it: at $\displaystyle h=1$ the left side is $\displaystyle \frac{5}{1} = 5$, not $\displaystyle 4$.

So factor first, and then cancel what you find: $\displaystyle \frac{h^{2}+h}{h} = \frac{h(h+1)}{h} = h+1$.

2. The minus sign. Subtracting $\displaystyle f(a)$ subtracts all of it.

$$-(a-b) \;=\; -a+b, \qquad\text{not}\qquad -a-b$$

Test it: $\displaystyle -(3-5) = -(-2) = 2$, not $\displaystyle -8$.

The habit that prevents it: write $\displaystyle \left[\,f(a+h)\,\right] - \left[\,f(a)\,\right]$ with both brackets actually on the page, and do not drop them until you multiply out.

3. Roots. A root splits over a product and never over a sum.

$$\sqrt{x+4} \;\neq\; \sqrt{x}+2, \qquad\text{but}\qquad \sqrt{4x} = 2\sqrt{x}$$

Test it: $\displaystyle \sqrt{9+16} = \sqrt{25} = 5$, while $\displaystyle \sqrt9+\sqrt{16} = 7$.

This is the whole reason the conjugate exists. Nothing lets you break up $\displaystyle \sqrt{x+4}-2$, but multiplying by $\displaystyle \sqrt{x+4}+2$ does:

$$\left(\sqrt{A}-B\right)\left(\sqrt{A}+B\right) \;=\; A - B^{2}$$

and the root is gone, because you multiplied rather than split.

4. A fraction inside a fraction. This one is not an illegal move — it is a place people stop. Take $\displaystyle f(x) = \frac1x$ and find $\displaystyle f'(2)$:

$$\frac{f(x)-f(2)}{x-2} \;=\; \frac{\frac1x - \frac12}{x-2}$$

Most of the work is getting the top over a common denominator, and most people do it correctly and then stall:

$$\frac1x - \frac12 \;=\; \frac{2-x}{2x}$$

The step that is missing is seeing that the bar is a division. Write the bottom as a fraction and invert it:

$$\frac{\frac{2-x}{2x}}{x-2} \;=\; \frac{2-x}{2x} \div \frac{x-2}{1} \;=\; \frac{2-x}{2x}\cdot\frac{1}{x-2}$$

Now finish, using $\displaystyle 2-x = -(x-2)$:

$$\frac{-(x-2)}{2x(x-2)} \;=\; -\frac{1}{2x}, \qquad\text{so } f'(2) = -\frac14$$

Those four cover it. If a step you are unsure of is not on this list, put a number in — that is the move this page is really teaching.

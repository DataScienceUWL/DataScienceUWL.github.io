Do the Hypotheses Hold? Three in Three Minutes

The Mean Value Theorem needs both of these, and they are not the same interval:

  • $\displaystyle f$ continuous on the closed interval $\displaystyle [a,b]$
  • $\displaystyle f$ differentiable on the open interval $\displaystyle (a,b)$
Your turn

For each one, does the MVT apply? One sentence of reason each — that sentence is the exam answer. (a) $\displaystyle f(x) = x^{3}+x$ on $\displaystyle [-1,2]$   (b) $\displaystyle f(x) = \dfrac{1}{x}$ on $\displaystyle [-1,1]$   (c) $\displaystyle f(x) = \left|x-2\right|$ on $\displaystyle [0,3]$

Show: all three, with the sentence that earns the point

(a) Yes. A polynomial is continuous everywhere, so it is continuous on $\displaystyle [-1,2]$; and differentiable everywhere, so differentiable on $\displaystyle (-1,2)$. (b) No. $\displaystyle f$ is undefined at $\displaystyle 0$, and $\displaystyle 0$ is inside the interval, so $\displaystyle f$ is not continuous on $\displaystyle [-1,1]$. The first hypothesis fails. (c) No. The absolute value is continuous everywhere, so that hypothesis is fine — but there is a corner at $\displaystyle x=2$, which is inside $\displaystyle (0,3)$, so $\displaystyle f'(2)$ does not exist. The second hypothesis fails.

Notice (c). Saying “it is not differentiable” is only half an answer — the credit is in naming which hypothesis fails and where. And notice that a bad point at an endpoint would not matter at all, because differentiability is only required on the open interval.

The MVT, All the Way Through

Your turn

Verify that $\displaystyle f(x) = \sqrt{x}$ satisfies the hypotheses of the MVT on $\displaystyle [0,4]$, and find all numbers $\displaystyle c$ the conclusion promises.

Show: step 1 — the hypotheses, and why this one is interesting

$\displaystyle f(x)=\sqrt{x}$ is continuous on $\displaystyle [0,4]$. Its derivative is $\displaystyle f'(x) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}$, which does not exist at $\displaystyle x=0$ — but $\displaystyle 0$ is an endpoint, and differentiability is only required on the open interval $\displaystyle (0,4)$. So both hypotheses hold.

Show: step 2 — the secant slope, which is the right-hand side
$$\frac{f(4)-f(0)}{4-0} = \frac{2-0}{4} = \frac{1}{2}$$
Show: step 3 — set $\displaystyle f'(c)$ equal to it and solve
$$\frac{1}{2\sqrt{c}} = \frac{1}{2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \sqrt{c} = 1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad c = 1$$

and $\displaystyle c=1$ is in $\displaystyle (0,4)$, so it counts. There is exactly one: $\displaystyle c=1$.

Two habits worth carrying in. The secant slope is a number — compute it first and it stops being frightening. And always check the $\displaystyle c$ you find is actually inside the open interval: the theorem promises nothing outside it, and an answer outside it is wrong even if the algebra was right.

Absolute Extrema: The Table Is the Method

Four steps, every time: critical numbers in $\displaystyle [a,b]$; a table of $\displaystyle x$ and $\displaystyle f(x)$ with the endpoints in it; the Extreme Value Theorem says the answers are in that table; write them in sentences.

Your turn

Find the absolute maximum and minimum of

$$f(x) = x^{4} - 2x^{2} + 3 \quad\text{on}\quad [-2,2]$$

All four steps. Write the answers in sentences.

Show: step 1 — critical numbers
$$f'(x) = 4x^{3} - 4x = 4x\left(x^{2}-1\right) = 4x\left(x-1\right)\left(x+1\right)$$
$$c = -1,\quad c = 0,\quad c = 1$$

$\displaystyle f'$ exists everywhere, so that is all of them — and all three are inside $\displaystyle [-2,2]$, so none gets discarded.

Show: step 2 — the table, endpoints included
Five candidates: three critical numbers and both endpoints
$\displaystyle x$$\displaystyle f(x)$why it is on the list
$\displaystyle -2$$\displaystyle 11$left endpoint
$\displaystyle -1$$\displaystyle 2$critical number
$\displaystyle 0$$\displaystyle 3$critical number
$\displaystyle 1$$\displaystyle 2$critical number
$\displaystyle 2$$\displaystyle 11$right endpoint
Show: steps 3 and 4 — compare, then say it properly

$\displaystyle f$ has an absolute maximum value of $\displaystyle 11$, at $\displaystyle x=-2$ and $\displaystyle x=2$; and an absolute minimum value of $\displaystyle 2$, at $\displaystyle x=-1$ and $\displaystyle x=1$.

The value is unique; the location need not be. There is one largest value, $\displaystyle 11$ — but it happens at two different places, and both belong in the answer. This is exactly why the sentence has to carry a $\displaystyle y$ and an $\displaystyle x$: “the maximum is $\displaystyle 11$” does not tell me where, and “the maximum is at $\displaystyle x=2$” leaves out the other place it happens.

Related Rates: Just the First Line

Every related-rates problem turns on one decision made before any calculus happens: which relation do you write down, and does it have a second changing length in it that has to go?

Your turn

Four setups. For each, say only two things — what relation you write first, and whether a second changing length has to be eliminated before you differentiate. Do not work any of them out. (a) A spherical balloon is inflated. How fast is the radius growing? (b) A kite stays $\displaystyle 100$ ft above the ground and drifts horizontally. How fast is the string paying out? (c) A trough whose cross-section is a triangle, narrow at the bottom, is filling with water. How fast is the depth rising? (d) A rocket rises straight up; an observer stands a fixed distance away. How fast is the angle of elevation changing?

Show: what each one needs, and which one is different

(a) $\displaystyle V = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^{3}$. Two changing quantities, $\displaystyle V$ and $\displaystyle r$; one equation. Complete — nothing to eliminate. (b) Pythagoras. Two changing quantities — the horizontal distance and the string — and one equation, since the $\displaystyle 100$ is a constant and not a variable. Complete. (c) Volume $\displaystyle =$ cross-sectional area $\displaystyle \times$ length, and the triangle's width grows as the depth grows. So the changing quantities are the volume, the width and the depth — three — against one equation. One short. Similar triangles supply the missing relation between width and depth, and that substitution happens before you differentiate. (d) A trigonometric ratio: $\displaystyle \tan\theta = \frac{\text{height}}{\text{distance}}$. Two changing quantities, the angle and the height, and one equation — the distance is constant. Complete.

Only one of those four needs a second relation, and there is a count that decides it every time: you need one fewer equation than you have changing quantities. $\displaystyle V = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^{3}$ has two changing quantities, $\displaystyle V$ and $\displaystyle r$, and one equation. $\displaystyle 2-1=1$, so it is complete — give me one rate and the other is determined. $\displaystyle V = \frac{1}{3}\pi r^{2}h$ has three, $\displaystyle V$ and $\displaystyle r$ and $\displaystyle h$, and still only one equation. You are one short, so go and find a second relation — and it will almost always be similar triangles, read off the picture. So the question is never “are there two letters”. It is do I have one fewer equation than I have changing quantities.

Why one fewer, and not the same number? Every equation you write removes a degree of freedom. With as many equations as quantities nothing could move at all. Leaving exactly one free is what lets the problem hand you a single rate and have everything else follow — which is precisely what a related-rates problem does.

One more thing that costs people the problem: a relation that is true at every instant goes in early; a value true at one instant goes in last. Substitute the one-instant number before you differentiate and you have frozen the quantity, and its derivative is zero.

Derive One You Have Not Been Asked For

Fourteen points ask you to derive a rule rather than use it. The two on the paper are not these two — but the moves are the same, and these are the two Quiz 6 said had not landed.

Your turn

Derive $\displaystyle \dfrac{d}{dx}\tan x$, using only the rules for sine and cosine.

Show: a quotient, and then one rearrangement
$$\tan x = \frac{\sin x}{\cos x} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{d}{dx}\tan x = \frac{\left(\cos x\right)\left(\cos x\right) - \left(\sin x\right)\left(-\sin x\right)}{\cos^{2}x}$$
$$= \frac{\cos^{2}x + \sin^{2}x}{\cos^{2}x} = \frac{1}{\cos^{2}x} = \sec^{2}x$$

The Pythagorean identity is doing the last step. Without it you stop one line short.

Your turn

Now derive $\displaystyle \dfrac{d}{dx}\sin^{-1}x$, using the trig derivatives but no inverse-trig rule.

Show: set it up so the inverse disappears

Let $\displaystyle y = \sin^{-1}x$. Take the sine of both sides to get rid of the inverse:

$$\sin y = x$$

Differentiate both sides with respect to $\displaystyle x$ — Chain Rule on the left:

$$\cos y\cdot\frac{dy}{dx} = 1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{1}{\cos y}$$
Show: now turn the $\displaystyle y$ back into an $\displaystyle x$

Draw the triangle: $\displaystyle \sin y = x$ means opposite $\displaystyle x$, hypotenuse $\displaystyle 1$, so the adjacent side is $\displaystyle \sqrt{1-x^{2}}$ and $\displaystyle \cos y = \sqrt{1-x^{2}}$.

$$\frac{d}{dx}\sin^{-1}x = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}$$

Every one of these derivations has the same three beats: rewrite so you can differentiate (a quotient, or take sine of both sides), differentiate, then convert back so no $\displaystyle y$ survives in the answer. An answer still containing $\displaystyle y$ is not finished.

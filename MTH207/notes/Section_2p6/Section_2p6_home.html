2.6  Limits at Infinity and Horizontal Asymptotes

MTH 207 Calculus I — Limits and Derivatives

Let me try the examples first →

A Different Question About the Same Graph

Every limit so far has asked the same kind of question: what happens near a point?

$$\lim_{x\to 2} f(x), \qquad \lim_{x\to 3^-} f(x), \qquad \lim_{x\to 0}\frac{1}{x^2}$$

Today the question changes. Not near a point — far out.

$$\lim_{x\to\infty} f(x) \qquad\text{and}\qquad \lim_{x\to-\infty} f(x)$$

"$\displaystyle x\to\infty$" is not a point we approach. It means: let $\displaystyle x$ grow without bound and watch what the outputs do.

The One Limit Everything Rests On

My turn

What happens to $\displaystyle \dfrac{1}{x}$ as $\displaystyle x$ grows?

Watch the values. $\displaystyle x = 10 \Rightarrow 0.1$.   $\displaystyle x = 1000 \Rightarrow 0.001$.   $\displaystyle x = 10^6 \Rightarrow 0.000001$.

The numerator stays at $\displaystyle 1$ and the denominator runs away, so the quotient is squeezed toward $\displaystyle 0$ — and never gets there.

$$\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{1}{x} = 0 \qquad\text{and}\qquad \lim_{x\to-\infty}\frac{1}{x} = 0$$

Same reasoning as Sep 14, run the other way. There it was number over small number is large. Here it is number over large number is small.

Now You

Your turn

Evaluate each. No calculator — name the size of the numerator and the denominator and read off the answer.

  1. $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{1}{x^2}$
  2. $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{5}{x^3}$
  3. $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to-\infty}\frac{1}{x^5}$
  4. $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}}$

Every one is $\displaystyle 0$ — a constant on top, something growing without bound underneath. Written out, the first one is

$$\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{1}{x^2} \;=\; 0$$

and the other three say the same thing with a different power.

$\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\frac{1}{x^{\,n}} = 0$ for every $\displaystyle n > 0$. This single fact does all the work in this section.

Saying It Precisely

Definition — Limit at infinity

$\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty} f(x) = L$ means the values of $\displaystyle f(x)$ can be made as close to $\displaystyle L$ as we like by taking $\displaystyle x$ large enough.

Definition — Horizontal asymptote

The line $\displaystyle y = L$ is a horizontal asymptote of $\displaystyle y = f(x)$ if either

$$\lim_{x\to\infty} f(x) = L \qquad\text{or}\qquad \lim_{x\to-\infty} f(x) = L$$

Read that "either" exactly. One direction is enough to earn an asymptote — which is why a graph can have two of them, one at each end.

Rational Functions: One Move

Substituting is useless here — $\displaystyle \infty/\infty$ is not a number, and it is not an answer.

My turn

$\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{3x^2 - x + 2}{5x^2 + 4}$

$\displaystyle \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{3x^2 - x + 2}{5x^2 + 4}$$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\dfrac{3x^2}{x^2} - \dfrac{x}{x^2} + \dfrac{2}{x^2}}{\dfrac{5x^2}{x^2} + \dfrac{4}{x^2}}$divide by $\displaystyle x^2$; legal since $\displaystyle x \neq 0$ out here
$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{3 - \dfrac{1}{x} + \dfrac{2}{x^2}}{5 + \dfrac{4}{x^2}}$simplify each piece
$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle \frac{3 - 0 + 0}{5 + 0}$each $\displaystyle \lim 1/x^{\,n} = 0$; the denominator's limit is $\displaystyle 5 \neq 0$
$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle \frac{3}{5}$arithmetic

Why the denominator's highest power? Because that is what forces the denominator to a finite nonzero number — and only then is the Quotient Law available at all.

Look at where $\displaystyle \lim$ appears: on every line until the values are actually put in, and then not at all. That is not decoration. Until you take the limit, each line is a function of $\displaystyle x$; the moment you drop the symbol you have written that a function equals a number, which is false. Write the working the way it is written here — on a quiz the symbols are part of the answer.

Three of Them

Day 10 — Wed Sep 23

Your turn

Same move on each. Divide by the highest power in the denominator.

  1. $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{2x+1}{x^2+1}$
  2. $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{3x^2-1}{2x^2+5}$
  3. $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{x^3}{x^2+1}$

$$\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{2x+1}{x^2+1} \;=\; \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\frac{2}{x} + \frac{1}{x^2}}{1 + \frac{1}{x^2}} \;=\; \frac{0+0}{1+0} \;=\; 0$$
$$\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{3x^2-1}{2x^2+5} \;=\; \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{3 - \frac{1}{x^2}}{2 + \frac{5}{x^2}} \;=\; \frac{3-0}{2+0} \;=\; \frac32$$
$$\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{x^3}{x^2+1} \;=\; \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{x}{1 + \frac{1}{x^2}} \;=\; \infty$$

The numerator grows without bound while the denominator tends to $\displaystyle 1$, so the limit does not exist. The last line is not a number — writing $\displaystyle =\infty$ records how it fails.

Three Cases, One Rule

Your turn

You just computed all three. What decides which case you land in?

Compare the degrees of numerator and denominator.

  • Degree on top smaller — limit is $\displaystyle 0$. Asymptote $\displaystyle y = 0$.
  • Degrees equal — limit is the ratio of the leading coefficients. Asymptote $\displaystyle y = a/b$.
  • Degree on top larger — no horizontal asymptote; the values run off without bound.

This is a shortcut for reading off the answer, not a substitute for the work. On an exam that asks you to justify, divide.

Roots: Dividing by the Highest Power

The move is the same as before — divide by the highest power in the denominator. The only new question is how to get $\displaystyle x$ inside a square root.

My turn

$\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{3x}{\sqrt{x^2+1}}$

Definition — The one fact this rests on
$$|x| \;=\; \sqrt{x^{2}} \;=\; \begin{cases} \phantom{-}x, & x \ge 0\\[3pt] -x, & x < 0 \end{cases}$$

The radical sign returns the non-negative root, which is why $\displaystyle \sqrt{x^2}$ is $\displaystyle |x|$ and not $\displaystyle x$: $\displaystyle \sqrt{(-5)^{2}} = \sqrt{25} = 5$, not $\displaystyle -5$.

Now turn it around. It is $\displaystyle x$ we are dividing by, so solve each line of that definition for $\displaystyle x$:

$$x \;=\; \begin{cases} \phantom{-}\sqrt{x^{2}}, & x \ge 0\\[3pt] -\sqrt{x^{2}}, & x < 0 \end{cases}$$

That is the form you substitute.

So dividing the denominator by $\displaystyle x$ means dividing inside the root by $\displaystyle x^{2}$ — and which line of that piecewise definition you use is decided entirely by which end you are heading for.

$\displaystyle \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{3x}{\sqrt{x^2+1}}$$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\dfrac{3x}{x}}{\dfrac{\sqrt{x^2+1}}{x}}$divide top and bottom by $\displaystyle x$
$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{3}{\dfrac{\sqrt{x^2+1}}{\sqrt{x^2}}}$$\displaystyle x=\sqrt{x^2}$, valid because $\displaystyle x>0$ out here
$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{3}{\sqrt{\dfrac{x^2+1}{x^2}}}$one root over another is the root of the quotient
$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{3}{\sqrt{1 + \dfrac{1}{x^2}}}$divide inside
$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle \frac{3}{\sqrt{1+0}} = 3$$\displaystyle \lim 1/x^2 = 0$; the root is continuous at $\displaystyle 1$

The Trap: $\sqrt{x^2}$ Is Not $x$

Your turn

Same function, other direction: $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to-\infty}\frac{3x}{\sqrt{x^2+1}}$. Exactly one line of the work above changes.

Line 3, the only one that carried a hypothesis. Out at $\displaystyle -\infty$ the variable $\displaystyle x$ is negative, so the second line of the piecewise definition applies:

$$x = -\sqrt{x^{2}} \qquad (x < 0)$$

Everything else in the chain is untouched.

$\displaystyle \lim_{x\to-\infty}\frac{3x}{\sqrt{x^2+1}}$$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle \lim_{x\to-\infty}\frac{\dfrac{3x}{x}}{\dfrac{\sqrt{x^2+1}}{x}}$divide top and bottom by $\displaystyle x$ — same as before
$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle \lim_{x\to-\infty}\frac{3}{\dfrac{\sqrt{x^2+1}}{-\sqrt{x^2}}}$here: $\displaystyle x=-\sqrt{x^2}$, because $\displaystyle x<0$ out here
$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle \lim_{x\to-\infty}\frac{3}{-\dfrac{\sqrt{x^2+1}}{\sqrt{x^2}}}$the minus sign comes out of the fraction
$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle \lim_{x\to-\infty}\frac{3}{-\sqrt{\dfrac{x^2+1}{x^2}}}$one root over another is the root of the quotient
$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle \lim_{x\to-\infty}\frac{3}{-\sqrt{1 + \dfrac{1}{x^2}}}$divide inside
$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle \frac{3}{-\sqrt{1+0}} = -3$$\displaystyle \lim 1/x^2 = 0$

Compare the two chains line by line. Only line 3 differs, and the minus sign it introduces is carried all the way to the answer. That is the entire difference between $\displaystyle 3$ and $\displaystyle -3$.

Two different horizontal asymptotes, from one function. The definition said either direction — this is what that word was protecting. And the sign came from one hypothesis on one line, which is why it is worth writing that line down every time.

What a Horizontal Asymptote Does Not Promise

Your turn

True or false, and say why. (a) A graph can never cross its horizontal asymptote. (b) If $\displaystyle f$ has no horizontal asymptote, then $\displaystyle f(x)$ must run off to $\displaystyle \pm\infty$.

$\displaystyle \dfrac{\sin x}{x}$ crosses $\displaystyle y = 0$ at every multiple of $\displaystyle \pi$ — infinitely often — and still approaches it. "Asymptote" constrains the tail behavior, not any particular point.

$\displaystyle \sin x$ stays between $\displaystyle -1$ and $\displaystyle 1$ forever and approaches nothing. Bounded, but no limit.

Your Calculator Will Lie to You Here

My turn

$\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty} \ln(\ln x)$ — let us check it numerically.

Plugging in large numbers. $\displaystyle x = 10^{12}$ gives about $\displaystyle 3.32$, and $\displaystyle x = 10^{14}$ gives about $\displaystyle 3.48$. It looks like it is settling down.

It is not settling. In fact $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\ln(\ln x) = \infty$. It just does it so slowly that no calculator you can type into will ever show you.

A table of values is evidence, never a proof. It samples finitely many points and says nothing about the rest.

What You Should Be Able to Do

  • Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\frac{1}{x^{\,n}}$ and use it without stopping to think.
  • Find a limit at infinity of a rational function by dividing by the highest power in the denominator, and say why that is the power to choose.
  • Read off which of the three degree cases you are in — and still show the division when asked to justify.
  • Handle $\displaystyle \sqrt{x^2} = |x|$ correctly as $\displaystyle x\to-\infty$, and explain why the two tails can settle at different heights.
  • Say what a horizontal asymptote does not promise: not that the graph stays off it, and not that its absence means the values blow up.

4.5  Summary of Curve Sketching

MTH 207 Calculus I — Applications of Differentiation

Everything You Know, in One Order

A graphing calculator draws curves. What it cannot do is tell you why the curve looks like that, or warn you that the window you chose is hiding a feature. This section is the systematic version.

Look at the right-hand column. There is nothing new in this section. Three steps are algebra you have had since precalculus, one is §2.6, and three are last week. What §4.5 adds is the order — and the discipline of doing all seven before drawing anything.

Step 3: Even, Odd, or Neither

You met this in precalculus and have not needed it since. It is worth two minutes because it is the cheapest step on the list — a line of algebra that can halve the work in steps 5, 6 and 7.

Definition — Even and Odd Functions

$\displaystyle f$ is even if $\displaystyle f(-x) = f(x)$ — the graph is a mirror image across the $\displaystyle y$-axis. $\displaystyle f$ is odd if $\displaystyle f(-x) = -f(x)$ — a half-turn about the origin maps the graph onto itself.

The fast test for a polynomial or rational function: every exponent even $\displaystyle \Rightarrow$ even, every exponent odd $\displaystyle \Rightarrow$ odd, a mixture $\displaystyle \Rightarrow$ neither. A constant term is $\displaystyle x^{0}$, so it counts as an even power.

Why bother? Because an even or odd function only has to be analysed on half the line. Work out the extrema, concavity and asymptotes for $\displaystyle x \ge 0$ and the rest is a mirror. On a 20-point exam question that is real time — which is why this is step 3 and not step 7.

Your turn

Even, odd, or neither? (a) $\displaystyle x^{4}-4x^{2}$   (b) $\displaystyle \frac{x^{3}}{x^{4}+1}$   (c) $\displaystyle x^{2}e^{-x}$   (d) $\displaystyle \frac{x^{2}}{x^{2}-1}$

Show: (a) even : exponents 4 and 2…

(a) even: exponents $\displaystyle 4$ and $\displaystyle 2$. (b) odd: the top flips sign, the bottom does not. (c) neither: $\displaystyle e^{-x}$ has no symmetry at all. (d) even: every power of $\displaystyle x$ is even. The quick test — all exponents even, all odd, or mixed — covers polynomials and most rational functions, which is most of what you will meet. An exponential breaks it, and (c) is the reminder to look rather than apply the rule blindly.

Step 4: The One Piece §4.3 Never Needed

Steps 5, 6 and 7 are last week exactly. Step 4 is the piece we have not used since Chapter 2.

Definition — Vertical and Horizontal Asymptotes

The line $\displaystyle x = a$ is a vertical asymptote if $\displaystyle f(x) \to \pm\infty$ as $\displaystyle x \to a$ from either side. The line $\displaystyle y = L$ is a horizontal asymptote if $\displaystyle \lim_{x \to \infty} f(x) = L$ or $\displaystyle \lim_{x \to -\infty} f(x) = L$.

For a rational function, a vertical asymptote is where the denominator is zero and the numerator is not. If both vanish there is a common factor to cancel first — and what is left may be a hole, not an asymptote.

A horizontal asymptote is just a limit at infinity, which means you already have two ways to find one: compare degrees the way we did in §2.6, or — when that fails — use l'Hospital's Rule from yesterday. That is why §4.4 came first.

⚠ “Horizontal asymptote” is about the ends of the graph, and the two ends are separate questions. A curve can have one on the right and nothing on the left, and it is allowed to cross a horizontal asymptote in the middle — asymptotes are a statement about limits, not a fence.

My Turn: A Rational Function, All Seven Steps

My turn

Sketch $\displaystyle f(x) = \frac{x^{2}}{x^{2}-1}$.

Show: steps 1–3 — the algebra, and it is quick

Domain: $\displaystyle x^{2}-1 = 0$ at $\displaystyle x = \pm 1$, so the domain is everything except $\displaystyle \pm 1$. Intercepts: $\displaystyle f(0) = 0$, and $\displaystyle f(x) = 0$ only when $\displaystyle x^{2}=0$. So the only intercept is the origin. Symmetry: $\displaystyle f(-x) = \frac{x^{2}}{x^{2}-1} = f(x)$, so $\displaystyle f$ is even — whatever happens on the right is mirrored on the left.

Show: step 4 — the asymptotes

Vertical: the denominator vanishes at $\displaystyle x = \pm 1$ and the numerator does not, so both are vertical asymptotes. Horizontal: divide top and bottom by $\displaystyle x^{2}$:

$$\lim_{x \to \pm\infty} \frac{x^{2}}{x^{2}-1} = \lim_{x \to \pm\infty} \frac{1}{1 - 1/x^{2}} = 1$$

so $\displaystyle y=1$ is a horizontal asymptote in both directions.

Show: steps 5, 6, 7 — the two sign charts and the shape line
$$f'(x) = \frac{-2x}{\left(x^{2}-1\right)^{2}} \qquad\qquad f''(x) = \frac{2\left(3x^{2}+1\right)}{\left(x^{2}-1\right)^{3}}$$

Each denominator is a power of $\displaystyle x^{2}-1$, so neither derivative exists at $\displaystyle \pm 1$. Above that line: $\displaystyle f'$ has the sign of $\displaystyle -2x$, and $\displaystyle f''$ has the sign of $\displaystyle (x^{2}-1)^{3}$, because $\displaystyle 3x^{2}+1$ is never zero.

Read down a column: $\displaystyle x=0$ is the only critical number and $\displaystyle f'$ turns $\displaystyle +$ to $\displaystyle -$ there, a local maximum at $\displaystyle (0,0)$. And there are no inflection points at all — the concavity does change at $\displaystyle \pm1$, but $\displaystyle f$ is not defined there, so those are not points on the curve.

Show: the sketch

⚠ The surprise in step 7 is the one to remember. A change in concavity is only an inflection point if the function is actually there. This is the same mistake as $\displaystyle f''(c)=0$ implying an inflection point, wearing different clothes — and it is why the domain is step 1.

Your Turn: When Step 1 Does Real Work

Every function so far has been defined almost everywhere, so step 1 was a formality. Not this one.

Your turn

Sketch $\displaystyle f(x) = x\sqrt{4-x^{2}}$. Work all seven steps in order. You may use

$$f''(x) = \frac{2x\left(x^{2}-6\right)}{\left(4-x^{2}\right)^{3/2}}$$

but find $\displaystyle f'$ yourself.

Show: steps 1–4 — and step 1 is the whole game

Domain: we need $\displaystyle 4-x^{2} \ge 0$, so $\displaystyle x^{2} \le 4$ and the domain is $\displaystyle \mathbf{[-2,\,2]}$ — closed and bounded. Intercepts: $\displaystyle f(x)=0$ when $\displaystyle x=0$ or $\displaystyle x=\pm2$. Symmetry: $\displaystyle f(-x) = -x\sqrt{4-x^{2}} = -f(x)$, so $\displaystyle f$ is odd — work out the right half and turn it round. Asymptotes: none. There is no infinity to run to; the domain stops at $\displaystyle \pm2$.

Show: steps 5, 6, 7 — the two sign charts and the shape line

Write the root as a power and factor out the smaller exponent:

$$f'(x) = \left(4-x^{2}\right)^{1/2} - x^{2}\left(4-x^{2}\right)^{-1/2} = \left(4-x^{2}\right)^{-1/2}\left[\left(4-x^{2}\right)-x^{2}\right] = \frac{2\left(2-x^{2}\right)}{\sqrt{4-x^{2}}}$$

⚠ Read the denominator. $\displaystyle \sqrt{4-x^{2}}$ is zero at $\displaystyle x=\pm2$, so $\displaystyle f'$ does not exist at the ends of the domain. As $\displaystyle x \to 2^{-}$ the top tends to $\displaystyle -4$ and the bottom to $\displaystyle 0$, so $\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 2^{-}} f'(x) = -\infty$. Hold on to that — it is not one of the seven steps, and it is the one thing that will decide whether your picture is right.

Show: the two traps, and the sketch

Trap 1. $\displaystyle f''=0$ at $\displaystyle x=0$ and at $\displaystyle x = \pm\sqrt{6} \approx \pm2.449$ — but those are not in the domain. The only inflection point is $\displaystyle (0,0)$. Trap 2, and it is not on the list at all. The seven steps never ask what $\displaystyle f'$ does at the ends — only where it is zero. But $\displaystyle f'$ runs off to $\displaystyle -\infty$ at $\displaystyle x=\pm2$, so the curve meets the $\displaystyle x$-axis with a vertical tangent instead of easing into it.

⚠ This is why it matters. The dashed curve has the same intercepts, the same symmetry, the same four shapes in the same order, the same single inflection point at the origin, and peaks at exactly $\displaystyle \pm2$ — everything you would check by eye. It is a cubic, and it is what you will draw if you never look at $\displaystyle f'$ at the ends. It is not a perfect match: a cubic turns at $\displaystyle \pm\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}} \approx \pm1.155$ rather than at $\displaystyle \pm\sqrt{2} \approx \pm1.414$, and it does not stop at $\displaystyle \pm2$ — it keeps going. But nobody catches that freehand. The ends are what give it away.

Look at the shape line: all four shapes, once each — the §4.3 alphabet in a single function. Then add the thing the list does not ask for: whenever the domain stops, check $\displaystyle f'$ at the ends. If $\displaystyle f'$ runs off to $\displaystyle \pm\infty$ the curve arrives vertically; if $\displaystyle f'$ is finite it arrives at a slant. Steps 1–7 fix where the curve goes; the endpoints fix how it gets there, and a sketch that ignores them is a cubic in disguise.

Where We Are

  • Seven steps, in order, before you draw anything.
  • Vertical asymptotes come from the domain; horizontal ones from limits at infinity, which is §2.6 or l'Hospital.
  • A concavity change is only an inflection point if $\displaystyle f$ is defined there.

Tomorrow: a function whose asymptote is one-sided, and then the exact shape Exam 3 asks for.

My Turn: An Asymptote on One Side Only

My turn

Sketch $\displaystyle f(x) = x^{2}e^{-x}$.

Show: steps 1–4

Domain: all real numbers. Intercepts: $\displaystyle f(0)=0$, and $\displaystyle x^{2}e^{-x} = 0$ only at $\displaystyle x=0$, since an exponential is never zero. Symmetry: neither. Asymptotes: no denominator, so no vertical ones. For horizontal, take both ends separately:

$$\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{x^{2}}{e^{x}} = 0 \qquad \lim_{x \to -\infty} x^{2}e^{-x} = \infty$$

The first is $\displaystyle \frac{\infty}{\infty}$ and needs l'Hospital twice — §4.4's work. So $\displaystyle y=0$ is a horizontal asymptote to the right only.

Show: steps 5, 6, 7 — the two sign charts and the shape line
$$f'(x) = 2xe^{-x} - x^{2}e^{-x} = -x\left(x-2\right)e^{-x} \qquad f''(x) = \left(x^{2}-4x+2\right)e^{-x}$$

$\displaystyle e^{-x}$ is always positive, so it never decides a sign: $\displaystyle f'$ follows $\displaystyle -x(x-2)$, and $\displaystyle f''$ follows $\displaystyle x^{2}-4x+2$, which is zero at $\displaystyle x = 2 \pm \sqrt{2}$.

Local minimum at $\displaystyle (0,0)$, local maximum at $\displaystyle \left(2,\,4e^{-2}\right) \approx (2,\,0.54)$, and inflection points at both of $\displaystyle 2 \pm \sqrt{2}$ — the f $\displaystyle ''$ cuts sit either side of the maximum, which is why the peak looks rounded rather than pointed.

Show: the sketch

Two things this function teaches that a polynomial cannot. The asymptote exists on one side only — the two ends are separate questions, every time. And the curve approaches $\displaystyle y=0$ without ever reaching it for $\displaystyle x>0$, while sitting on it at $\displaystyle x=0$: touching an asymptote is allowed.

Your Turn: Exactly What Exam 3 Asks

Exam 3's biggest question gives you the function and both derivatives, then asks for the four readings and a sketch. Here is that shape.

Your turn

Let $\displaystyle f(x) = \frac{x^{2}-4}{x^{2}+4}$. You are given

$$f'(x) = \frac{16x}{\left(x^{2}+4\right)^{2}} \qquad f''(x) = \frac{-16\left(3x^{2}-4\right)}{\left(x^{2}+4\right)^{3}}$$

(a) domain, intercepts and symmetry; (b) asymptotes; (c) increase, decrease and extrema; (d) concavity and inflection points; then sketch.

Show: (a) and (b)

Domain: $\displaystyle x^{2}+4$ is never zero, so all real numbers — and therefore no vertical asymptotes at all. Intercepts: $\displaystyle f(0) = -1$, and $\displaystyle f(x)=0$ when $\displaystyle x^{2}=4$, so $\displaystyle x=\pm2$. Symmetry: every power is even, so $\displaystyle f$ is even. Horizontal: divide by $\displaystyle x^{2}$ — $\displaystyle \lim_{x \to \pm\infty} f(x) = 1$, so $\displaystyle y=1$ both ways. Note the curve stays below $\displaystyle y=1$ the whole time, because the numerator is always 8 less than the denominator.

Show: (c) increase, decrease, extrema

The denominator of $\displaystyle f'$ is a square, so the sign of $\displaystyle f'$ is the sign of $\displaystyle 16x$: negative for $\displaystyle x<0$, positive for $\displaystyle x>0$. Decreasing on $\displaystyle (-\infty,0)$, increasing on $\displaystyle (0,\infty)$, and the only critical number is $\displaystyle x=0$, where $\displaystyle f'$ turns $\displaystyle -$ to $\displaystyle +$: a local minimum at $\displaystyle (0,-1)$. By the symmetry, that is also the absolute minimum.

Show: (d) concavity and inflection

The denominator of $\displaystyle f''$ is positive, so the sign of $\displaystyle f''$ is the sign of $\displaystyle -16(3x^{2}-4)$, which is positive when $\displaystyle 3x^{2} < 4$, i.e. when $\displaystyle |x| < \frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}$.

$$\text{concave up on } \left(-\tfrac{2}{\sqrt{3}},\ \tfrac{2}{\sqrt{3}}\right); \quad \text{concave down outside it}$$

The sign changes at both, so there are inflection points at $\displaystyle x = \pm\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}$, where $\displaystyle f = -\frac{1}{2}$.

Show: (c) and (d) on three lines — then the sketch draws itself

The shape line is already the sketch — four arcs in order, and all part (d) of the exam asks is that you draw them joined up with the asymptote. Notice it is symmetric about the $\displaystyle y$-axis, exactly as step 3 promised: on an even function the right half is all you ever had to work out.

Show: the sketch

Count what you actually did: four sign arguments and two limits. No Quotient Rule, because the derivatives were handed to you — and on Exam 3 they will be. The 20 points are for assembling, so practise assembling.

For Reference: A Fractional Exponent, and a Cusp

Not worked in class — here so you have one of each shape to copy from. This one is the §4.3 algebra you have the Simplifying Derivatives handout for, and it produces a shape the examples above never did.

Show: $\displaystyle f(x) = x^{2/3}(x-5)$ — steps 1–4

Domain: all real numbers — a cube root accepts negatives. Intercepts: $\displaystyle f(0)=0$ and $\displaystyle f(5)=0$. Symmetry: neither. Asymptotes: none; it is a power function times a polynomial.

Show: steps 5, 6, 7 — and the one new feature

Write it as $\displaystyle f(x) = x^{5/3} - 5x^{2/3}$ first, then differentiate term by term and factor out the smaller exponent:

$$f'(x) = \tfrac{5}{3}x^{2/3} - \tfrac{10}{3}x^{-1/3} = \tfrac{5}{3}x^{-1/3}\left(x - 2\right) = \frac{5(x-2)}{3x^{1/3}}$$
$$f''(x) = \tfrac{10}{9}x^{-1/3} + \tfrac{10}{9}x^{-4/3} = \tfrac{10}{9}x^{-4/3}\left(x+1\right) = \frac{10(x+1)}{9x^{4/3}}$$
Show: the sketch

⚠ $\displaystyle x=0$ is a critical number even though $\displaystyle f'(0)$ does not exist — the definition is “$\displaystyle f'(c)=0$ or $\displaystyle f'(c)$ does not exist”. The graph comes to a point there instead of turning smoothly. $\displaystyle x^{4/3}$ in the second derivative is positive on both sides, which is why the concavity does not change at $\displaystyle 0$.

For Reference: A Trigonometric One

Also not worked in class. The checklist does not change for trig — but the interval does, and the cuts come from solving a trig equation rather than factoring.

Show: $\displaystyle g(x) = x + 2\cos x$ on $\displaystyle [0,2\pi]$ — steps 1–4

Domain: given as $\displaystyle [0,2\pi]$, so the two endpoints are candidates the way they were in §4.1. Intercepts: $\displaystyle g(0)=2$. Symmetry: $\displaystyle \cos$ is even and $\displaystyle x$ is odd, so the sum is neither — and on a restricted interval symmetry buys nothing anyway. Asymptotes: none on a closed interval.

Show: steps 5, 6, 7 — solving instead of factoring
$$g'(x) = 1 - 2\sin x = 0 \iff \sin x = \tfrac{1}{2} \iff x = \tfrac{\pi}{6},\ \tfrac{5\pi}{6}$$
$$g''(x) = -2\cos x = 0 \iff \cos x = 0 \iff x = \tfrac{\pi}{2},\ \tfrac{3\pi}{2}$$
Show: the sketch

Two differences from every example above. The critical numbers come from solving a trig equation, so there can be several in one period and you must keep only those inside the interval. And on a closed interval the endpoints are candidates for absolute extrema even though $\displaystyle g'$ is not zero there — here $\displaystyle g(2\pi) = 2\pi + 2$ is the absolute maximum, beating the local maximum at $\displaystyle \pi/6$.

What You Should Be Able to Do

  • Run the seven-step checklist in order on a polynomial, a rational function and an exponential.
  • Find vertical asymptotes from the domain and horizontal ones from limits at infinity — each end separately.
  • Use even or odd symmetry to halve the work.
  • Say why a concavity change is not always an inflection point.
  • Given $\displaystyle f$, $\displaystyle f'$ and $\displaystyle f''$, produce the four readings and an accurate sketch.

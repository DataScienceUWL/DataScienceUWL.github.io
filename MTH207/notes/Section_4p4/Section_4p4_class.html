4.4  Indeterminate Forms and l'Hospital's Rule

MTH 207 Calculus I — Applications of Differentiation

A Limit None of Our Tools Can Touch

Here is a limit that looks harmless:

$$\lim_{x \to 1} \frac{\ln x}{x - 1}$$

Substitute and you get $\displaystyle \frac{\ln 1}{1-1} = \frac{0}{0}$ — not an answer, just a report that the limit laws gave up. So reach for the Chapter 2 toolkit:

  • Factor and cancel? Nothing to factor — $\displaystyle \ln x$ is not a polynomial and shares no factor with $\displaystyle x-1$.
  • Rationalise, or multiply by a conjugate? There is no radical.
  • Divide by the highest power? That is for limits at infinity.

Every technique we own is exhausted — and yet the limit plainly exists: plot it and the curve walks through $\displaystyle x=1$ without a flicker. We are not stuck because the problem is hard. We are stuck because we are missing a tool.

By the end of today this is one line.

Indeterminate Does Not Mean Hard. It Means Undecided.

Definition — Indeterminate Form

An indeterminate form is a combination of limiting values that the limit laws cannot evaluate — the answer depends on which functions produced it, not on the form alone.

Look at the two columns side by side. $\displaystyle \frac{5}{0}$ is not indeterminate — a fixed numerator over something shrinking to zero blows up, every time. $\displaystyle \frac{0}{0}$ is, because the answer can be anything: $\displaystyle \frac{2x}{x} \to 2$, $\displaystyle \frac{x^{2}}{x} \to 0$ and $\displaystyle \frac{x}{x^{3}} \to \infty$ are all $\displaystyle \frac{0}{0}$ at $\displaystyle x=0$.

l'Hospital's Rule

Theorem — l'Hospital's Rule

Suppose $\displaystyle f$ and $\displaystyle g$ are differentiable near $\displaystyle a$ and $\displaystyle g'(x) \neq 0$ near $\displaystyle a$. If $\displaystyle \lim_{x \to a} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)}$ has the form $\displaystyle \frac{0}{0}$ or $\displaystyle \frac{\infty}{\infty}$, then

$$\lim_{x \to a} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \lim_{x \to a} \frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}$$

provided the limit on the right exists or is $\displaystyle \pm\infty$.

And $\displaystyle a$ does not have to be a number. Everything above stays true when $\displaystyle x \to a$ is replaced by any of

$$x \to a^{+} \qquad x \to a^{-} \qquad x \to \infty \qquad x \to -\infty$$

⚠ You are not using the Quotient Rule. Differentiate the top. Separately, differentiate the bottom. Put one over the other. Writing $\displaystyle \frac{f'g - fg'}{g^{2}}$ here is the single most common way to lose these marks, and HW33 has a True/False item about exactly it.

⚠ That last clause is not small print. A large share of what you will do with this rule happens at infinity: the growth comparisons later today, four of the five problems in tonight's homework, and Exam 3's $\displaystyle \lim_{x\to\infty} \frac{x^{2}}{e^{3x}}$. “$\displaystyle \frac{\infty}{\infty}$ as $\displaystyle x \to \infty$” is just as legal as “$\displaystyle \frac{0}{0}$ at a point”, and you do not need a different theorem for it.

And the hypothesis is not decoration. Both have to vanish (or both blow up) — that is what creates the common factor the picture cancels. If only one of them is zero there is nothing to cancel and the rule is simply false.

My Turn: The Limit We Opened With

My turn

Evaluate $\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 1} \frac{\ln x}{x-1}$.

one application, and it is finished

$\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 1} \frac{\ln x}{x-1}$substituting gives $\displaystyle \frac{\ln 1}{1-1} = \frac{0}{0}$ — indeterminate, so the rule applies
$\displaystyle = \lim_{x \to 1} \frac{1/x}{1}$derivative of the top over derivative of the bottom: $\displaystyle \frac{d}{dx}\ln x = \frac{1}{x}$, and $\displaystyle \frac{d}{dx}(x-1) = 1$
$\displaystyle = \lim_{x \to 1} \frac{1}{x} = 1$check the form — $\displaystyle \frac{1}{1}$ is a number, not indeterminate, so STOP and substitute

Three lines, and the thing Chapter 2 could not touch is done. Two habits to take from it: the rule is one derivative on top, one on the bottom and never the Quotient Rule — and you stop the moment the form stops being indeterminate.

Your Turn: The Same Limit, Two Ways

Your turn

Evaluate $\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 4} \frac{x^{2}-2x-8}{x^{2}-4x}$ twice — once by factoring, the way you would have done it in Chapter 2, and once with l'Hospital's Rule.

Show: by factoring, the Chapter 2 way

Both top and bottom are zero at $\displaystyle x=4$, so $\displaystyle (x-4)$ is a factor of each. Factor, cancel, then substitute — and carry the limit symbol until the limit is actually taken:

$$\lim_{x \to 4} \frac{x^{2}-2x-8}{x^{2}-4x} = \lim_{x \to 4} \frac{(x-4)(x+2)}{x(x-4)}$$
$$= \lim_{x \to 4} \frac{x+2}{x} = \frac{4+2}{4} = \frac{6}{4} = \frac{3}{2}$$

The $\displaystyle (x-4)$ cancels because $\displaystyle x \neq 4$ on the way to $\displaystyle 4$ — that is what makes the cancelling legal.

Show: by l'Hospital, in one line

The form is $\displaystyle \frac{16-8-8}{16-16} = \frac{0}{0}$, so the rule applies:

$$\lim_{x \to 4} \frac{x^{2}-2x-8}{x^{2}-4x} = \lim_{x \to 4} \frac{2x-2}{2x-4} = \frac{6}{4} = \frac{3}{2}$$

Check the form before stopping: $\displaystyle \frac{6}{4}$ is a number.

Same answer — and that is the point. The rule is not a different kind of mathematics, it is a shortcut past the cancelling. Notice why it worked: factoring found a common factor of $\displaystyle (x-4)$, and the picture two frames ago is that same common factor being cancelled automatically.

So when both routes are open, take whichever is shorter. The rule earns its keep on the limits where the Chapter 2 toolkit has nothing to offer — like the one we opened on.

My Turn: When Once Is Not Enough

My turn

Evaluate $\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1 - \cos x}{x^{2}}$.

one application is not enough

$\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1-\cos x}{x^{2}}$substituting gives $\displaystyle \frac{1-1}{0} = \frac{0}{0}$ — indeterminate, so the rule applies
$\displaystyle = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x}{2x}$derivative of the top, over the derivative of the bottom. Not the Quotient Rule
STOP and look. Substituting now gives $\displaystyle \frac{0}{0}$ again, so we are allowed to go round once more — and only because we checked
$\displaystyle = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\cos x}{2}$differentiate top and bottom again
$\displaystyle = \frac{\cos 0}{2} = \frac{1}{2}$now substitution works: the form is $\displaystyle \frac{1}{2}$, which is a number, so we STOP

The rule does not promise to finish in one step. It promises to replace one indeterminate quotient with another limit — which may need the rule again, or may not need it at all. Re-check the form before every application, and stop the instant it is no longer indeterminate.

⚠ The commonest wreck: applying it a third time here. After $\displaystyle \frac{\cos x}{2}$ the form is $\displaystyle \frac{1}{2}$, not $\displaystyle \frac{0}{0}$ — differentiating again gives $\displaystyle \frac{-\sin x}{0}$, which is nonsense. The rule is a loop with an exit condition, and the exit condition is the form.

Infinity Over Infinity, and Who Wins the Race

The other quotient form works exactly the same way.

My turn

Evaluate $\displaystyle \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{\ln x}{x}$. Note what we are relying on: $\displaystyle x \to \infty$, not $\displaystyle x \to a$. That is the clause the theorem added.

Show: Both pieces run off to , so…

Both pieces run off to $\displaystyle \infty$, so the form is $\displaystyle \frac{\infty}{\infty}$ and the rule applies. Differentiate top and bottom separately, one line at a time:

$$\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{\ln x}{x} = \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{\frac{d}{dx}\left[\ln x\right]}{\frac{d}{dx}\left[x\right]} = \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{1/x}{1}$$
$$= \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{1}{x} = 0$$

Check the form before stopping: $\displaystyle \frac{1}{x} \to 0$, which is a number. The bottom wins.

l'Hospital settles who wins, and the answer never changes: exponential beats every power, and every power beats every logarithm. Worth knowing before you start, because it tells you what the answer should be — and if your work disagrees with the picture, your work is wrong.

Your Turn: Two Limits That Are NOT Indeterminate

Your turn

Neither of these needs l'Hospital's Rule. For each one, say what the form is and find the limit. (a) $\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{x}{x+1}$    (b) $\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 0^{+}} \frac{\cos x}{x}$

Show: (a) the form is $\displaystyle 0/1$

Substitute, and the limit laws finish it:

$$\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{x}{x+1} = \frac{\lim_{x \to 0} x}{\lim_{x \to 0} \left(x+1\right)} = \frac{0}{0+1} = \frac{0}{1} = 0$$

A zero over a nonzero number is just zero. Nothing is indeterminate, so there is nothing to do. ⚠ Apply the rule anyway and you get

$$\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\frac{d}{dx}\left[x\right]}{\frac{d}{dx}\left[x+1\right]} = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1}{1} = 1$$

which is wrong. The rule is not merely unnecessary here; it lies.

Show: (b) the form is $\displaystyle 1/0^{+}$

Take the two pieces separately:

$$\lim_{x \to 0^{+}} \cos x = \cos 0 = 1 \qquad \lim_{x \to 0^{+}} x = 0^{+}$$

A fixed nonzero numerator over a denominator shrinking to zero through positive values grows without bound:

$$\lim_{x \to 0^{+}} \frac{\cos x}{x} = +\infty$$
Again there is nothing to differentiate. The rule would give
$$\lim_{x \to 0^{+}} \frac{\frac{d}{dx}\left[\cos x\right]}{\frac{d}{dx}\left[x\right]} = \lim_{x \to 0^{+}} \frac{-\sin x}{1} = 0$$

which is wrong by as much as it is possible to be wrong.

These two are the whole reason we spent the first ten minutes on forms. Substitute first, every time. Only if what comes back is one of the seven does anything else happen, and HW33 contains two problems designed to catch you here.

Where We Are

  • Substitute first. Write down the form.
  • If it is $\displaystyle \frac{0}{0}$ or $\displaystyle \frac{\infty}{\infty}$, l'Hospital applies: differentiate top and bottom separately.
  • Re-check the form before applying it again, and stop as soon as it is not indeterminate.
  • If it is not indeterminate at all, just evaluate it.

Tomorrow: the other five forms — $\displaystyle 0 \cdot \infty$, $\displaystyle \infty - \infty$, $\displaystyle 1^{\infty}$, $\displaystyle 0^{0}$ and $\displaystyle \infty^{0}$. None of them is a quotient, so none of them can be fed to the rule directly. Every one is handled by rewriting it as a quotient first.

Zero Times Infinity: Make It a Quotient

l'Hospital's Rule only ever reads a quotient. So every remaining form is handled the same way: turn it into one.

A product $\displaystyle f \cdot g$ can always be written as a quotient in two ways: $\displaystyle \frac{f}{1/g}$ or $\displaystyle \frac{g}{1/f}$. They are both correct and one of them is usually much easier — so look before you leap.

My turn

Evaluate $\displaystyle \lim_{x \to \infty} x \sin\!\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)$.

flip the factor that is easier to differentiate

$\displaystyle \lim_{x \to \infty} x \sin\!\left(\tfrac{1}{x}\right)$as $\displaystyle x \to \infty$ the first factor goes to $\displaystyle \infty$ and the second to $\displaystyle \sin 0 = 0$: the form is $\displaystyle 0 \cdot \infty$, indeterminate
$\displaystyle = \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{\sin\left(1/x\right)}{1/x}$flip the $\displaystyle x$ into the denominator, since $\displaystyle \frac{1}{1/x} = x$. Now the form is $\displaystyle \frac{0}{0}$ and the rule applies
$\displaystyle = \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{\cos\left(1/x\right) \cdot \left(-1/x^{2}\right)}{-1/x^{2}}$Chain Rule on top, Power Rule on the bottom
$\displaystyle = \lim_{x \to \infty} \cos\!\left(\tfrac{1}{x}\right) = 1$the $\displaystyle -1/x^{2}$ cancels, and $\displaystyle 1/x \to 0$
Your turn

Now you: $\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 0^{+}} \sqrt{x}\,\ln x$.

Show: The form is 0 (- ) ,…

The form is $\displaystyle 0 \cdot (-\infty)$, so rewrite it as a quotient. Flip the $\displaystyle \sqrt{x}$ into the denominator, remembering $\displaystyle \frac{1}{\sqrt{x}} = x^{-1/2}$:

$$\lim_{x \to 0^{+}} \sqrt{x}\,\ln x = \lim_{x \to 0^{+}} \frac{\ln x}{x^{-1/2}}$$

That form is $\displaystyle \frac{-\infty}{\infty}$, so the rule applies:

$$= \lim_{x \to 0^{+}} \frac{\frac{d}{dx}\left[\ln x\right]}{\frac{d}{dx}\left[x^{-1/2}\right]} = \lim_{x \to 0^{+}} \frac{1/x}{-\frac{1}{2}x^{-3/2}}$$

Now the exponent algebra, one move per line. Write $\displaystyle \frac{1}{x}$ as $\displaystyle x^{-1}$, pull the constant out, and subtract exponents:

$$\frac{x^{-1}}{-\frac{1}{2}x^{-3/2}} = -2\,\frac{x^{-1}}{x^{-3/2}} = -2\,x^{-1-\left(-3/2\right)} = -2\,x^{1/2}$$

So the limit is

$$\lim_{x \to 0^{+}} \sqrt{x}\,\ln x = \lim_{x \to 0^{+}} \left(-2\sqrt{x}\right) = -2\cdot 0 = 0$$
⚠ Flipping the other factor gives $\displaystyle \frac{\sqrt{x}}{1/\ln x}$, which is $\displaystyle \frac{0}{0}$ and technically fine — but differentiating $\displaystyle 1/\ln x$ makes it worse, not better. Choosing badly is not wrong, it is just long.

Infinity Minus Infinity: Make It One Fraction

$\displaystyle \infty - \infty$ is indeterminate for the same reason as the others: it depends entirely on how fast each piece grows. The fix is algebra, not calculus — combine the two terms over a common denominator and a quotient appears.

My turn

Evaluate $\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 0^{+}} \left(\frac{1}{x} - \frac{1}{\sin x}\right)$.

combine first, differentiate second

$\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 0^{+}} \left(\frac{1}{x} - \frac{1}{\sin x}\right)$both terms run to $\displaystyle +\infty$ as $\displaystyle x \to 0^{+}$, so the form is $\displaystyle \infty - \infty$
$\displaystyle = \lim_{x \to 0^{+}} \frac{\sin x - x}{x \sin x}$one common denominator: $\displaystyle \frac{1}{x} - \frac{1}{\sin x} = \frac{\sin x}{x\sin x} - \frac{x}{x \sin x}$. Nothing clever, just fractions
now substitute: $\displaystyle \frac{0-0}{0 \cdot 0} = \frac{0}{0}$, so the rule applies
$\displaystyle = \lim_{x \to 0^{+}} \frac{\cos x - 1}{\sin x + x\cos x}$Product Rule on the bottom, since it is $\displaystyle x$ times $\displaystyle \sin x$
check again: $\displaystyle \frac{1-1}{0+0} = \frac{0}{0}$, so once more
$\displaystyle = \lim_{x \to 0^{+}} \frac{-\sin x}{2\cos x - x \sin x} = \frac{0}{2} = 0$and now the form is a number, so we stop

The Power Forms: Take a Logarithm

Three forms are left — $\displaystyle 1^{\infty}$, $\displaystyle 0^{0}$ and $\displaystyle \infty^{0}$ — and all three are an exponent problem. You cannot differentiate your way out of an exponent, so you take a logarithm first. This is the same move as logarithmic differentiation in §3.6.

Theorem — The Recipe for a Power Form

Let $\displaystyle y = \left[f(x)\right]^{g(x)}$. Then:

  1. take $\displaystyle \ln$ of both sides: $\displaystyle \ln y = g(x)\ln f(x)$;
  2. find $\displaystyle \lim \ln y$ — it will be a $\displaystyle 0 \cdot \infty$ form, which you already know how to do;
  3. exponentiate: if $\displaystyle \lim \ln y = L$, then $\displaystyle \lim y = e^{L}$.

My turn

Evaluate $\displaystyle \lim_{x \to \infty} \left(1 + \frac{3}{x}\right)^{x}$.

the three steps, in order

$\displaystyle y = \left(1 + \tfrac{3}{x}\right)^{x}$the base goes to $\displaystyle 1$ and the exponent to $\displaystyle \infty$: the form is $\displaystyle 1^{\infty}$, indeterminate
$\displaystyle \ln y = x \ln\!\left(1 + \tfrac{3}{x}\right)$step 1 — take $\displaystyle \ln$ of both sides. A logarithm turns the exponent into a factor
$\displaystyle \lim_{x \to \infty} \ln y = \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{\ln\left(1 + 3/x\right)}{1/x}$step 2 — that is $\displaystyle \infty \cdot 0$, so flip the $\displaystyle x$ into the denominator and the form becomes $\displaystyle \frac{0}{0}$
$\displaystyle = \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{\frac{1}{1+3/x} \cdot \left(-3/x^{2}\right)}{-1/x^{2}}$Chain Rule on top, Power Rule on the bottom
$\displaystyle = \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{3}{1 + 3/x} = 3$the $\displaystyle -1/x^{2}$ cancels again, and $\displaystyle 3/x \to 0$
$\displaystyle \lim_{x \to \infty} y = e^{3}$step 3 — and this is the step everyone forgets. $\displaystyle \lim \ln y = 3$, so $\displaystyle \lim y = e^{3}$, NOT $\displaystyle 3$

⚠ Step 3 is where the marks go. You found the limit of $\displaystyle \ln y$, not of $\displaystyle y$. Writing $\displaystyle 3$ as the final answer is the single most common error on this kind of problem — the last line must undo the logarithm.

Your Turn: A Famous One

Your turn

Evaluate $\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 0^{+}} x^{x}$. Before you start: what form is it, and what would you guess the answer is?

Show: step 1 — the form, and why guessing fails

The base goes to $\displaystyle 0$ and the exponent goes to $\displaystyle 0$, so the form is $\displaystyle 0^{0}$. Both guesses are defensible and both are wrong: “anything to the zero is $\displaystyle 1$” says $\displaystyle 1$, and “zero to anything is $\displaystyle 0$” says $\displaystyle 0$. That disagreement is exactly what indeterminate means — the form alone does not decide.

Show: steps 2 and 3 — do it properly

Let $\displaystyle y = x^{x}$. Then $\displaystyle \ln y = x \ln x$, which is $\displaystyle 0 \cdot (-\infty)$. Flip the $\displaystyle x$:

$$\lim_{x \to 0^{+}} \frac{\ln x}{1/x} = \lim_{x \to 0^{+}} \frac{1/x}{-1/x^{2}} = \lim_{x \to 0^{+}} \left(-x\right) = 0$$

So $\displaystyle \ln y \to 0$, and therefore $\displaystyle y \to e^{0} = \mathbf{1}$.

One of the two guesses happened to be right, and it was right by accident. On Exam 3 a correct number with no work behind it is not worth much — the method is what is being bought.

Your Turn: When the Rule Goes Round in Circles

Your turn

Evaluate $\displaystyle \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{e^{x}+e^{-x}}{e^{x}-e^{-x}}$. Apply the rule once. Then apply it again. What do you notice?

Show: apply it twice and look carefully

The form is $\displaystyle \frac{\infty}{\infty}$, so the rule is allowed:

$$\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{e^{x}+e^{-x}}{e^{x}-e^{-x}} = \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{e^{x}-e^{-x}}{e^{x}+e^{-x}}$$

The numerator and denominator have simply swapped. Apply it again and they swap back — you are exactly where you started. The form is still $\displaystyle \frac{\infty}{\infty}$ every time, so the rule never stops being legal; it just never gets anywhere.

Show: so go back to Chapter 2

Divide the top and the bottom by $\displaystyle e^{x}$, the fastest-growing piece:

$$\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{e^{x}+e^{-x}}{e^{x}-e^{-x}} = \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{1+e^{-2x}}{1-e^{-2x}} = \frac{1+0}{1-0} = 1$$

Done in one line, by the §2.6 method, with no derivatives at all.

So the rule has three ways of not helping, and you have now seen all of them:

  • the form is not indeterminate — using it gives a wrong answer (yesterday's two traps);
  • it is legal, but the new limit is harder than the old one;
  • it is legal forever and cycles — as here.
In all three the answer is the same: the Chapter 2 toolkit never stopped working. l'Hospital's Rule was added to it, not a replacement for it.

What You Should Be Able to Do

  • Substitute, and name the form before doing anything else.
  • Say which forms are indeterminate and which the limit laws already settle.
  • Apply l'Hospital's Rule to $\displaystyle \frac{0}{0}$ and $\displaystyle \frac{\infty}{\infty}$, differentiating top and bottom separately, and re-check the form before each repeat.
  • Rewrite $\displaystyle 0 \cdot \infty$ as a quotient and $\displaystyle \infty - \infty$ as one fraction.
  • Handle $\displaystyle 1^{\infty}$, $\displaystyle 0^{0}$ and $\displaystyle \infty^{0}$ by taking a logarithm — and remember to exponentiate at the end.
  • Recognise the three ways the rule does not help — a form that was never indeterminate, a new limit that is worse, and one that cycles — and fall back on the Chapter 2 toolkit in each case.

5.1  Areas and Distances

MTH 207 Calculus I — Integrals

A Problem With No Formula

You can find the area of a rectangle, a triangle, a circle. Now look at the region under $\displaystyle y = 4-x^{2}$ from $\displaystyle x=0$ to $\displaystyle x=2$ and try to name its area.

There is no formula, and there is not going to be one. The move instead is the one calculus always makes: approximate with something you can measure, then let the approximation get better without limit. In Chapter 2 that was secant lines becoming a tangent. Here it is rectangles becoming an area.

Three Ways to Pick a Height

Cut $\displaystyle [0,2]$ into $\displaystyle n$ strips of equal width $\displaystyle \Delta x = \frac{2-0}{n}$. Each strip gets one rectangle — but how tall?

⚠ Read the picture, not a rule you memorized. This function is decreasing, so on each strip the tallest point is on the left — which is why the left rectangles overshoot and the right ones fall short. On an increasing function every one of those words swaps. There is no such thing as “right endpoints are too big”.

My Turn: Four Right-Endpoint Rectangles

My turn

Estimate the area under $\displaystyle f(x) = 4-x^{2}$ from $\displaystyle x=0$ to $\displaystyle x=2$ using four rectangles of equal width and right endpoints.

Show: set up the strips first — width, then the sample points
$$\Delta x = \frac{2-0}{4} = \frac{1}{2}$$

The strips are $\displaystyle [0,\tfrac12]$, $\displaystyle [\tfrac12,1]$, $\displaystyle [1,\tfrac32]$, $\displaystyle [\tfrac32,2]$, so the right endpoints are $\displaystyle x = \tfrac12,\ 1,\ \tfrac32,\ 2$.

heights, then one common factor

$\displaystyle f\!\left(\tfrac12\right) = 4 - \tfrac14 = \tfrac{15}{4}$height of the first rectangle
$\displaystyle f(1) = 4 - 1 = 3$the second
$\displaystyle f\!\left(\tfrac32\right) = 4 - \tfrac94 = \tfrac74$the third
$\displaystyle f(2) = 4 - 4 = 0$the fourth — a rectangle of zero height is still a strip
$\displaystyle R_{4} = \tfrac12\left(\tfrac{15}{4} + 3 + \tfrac74 + 0\right)$every width is the same, so factor $\displaystyle \Delta x$ out
$\displaystyle R_{4} = \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac{34}{4}$add the four heights
$\displaystyle R_{4} = \tfrac{17}{4} = 4.25$now multiply by the width, once
Show: is it too big or too small — without finding the true area?

$\displaystyle f$ is decreasing on $\displaystyle [0,2]$, so on every strip the right endpoint is where $\displaystyle f$ is smallest. Every rectangle therefore sits under the curve, and

$$R_{4} = 4.25 \ \lt\ \text{true area}$$

⚠ Note what that argument did not use: the value $\displaystyle 16/3$. It is an argument about the shape, and it is the one an exam asks for.

Two habits worth copying. Factor $\displaystyle \Delta x$ out rather than multiplying four separate products — it is the same move that makes the $\displaystyle \Sigma$ notation of §5.2 readable. And decide over/under from monotonicity, never by comparing with an answer you had to compute.

Your Turn: The Other End of Each Strip

Your turn

Same function, same four strips, but left endpoints. Find $\displaystyle L_{4}$, and say — from the shape, not from arithmetic — whether it is too big or too small. Then sandwich the true area between your two estimates.

Show: The left endpoints are x = 0,…

The left endpoints are $\displaystyle x = 0,\ \tfrac12,\ 1,\ \tfrac32$, so the heights are $\displaystyle 4,\ \tfrac{15}{4},\ 3,\ \tfrac74$.

$$L_{4} = \tfrac12\left(4 + \tfrac{15}{4} + 3 + \tfrac74\right) = \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac{50}{4} = \tfrac{25}{4} = 6.25$$

$\displaystyle f$ is decreasing, so the left endpoint is the tallest point of each strip: every rectangle pokes above the curve and $\displaystyle L_{4}$ is an over-estimate. Putting the two together, with no integration at all:

$$4.25 \ \lt\ \text{true area} \ \lt\ 6.25$$

⚠ You have now trapped the answer without computing it. That is worth more than either estimate on its own — and the gap, $\displaystyle 6.25-4.25 = 2$, is exactly $\displaystyle \Delta x\left[f(0)-f(2)\right]$. Halve the strip width and you halve the gap, which is the whole reason the next step works.

Now Let the Strips Get Thin

Definition — The Area Under a Curve

If $\displaystyle f$ is continuous and $\displaystyle f(x) \ge 0$ on $\displaystyle [a,b]$, the area of the region under the graph is

$$A = \lim_{n \to \infty} \left[f(x_{1})\Delta x + f(x_{2})\Delta x + \cdots + f(x_{n})\Delta x\right]$$

where $\displaystyle \Delta x = \frac{b-a}{n}$ and $\displaystyle x_{i}$ is a sample point in the $\displaystyle i$th strip.

⚠ The limit does not care which rule you used. Left, right and midpoint all march to the same number, because the gap between the over-estimate and the under-estimate shrinks to nothing. That is why the definition says “a sample point” and not “the right endpoint”. Tomorrow that bracket gets a name and a symbol — $\displaystyle \sum$ — and the limit gets evaluated rather than guessed at.

For Reference: The Distance Problem Is the Same Problem

Not worked in class — but it is why the same machinery answers two questions.

A car's speedometer is read every $\displaystyle 2$ seconds: $\displaystyle 0,\ 10,\ 18,\ 24$ m/s. Roughly how far did it travel in those $\displaystyle 6$ seconds?

Your turn

Estimate the distance two ways, and say which is which.

Show: Assume the speed over each 2 -second…

Assume the speed over each $\displaystyle 2$-second interval is the reading at the start: $\displaystyle (0)(2) + (10)(2) + (18)(2) = 56$ m. Assume instead it is the reading at the end: $\displaystyle (10)(2) + (18)(2) + (24)(2) = 104$ m. The car is speeding up, so the first is an under-estimate and the second an over-estimate: the true distance is between $\displaystyle 56$ and $\displaystyle 104$ metres.

Look at what you just computed: a sum of (height) times (width), where the height is a velocity and the width is a time. That is the same arithmetic as the area problem, with different units — so distance travelled is the area under a velocity graph. One limit, two meanings.

What You Should Be Able to Do

  • Cut $\displaystyle [a,b]$ into $\displaystyle n$ equal strips and write down $\displaystyle \Delta x$.
  • Build $\displaystyle L_{n}$, $\displaystyle R_{n}$ or $\displaystyle M_{n}$ — heights first, then factor $\displaystyle \Delta x$ out once.
  • Say whether an estimate is too big or too small from whether $\displaystyle f$ is increasing or decreasing, without computing the true area.
  • State the area as the limit of those sums as $\displaystyle n \to \infty$.

⚠ The over/under question is worth real points and is the one most often answered by reciting a rule. Exam 3 asks it on an increasing function, where every verdict in today's example is reversed. Draw the strip and look.

4.1  Maximum and Minimum Values

MTH 207 Calculus I — Applications of Differentiation

What We Are Actually Looking For

Chapter 3 was about computing derivatives. Chapter 4 is about using them, and the first use is the one every application wants: find the biggest or smallest value something can take.

Definition — Absolute and Local Extrema

Let $\displaystyle c$ be in the domain of $\displaystyle f$.

  • $\displaystyle f(c)$ is the absolute maximum if $\displaystyle f(c) \geq f(x)$ for all $\displaystyle x$ in the domain.
  • $\displaystyle f(c)$ is a local maximum if $\displaystyle f(c) \geq f(x)$ for all $\displaystyle x$ near $\displaystyle c$.
Absolute and local minimum are the same with $\displaystyle \leq$.

The only difference between the two definitions is the range of the comparison: all $\displaystyle x$, or $\displaystyle x$ near $\displaystyle c$. A local maximum is a hilltop; an absolute maximum is the highest hilltop — and on this curve it is the edge of the map, not a hilltop at all.

Now look at the tangents, because that is the next hour. At $\displaystyle x=2$, $\displaystyle x=4$ and $\displaystyle x=7.5$ the tangent is flat. At $\displaystyle x=6$ there is a local maximum and no tangent at all. At $\displaystyle x=b$ there is the absolute maximum and the slope is nowhere near zero. Any rule connecting extrema to derivatives has to survive all three of those.

Fermat's Theorem: Where an Extremum Can Hide

Searching every $\displaystyle x$ is hopeless — there are infinitely many. Calculus narrows the search to a handful.

Theorem — Fermat's Theorem

If $\displaystyle f$ has a local maximum or minimum at $\displaystyle c$, and $\displaystyle f'(c)$ exists, then

$$f'(c) = 0.$$

Check it against the picture: at $\displaystyle x=2$, $\displaystyle x=4$ and $\displaystyle x=7.5$ there is a local extremum, the derivative exists, and the tangent is flat — three for three. At $\displaystyle x=6$ there is a local extremum and the derivative does not exist, so Fermat's second hypothesis fails and the theorem simply does not apply. It is not violated; it was never invoked.

This is why the derivative is the right tool: it turns “find the highest point” into “solve $\displaystyle f'(x)=0$” — an equation, which is something algebra can do.

Fermat Runs One Way

Fermat says: extremum $\displaystyle \Rightarrow$ flat tangent. Students read it backwards — flat tangent $\displaystyle \Rightarrow$ extremum — and that statement is false.

Your turn

Two questions, no computation needed.

  1. $\displaystyle f(x)=x^{3}$ has $\displaystyle f'(0)=0$. Does $\displaystyle f$ have a local max or min at $\displaystyle 0$?
  2. $\displaystyle f(x)=|x|$ has a local minimum at $\displaystyle 0$. What is $\displaystyle f'(0)$?   (You have met this one already — it is the corner at $\displaystyle x=6$ on the first picture today.)

Show: both answers, in one picture

1. No. $\displaystyle f'(0)=0$ and the curve goes straight through — flat is not the same as turning around.

2. $\displaystyle f'(0)$ does not exist. The corner has no single tangent line, so Fermat's hypothesis fails and Fermat says nothing at all.

So $\displaystyle f'(c)=0$ makes $\displaystyle c$ a candidate, never a conclusion — and an extremum can sit where $\displaystyle f'$ does not exist, which Fermat never promised to find. Both gaps are why the next definition is written the way it is.

Critical Numbers: the Candidate List

Both gaps go into the definition, which is why it has two clauses.

Definition — Critical Number

A critical number of $\displaystyle f$ is a number $\displaystyle c$ in the domain of $\displaystyle f$ such that either

$$f'(c) = 0 \qquad\text{or}\qquad f'(c) \text{ does not exist.}$$
Your turn

Find all critical numbers of $\displaystyle f(x) = x^{3} - 6x^{2} + 5$.

Show: differentiate, then solve
$$f'(x) = 3x^{2} - 12x$$

$\displaystyle f'$ is a polynomial, so it exists everywhere — only the first clause can fire:

$$3x^{2} - 12x = 0$$
$$3x\left(x - 4\right) = 0$$
$$c = 0 \quad\text{and}\quad c = 4$$
Your turn

Now $\displaystyle g(x) = \dfrac{x}{x^{2}+1}$.

Show: Quotient Rule, then set the numerator to zero
$$g'(x) = \frac{\left(x^{2}+1\right)\left(1\right) - x\left(2x\right)} {\left(x^{2}+1\right)^{2}} = \frac{1 - x^{2}}{\left(x^{2}+1\right)^{2}}$$

A fraction is zero exactly when its numerator is zero:

$$1 - x^{2} = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad c = -1,\; 1$$

The denominator is never zero, so $\displaystyle g'$ exists everywhere and there is nothing from the second clause.

Where the Derivative Fails to Exist

The second clause is the one the homework hides problems in, and it is the one students skip.

Your turn

Find all critical numbers of $\displaystyle h(x) = \left(x-1\right)^{2/3}$.

Show: the derivative is never zero — so look at the other clause
$$h'(x) = \tfrac{2}{3}\left(x-1\right)^{-1/3} = \frac{2}{3\sqrt[3]{x-1}}$$

Set it to zero: a fraction with numerator $\displaystyle 2$ is never zero, so the first clause gives nothing. Now the second clause. $\displaystyle h'(x)$ is undefined at $\displaystyle x = 1$, and $\displaystyle x = 1$ is in the domain of $\displaystyle h$ — $\displaystyle h(1) = 0$. So

$$c = 1$$

is a critical number, and it is the only one.

Two things have to be true: $\displaystyle f'(c)$ fails to exist, and $\displaystyle c$ is in the domain of $\displaystyle f$. A point where the function is undefined is not a critical number — there is no value there to be a maximum.

Your turn

Quick check: does $\displaystyle k(x) = \dfrac{1}{x}$ have a critical number at $\displaystyle 0$?

Show: no — and the reason is the domain, not the derivative

$\displaystyle k'(x) = -\dfrac{1}{x^{2}}$ is never zero, and it fails to exist at $\displaystyle x=0$ — but $\displaystyle k(0)$ is undefined, so $\displaystyle 0$ is not in the domain and cannot be a critical number. $\displaystyle k$ has no critical numbers at all.

Does a Maximum Even Exist?

Yesterday we built a candidate list. Today we use it — but first, a question worth asking before any search: is there anything to find?

Your turn

Does $\displaystyle f(x) = x$ have an absolute maximum on the interval $\displaystyle (0,1)$? On $\displaystyle [0,1]$?

Show: the first has none, and the reason is the missing endpoint

On $\displaystyle (0,1)$ the values climb toward $\displaystyle 1$ and never reach it — for any $\displaystyle x$ you pick, $\displaystyle \frac{x+1}{2}$ is bigger. No absolute maximum exists. On $\displaystyle [0,1]$ the endpoint is included, so the maximum is $\displaystyle f(1)=1$.

Theorem — The Extreme Value Theorem

If $\displaystyle f$ is continuous on a closed, bounded interval $\displaystyle [a,b]$, then $\displaystyle f$ attains an absolute maximum and an absolute minimum somewhere on $\displaystyle [a,b]$.

Both hypotheses do real work, and the right-hand picture is the other failure: closed but discontinuous, and again nothing is attained. Drop closed or drop continuous and the guarantee is gone.

The Closed Interval Method

The EVT says the answer exists. Fermat says it is at a critical number — or at an endpoint, which Fermat never covered because Fermat is about interior points. So check both.

To find the absolute extrema of a continuous $\displaystyle f$ on $\displaystyle [a,b]$:

  1. Find the critical numbers of $\displaystyle f$ in $\displaystyle (a,b)$.
  2. Evaluate $\displaystyle f$ at each one, and at both endpoints $\displaystyle a$ and $\displaystyle b$.
  3. The largest value on that list is the absolute maximum; the smallest is the absolute minimum.
No derivative test, no graph. It is a finite list and you compare numbers.

My turn

Find the absolute maximum and minimum of $\displaystyle f(x) = x^{3} - 3x + 1$ on $\displaystyle [0,3]$. Watch this one. You get the next.

Show: step 1 — the critical numbers
$$f'(x) = 3x^{2} - 3 = 3\left(x^{2}-1\right) = 3\left(x-1\right)\left(x+1\right)$$
$$c = 1 \quad\text{and}\quad c = -1$$

⚠ $\displaystyle c=-1$ is not in $\displaystyle [0,3]$, so it is thrown away. Only $\displaystyle c=1$ survives.

Show: step 2 — evaluate at the survivor and both endpoints
$$f(0) = 0 - 0 + 1 = 1$$
$$f(1) = 1 - 3 + 1 = -1$$
$$f(3) = 27 - 9 + 1 = 19$$
Show: step 3 — compare the three numbers

Largest is $\displaystyle 19$, smallest is $\displaystyle -1$:

$$\text{absolute max } = 19 \text{ at } x = 3, \qquad \text{absolute min } = -1 \text{ at } x = 1$$

Notice Where the Maximum Was

The maximum was at $\displaystyle x=3$ — an endpoint, where $\displaystyle f'(3) = 24 \neq 0$.

An absolute extremum on a closed interval can sit at an endpoint, where the derivative is nothing in particular. That is exactly why step 2 evaluates the endpoints every time, even when they look uninteresting — and it is why “if $\displaystyle f$ has an absolute minimum at $\displaystyle c$, then $\displaystyle f'(c)=0$” is false.

Your turn

Find the absolute maximum and minimum of $\displaystyle g(x) = 2x^{3} + 3x^{2} - 12x$ on $\displaystyle [-3, 2]$.

Show: critical numbers first
$$g'(x) = 6x^{2} + 6x - 12 = 6\left(x^{2}+x-2\right) = 6\left(x+2\right)\left(x-1\right)$$
$$c = -2 \quad\text{and}\quad c = 1 \qquad\text{--- both are inside } [-3,2]$$
Show: evaluate at all four
$$g(-3) = -54 + 27 + 36 = 9$$
$$g(-2) = -16 + 12 + 24 = 20$$
$$g(1) = 2 + 3 - 12 = -7$$
$$g(2) = 16 + 12 - 24 = 4$$
Show: compare
$$\text{absolute max } = 20 \text{ at } x = -2, \qquad \text{absolute min } = -7 \text{ at } x = 1$$

This time both winners are interior critical numbers — which is why you cannot guess, you evaluate.

When the Winner Has No Derivative

Your turn

Find the absolute maximum and minimum of $\displaystyle f(x) = x^{2/3}$ on $\displaystyle \left[-1, 8\right]$.

Show: the critical number comes from the SECOND clause
$$f'(x) = \tfrac{2}{3}x^{-1/3} = \frac{2}{3\sqrt[3]{x}}$$

Never zero. But $\displaystyle f'(0)$ does not exist, and $\displaystyle 0$ is in the domain ($\displaystyle f(0)=0$), so

$$c = 0$$

is the only critical number.

Show: evaluate at the critical number and both endpoints
$$f(-1) = \left(-1\right)^{2/3} = 1$$
$$f(0) = 0$$
$$f(8) = 8^{2/3} = \left(\sqrt[3]{8}\right)^{2} = 4$$
$$\text{absolute max } = 4 \text{ at } x = 8, \qquad \text{absolute min } = 0 \text{ at } x = 0$$

The minimum sits exactly where the derivative fails to exist. A student who only ever solves $\displaystyle f'(x)=0$ never finds it — and that is the whole reason the definition of a critical number has two clauses.

What You Should Be Able to Do

  • State the difference between a local and an absolute extremum — the quantifier, “near $\displaystyle c$” versus “all $\displaystyle x$”.
  • Find critical numbers: solve $\displaystyle f'(x)=0$ and find where $\displaystyle f'$ fails to exist, keeping only numbers in the domain.
  • Say why $\displaystyle f'(c)=0$ makes $\displaystyle c$ a candidate, not an answer, and give $\displaystyle x^{3}$ as the counterexample.
  • State the Extreme Value Theorem with both hypotheses, and say what fails without each.
  • Run the Closed Interval Method: critical numbers inside, then both endpoints, then compare the list.

The derivative does not hand you the maximum. It hands you a short list, and you finish the job by evaluating and comparing.

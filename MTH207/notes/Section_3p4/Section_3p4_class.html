Something You Cannot Differentiate Yet

Six trig derivatives, three rules. Two functions — one you can handle.

Your turn

Differentiate each, or get as far as you can.

$$\text{(a)}\;\; y = \left(x^{2}+1\right)^{3} \qquad\qquad \text{(b)}\;\; y = \sin\!\left(x^{3}\right)$$

For (a), do not multiply it out. Write it as $\displaystyle \left(x^{2}+1\right)\left(x^{2}+1\right)\left(x^{2}+1\right)$ and use yesterday's three-factor Product Rule.

(a) three identical factors

$\displaystyle y = \left(x^{2}+1\right)\left(x^{2}+1\right)\left(x^{2}+1\right)$as the prompt asked — not multiplied out
$\displaystyle f = g = h = x^{2}+1 \qquad f' = g' = h' = 2x$name them
$\displaystyle \left(fgh\right)' = f'gh + fg'h + fgh'$yesterday's rule for three
$\displaystyle y' = (2x)\left(x^{2}+1\right)\left(x^{2}+1\right) + \left(x^{2}+1\right)(2x)\left(x^{2}+1\right) + \left(x^{2}+1\right)\left(x^{2}+1\right)(2x)$all three terms come out the same — the factors are identical
$\displaystyle y' = 3\left(x^{2}+1\right)^{2}\cdot 2x$collect

Read what came out: the exponent came down, the inside was left alone, times the derivative of the inside. Hold on to that.

Show: (b) is a wall

Nothing to multiply out. $\displaystyle \sin\!\left(x^{3}\right)$ is not a product, not a quotient, not a power of $\displaystyle x$ — and every rule you own needs pieces added, multiplied or divided. This one is built by substitution: take $\displaystyle x$, cube it, take the sine.

A function built by feeding one function into another is a composite, and you have no rule for one. That is §3.4. After it, only logarithms and $\displaystyle a^{x}$ are left — then every standard function has a rule.

The Chain Rule

Theorem — The Chain Rule

If $\displaystyle g$ is differentiable at $\displaystyle x$ and $\displaystyle f$ is differentiable at $\displaystyle g(x)$, then

$$\frac{d}{dx}\,f\!\left(g(x)\right) \;=\; f'\!\left(g(x)\right)\cdot g'(x)$$

Same rule, in the words you have been using all period:

$$\frac{d}{dx}\,\text{out}\!\left(\text{in}(x)\right) \;=\; \text{out}'\!\left(\text{in}(x)\right)\cdot\text{in}'(x)$$

Derivative of the outside, left alone on the inside, times the derivative of the inside. The part everyone drops is the second factor. The part everyone gets wrong is the first: it is $\displaystyle f'$ evaluated at $\displaystyle g(x)$, not at $\displaystyle x$ — which the second line says out loud: $\displaystyle \text{out}'$ is evaluated at $\displaystyle \text{in}(x)$.

Back to the wall from the start of class:

$$\frac{d}{dx}\sin\!\left(x^{3}\right) \;=\; \underbrace{\cos\!\left(x^{3}\right)}_{\text{outside}'\text{, at the inside}}\cdot\underbrace{3x^{2}}_{\text{inside}'}$$

And back to the one you did by hand:

$$\frac{d}{dx}\left(x^{2}+1\right)^{3} \;=\; 3\left(x^{2}+1\right)^{2}\cdot 2x$$

which is exactly what multiplying out and differentiating gave you.

One more, where the outside is a root. Rewrite it first so the outside is a power:

$$y = \sqrt{7x^{3}+5x} = \left(7x^{3}+5x\right)^{1/2} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad y' = \underbrace{\tfrac12\left(7x^{3}+5x\right)^{-1/2}}_{\text{outside}'\text{, at the inside}} \cdot\underbrace{\left(21x^{2}+5\right)}_{\text{inside}'}$$

$\displaystyle \cos\!\left(x^{3}\right)$ alone is wrong. It is the most common error in this section by a distance. The inside leaves a fingerprint — if $\displaystyle g'(x)$ is not in your answer, you have not used the rule.

Chain Rule for Exponentials

Someone asked about a homework problem. Start there.

My turn

First the exponential on its own, where the outside is its own derivative.

outside unchanged, times the inside's derivative

$\displaystyle y = e^{\,x^{3}-2x}$outside is $\displaystyle e^{(\;)}$, inside is $\displaystyle x^{3}-2x$
$\displaystyle y' = e^{\,x^{3}-2x}\cdot\frac{d}{dx}\left[\,x^{3}-2x\,\right]$the outside's derivative is itself
$\displaystyle y' = \left(3x^{2}-2\right)e^{\,x^{3}-2x}$now the inside

$\displaystyle e$ to anything keeps its exponent. $\displaystyle e^{3x^{2}-2}$ is wrong — the exponent is untouched, and its derivative multiplies on the outside.

My turn

Now the homework's shape: $\displaystyle \;y = e^{-5t}\sin(4t)$.

outermost first, chain inside each factor

$\displaystyle y = e^{-5t}\sin(4t)$a product, so the Product Rule runs first
$\displaystyle y' = \frac{d}{dt}\left[\,e^{-5t}\,\right]\sin(4t) + e^{-5t}\,\frac{d}{dt}\left[\,\sin(4t)\,\right]$write $\displaystyle f'g+fg'$ out before computing anything
$\displaystyle y' = \left(-5e^{-5t}\right)\sin(4t) + e^{-5t}\left(4\cos(4t)\right)$each $\displaystyle \frac{d}{dt}$ is a Chain Rule of its own

Two rules in one problem is the normal case. Name the outermost operation, run that rule, and let the Chain Rule handle what is inside.

Now You: Outside, Then Inside

Your turn

Differentiate. For each one, say the inside out loud before you write anything.

$$\text{(a)}\;\; y = \left(3x+1\right)^{4} \qquad \text{(b)}\;\; y = \sin\!\left(5x\right) \qquad \text{(c)}\;\; y = e^{\,x^{2}+\sin x} \qquad \text{(d)}\;\; y = \sqrt{x^{2}+9}$$
Show: all four, same two steps

(a) inside $\displaystyle 3x+1$, outside $\displaystyle u^{4}$: $\displaystyle \;y' = 4\left(3x+1\right)^{3}\cdot 3 = 12\left(3x+1\right)^{3}$

(b) inside $\displaystyle 5x$, outside $\displaystyle \sin u$: $\displaystyle \;y' = \cos(5x)\cdot 5 = 5\cos(5x)$

(c) inside $\displaystyle x^{2}+\sin x$, outside $\displaystyle e^{(\;)}$. The inside is itself a sum, so differentiate it term by term:

$$\frac{d}{dx}\left(x^{2}+\sin x\right) = 2x+\cos x$$

Then

$$y' = e^{\,x^{2}+\sin x}\cdot\left(2x+\cos x\right) \;=\; \left(2x+\cos x\right)e^{\,x^{2}+\sin x}$$

(d) rewrite as $\displaystyle \left(x^{2}+9\right)^{1/2}$, inside $\displaystyle x^{2}+9$:

$$y' = \tfrac12\left(x^{2}+9\right)^{-1/2}\cdot 2x \;=\; \frac{x}{\sqrt{x^{2}+9}}$$

Every one of these is two steps and a multiplication. If your answer does not have the inside's derivative multiplied onto it, go back.

Which Rule, Before Any Work

Four rules now. Picking is a separate skill from running — and it decides most exam questions.

Evaluating and differentiating run in opposite orders. Ask what you would do last plugging a number in; that is what you differentiate first. For $\displaystyle x^{2}\sin x$ the last step is multiply, so Product first. For $\displaystyle \sin\!\left(x^{2}\right)$ it is the sine, so Chain first — and the squaring comes last.

My turn

From yesterday's quiz: first rule for $\displaystyle y = \frac{x^{2}}{3}$?

Show: look at what is downstairs

The number $\displaystyle 3$ — nothing is divided by a varying quantity. So

$$y = \tfrac13 x^{2} \qquad y' = \tfrac13\cdot 2x = \tfrac23 x$$

Power Rule, nothing else. The Quotient Rule gets there in four times the writing: not wrong, just not the first move.

Your turn

Name the rule you would use first. Do not differentiate anything.

$$\text{(1)}\;\; y = x^{2}\sin x \qquad \text{(2)}\;\; y = \sin\!\left(x^{2}\right) \qquad \text{(3)}\;\; y = \frac{\sin x}{x^{2}}$$
$$\text{(4)}\;\; y = \frac{x^{2}}{5} \qquad\qquad \text{(5)}\;\; y = \left(\frac{x+1}{x-1}\right)^{3}$$
Show: and the reason in each case

1. Product — two things multiplied.   2. Chain — one thing fed into another.   3. Quotient — one thing over another.

4. Power, not Quotient — the denominator is the constant $\displaystyle 5$, so $\displaystyle y = \tfrac15 x^{2}$. A fraction bar alone is no reason to reach for the Quotient Rule.

5. Chain first, Quotient inside it — the outermost operation is the cubing.

Ask one question: what is the LAST thing done to $\displaystyle x$? That is the outermost operation, and it names the rule.

Two Rules at Once

A composite inside a product, or a chain inside a chain. Start from the outside and work in.

My turn

$\displaystyle y = x^{3}\sin(4x)$. Outermost operation is a product.

$\displaystyle y'$$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle \frac{d}{dx}\left[x^{3}\right]\cdot\sin(4x) + x^{3}\cdot\frac{d}{dx}\left[\sin(4x)\right]$Product Rule — the outermost operation
$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle 3x^{2}\sin(4x) + x^{3}\cdot\left[\cos(4x)\cdot 4\right]$Chain Rule on $\displaystyle \sin(4x)$
$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle 3x^{2}\sin(4x) + 4x^{3}\cos(4x)$
Your turn

Now these two.

$$\text{(a)}\;\; y = \left(x^{2}+1\right)^{4}\cos x \qquad\qquad \text{(b)}\;\; y = \sin\!\left(\cos x\right)$$
Show: (a) product then chain, (b) chain inside chain

(a) The outermost operation is a product, so start there:

$$f = \left(x^{2}+1\right)^{4} \qquad\qquad g = \cos x,\quad g' = -\sin x$$

$\displaystyle f$ is a composite, so finding $\displaystyle f'$ needs the Chain Rule — outside $\displaystyle (\;)^{4}$, inside $\displaystyle x^{2}+1$:

$$f' = 4\left(x^{2}+1\right)^{3}\cdot\frac{d}{dx}\left(x^{2}+1\right) = 4\left(x^{2}+1\right)^{3}(2x) = 8x\left(x^{2}+1\right)^{3}$$

Now the Product Rule $\displaystyle \left(fg\right)' = f'g + fg'$:

$$y' = \underbrace{8x\left(x^{2}+1\right)^{3}}_{f'}\underbrace{\cos x}_{g} + \underbrace{\left(x^{2}+1\right)^{4}}_{f}\underbrace{\left(-\sin x\right)}_{g'}$$
$$y' = 8x\left(x^{2}+1\right)^{3}\cos x - \left(x^{2}+1\right)^{4}\sin x$$

(b) A composite with no product anywhere. Outside is $\displaystyle \sin$, inside is $\displaystyle \cos x$. The Chain Rule is

$$\frac{d}{dx}f\!\left(g(x)\right) = f'\!\left(g(x)\right)\cdot g'(x)$$

Here $\displaystyle f' = \cos$ and $\displaystyle g' = -\sin x$, so the outer derivative is evaluated at the inside:

$$y' = \cos\!\left(\cos x\right)\cdot\left(-\sin x\right) = -\sin x\,\cos\!\left(\cos x\right)$$

Note what did not happen: the outer $\displaystyle \cos$ is evaluated at $\displaystyle \cos x$, not at $\displaystyle x$.

Three Deep

The rule does not care how many layers there are. Peel one at a time, from the outside in.

Your turn

Differentiate $\displaystyle \;y = \sin\!\Big(\tan\!\big(\sqrt{x^{2}+5}\big)\Big)$. Work it before we go over it.

Show: one layer at a time

Name the layers from the outside in, by asking what you would do last when plugging a number in:

$$\text{outside } = \sin(\;) \qquad \text{then } \tan(\;) \qquad \text{then } \sqrt{\;} \qquad \text{innermost } = x^{2}+5$$

Now differentiate outside-in, multiplying as you go:

$$y' = \cos\!\Big(\tan\!\big(\sqrt{x^{2}+5}\big)\Big) \cdot \sec^{2}\!\big(\sqrt{x^{2}+5}\big) \cdot \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x^{2}+5}} \cdot 2x$$

The last two factors tidy into one:

$$y' = \frac{x\,\cos\!\Big(\tan\!\big(\sqrt{x^{2}+5}\big)\Big) \,\sec^{2}\!\big(\sqrt{x^{2}+5}\big)}{\sqrt{x^{2}+5}}$$

Each layer contributes one factor, and the inside of every factor is left exactly as it was. Count the layers before you start: three layers means three factors, and if your answer has two you dropped one.

The Factor You Can See Coming

When the Chain Rule runs inside a Product or Quotient Rule, the answer always factors.

My turn

A quotient with a chain inside each piece. Rule first, tidying after.

$$y = \frac{\left(x^{2}+3\right)^{5}}{\left(2x+5\right)^{4}} \qquad\qquad \text{hi} = \left(x^{2}+3\right)^{5}, \quad \text{lo} = \left(2x+5\right)^{4}$$
$\displaystyle y'$$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle \frac{\left(2x+5\right)^{4}\cdot\frac{d}{dx}\left(x^{2}+3\right)^{5} - \left(x^{2}+3\right)^{5}\cdot\frac{d}{dx}\left(2x+5\right)^{4}}{\left(2x+5\right)^{8}}$write the rule down before computing either derivative
$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle \frac{\left(2x+5\right)^{4}\cdot 5\left(x^{2}+3\right)^{4}(2x) - \left(x^{2}+3\right)^{5}\cdot 4\left(2x+5\right)^{3}(2)}{\left(2x+5\right)^{8}}$each D is its own Chain Rule
$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle \frac{10x\left(x^{2}+3\right)^{4}\left(2x+5\right)^{4} - 8\left(x^{2}+3\right)^{5}\left(2x+5\right)^{3}}{\left(2x+5\right)^{8}}$constants only — do not multiply out
$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle \frac{2\left(x^{2}+3\right)^{4}\left(2x+5\right)^{3}\left[\,5x\left(2x+5\right)-4\left(x^{2}+3\right)\,\right]}{\left(2x+5\right)^{8}}$factor: lower power of each repeated piece, and the common $\displaystyle 2$
$\displaystyle =$$\displaystyle \frac{2\left(x^{2}+3\right)^{4}\left(6x^{2}+25x-12\right)}{\left(2x+5\right)^{5}}$bracket is $\displaystyle 10x^{2}+25x-4x^{2}-12$, and $\displaystyle \left(2x+5\right)^{3}$ cancels

You can call the factor in advance. The Chain Rule on $\displaystyle \left(\text{stuff}\right)^{n}$ leaves $\displaystyle \left(\text{stuff}\right)^{n-1}$, one power below the copy in the other term — so the lower power is a common factor every time. Predict it, then confirm it; that is how you catch an algebra slip. And §4.1 asks where this is zero, which only the factored form answers.

My turn

Watch one. Let $\displaystyle h(x) = f\!\left(g(x)\right)$. Find $\displaystyle h'(2)$.

write the rule first, then read the table

$\displaystyle h'(x) = f'\!\left(g(x)\right)\cdot g'(x)$the rule, before touching the table
$\displaystyle h'(2) = f'\!\left(g(2)\right)\cdot g'(2)$at $\displaystyle x=2$
$\displaystyle h'(2) = f'(3)\cdot 7$the table says $\displaystyle g(2)=3$ — so $\displaystyle f'(3)$, not $\displaystyle f'(2)$

Your turn

Your turn. Find $\displaystyle h'(1)$.

Show: write the rule first, then fill it in

The rule says $\displaystyle h'(x) = f'\!\left(g(x)\right)\cdot g'(x)$, so

$$h'(1) = f'\!\left(g(1)\right)\cdot g'(1)$$

From the table, $\displaystyle g(1) = 2$. So the first factor is $\displaystyle f'(2) = 6$, not $\displaystyle f'(1)$. And $\displaystyle g'(1) = 5$.

$$h'(1) = 6 \cdot 5 = \mathbf{30}$$

The whole question is which row you read $\displaystyle f'$ from — the row numbered by $\displaystyle g(1)$, not by $\displaystyle 1$. Writing the rule down first is what stops the mistake.

How You Show a Statement Is False

Question 1 yesterday said

$$\frac{d}{dx}\!\left[\frac{f}{g}\right] = \frac{f'}{g'}$$

Most of you answered by writing the correct Quotient Rule. That earns the credit — and it is not yet a proof.

My turn

Watch that argument fail.

Show: pick an f and g, compute the left side

$\displaystyle f = e^{4x}$, $\displaystyle g = e^{2x}$. Then $\displaystyle \frac{f}{g} = e^{2x}$, so

$$\frac{d}{dx}\!\left[\frac{f}{g}\right] = 2e^{2x}$$
Show: now the right side

$\displaystyle f' = 4e^{4x}$, $\displaystyle g' = 2e^{2x}$, so

$$\frac{f'}{g'} = \frac{4e^{4x}}{2e^{2x}} = 2e^{2x}$$

The false rule got the right answer.

Show: so what actually kills a statement

“The real rule looks different” settles nothing — two formulas that look nothing alike can agree. You need one case where the two sides disagree.

Pick an $\displaystyle f$ and $\displaystyle g$ you can compute both sides of, compute both, point at the disagreement. One case is enough. $\displaystyle x$ and $\displaystyle x^{2}$ are the cheapest — but not ones so simple that both sides collapse to the same thing.

Your turn

Show that $\displaystyle \frac{d}{dx}\left[f(x)g(x)\right] = f'(x)g'(x)$ is false.

Show: one case, and a number at the end

Take $\displaystyle f = g = x$. Then $\displaystyle fg = x^{2}$, so the left side is $\displaystyle 2x$; the right side is $\displaystyle f'g' = 1\cdot 1 = 1$. At $\displaystyle x = 3$: $\displaystyle 6$ against $\displaystyle 1$. They disagree, so the statement is false.

Any Base at All

§3.1 left a loose end. The Power Rule cannot touch $\displaystyle 2^{x}$ — the variable is in the exponent.

Every exponential is $\displaystyle e$ to something. Since $\displaystyle a = e^{\ln a}$,

$$a^{x} \;=\; \left(e^{\ln a}\right)^{x} \;=\; e^{\,x\ln a}$$

and a composite is something you can already differentiate.

My turn

So what is $\displaystyle \frac{d}{dx}2^{x}$?

rewrite, chain, rewrite back

$\displaystyle y = 2^{x} = e^{\,x\ln 2}$rewrite with base $\displaystyle e$
$\displaystyle \text{outside } = e^{(\;)} \qquad \text{inside } = x\ln 2$$\displaystyle \ln 2$ is a number, about $\displaystyle 0.693$
$\displaystyle \frac{d}{dx}e^{\,x\ln 2} = e^{\,x\ln 2}\cdot\frac{d}{dx}\left(x\ln 2\right) = e^{\,x\ln 2}\cdot\ln 2$Chain Rule: $\displaystyle e^{(\;)}$ is its own derivative, and the inside is a number times $\displaystyle x$
$\displaystyle \frac{d}{dx}2^{x} = 2^{x}\ln 2$$\displaystyle e^{\,x\ln 2}$ is where we started — put $\displaystyle 2^{x}$ back
Theorem — Derivative of a General Exponential

For any base $\displaystyle a > 0$,

$$\frac{d}{dx}\,a^{x} \;=\; a^{x}\ln a$$

Check it on a base you know. At $\displaystyle a = e$, $\displaystyle \ln e = 1$, so the rule gives $\displaystyle e^{x}\cdot 1 = e^{x}$. §3.1 was the special case all along — $\displaystyle e$ is the base whose $\displaystyle \ln$ is $\displaystyle 1$.

Your turn

Differentiate.

$$\text{(a)}\;\; y = 3^{x} \qquad \text{(b)}\;\; y = 2^{5x} \qquad \text{(c)}\;\; y = x\,4^{x}$$
Show: (a) straight off the rule
$$\frac{d}{dx}3^{x} = 3^{x}\ln 3$$
Show: (b) the rule, then the Chain Rule again

Outside $\displaystyle 2^{(\;)}$, inside $\displaystyle 5x$:

$$\frac{d}{dx}2^{5x} = \underbrace{2^{5x}\ln 2}_{\text{outside}}\cdot \underbrace{\frac{d}{dx}(5x)}_{\text{inside}} = 5\ln 2\,\cdot 2^{5x}$$
Show: (c) a product, with an exponential in it

$\displaystyle f = x$, $\displaystyle f' = 1$;   $\displaystyle g = 4^{x}$, $\displaystyle g' = 4^{x}\ln 4$. Then $\displaystyle f'g + fg'$:

$$y' = (1)\left(4^{x}\right) + (x)\left(4^{x}\ln 4\right) = 4^{x} + x\,4^{x}\ln 4$$

Both terms carry $\displaystyle 4^{x}$, so it factors:

$$y' = 4^{x}\left(1 + x\ln 4\right)$$

Worked for You: A Tangent Line

Not done in class. Read it, then practise it in the online set — every problem there asks for the same three numbers.

Find the equation of the tangent line to $\displaystyle y = \sin^{2}x$ at $\displaystyle x = \frac{\pi}{4}$.

Show: the three numbers, then the line

First rewrite it so the composite is visible: $\displaystyle y = \sin^{2}x = \left(\sin x\right)^{2}$.

Start from the equation of any line through $\displaystyle (x_0,y_0)$ with slope $\displaystyle m$:

$$y - y_0 = m\left(x - x_0\right)$$

Three numbers are needed, and none of them may still contain an $\displaystyle x$.

  • $\displaystyle x_0 = \frac{\pi}{4}$, given.
  • $\displaystyle y_0 = \sin^{2}\!\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right) = \left(\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\right)^{2} = \frac{2}{4} = \frac12$.
  • $\displaystyle m = y'\!\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right)$. The slope function is $\displaystyle y' = 2\left(\sin x\right)^{1}\cdot\cos x = 2\sin x\cos x$, so

    $$y'\!\left(\tfrac{\pi}{4}\right) = 2\cdot\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\cdot\frac{\sqrt2}{2} = 2\cdot\frac{2}{4} = 1$$

Substitute all three:

$$y - \frac12 = 1\left(x - \frac{\pi}{4}\right) \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad y = x - \frac{\pi}{4} + \frac12$$

Four things had to go right: rewriting $\displaystyle \sin^{2}x$ as $\displaystyle \left(\sin x\right)^{2}$, the Chain Rule, the unit circle at $\displaystyle \frac{\pi}{4}$, and evaluating before assembling the line. Miss any one and the answer is not a line.

What You Should Be Able to Do

  • Look at a function and say what is inside and what is outside — the calculator test: which button first?
  • Differentiate a composite: outer derivative evaluated at the inner function, times the inner derivative.
  • Say why $\displaystyle \cos\!\left(x^{3}\right)$ is not the derivative of $\displaystyle \sin\!\left(x^{3}\right)$.
  • Explain in one sentence why the rule is a product — rates multiply through a chain.
  • Choose between Power, Product, Quotient and Chain by asking what the last thing done to $\displaystyle x$ is.
  • Combine the Chain Rule with the Product and Quotient Rules, and handle a composite inside a composite.
  • Use the Chain Rule on a function given only by a table.
  • Differentiate any exponential: rewrite $\displaystyle a^{x} = e^{\,x\ln a}$, or quote $\displaystyle \frac{d}{dx}a^{x} = a^{x}\ln a$ — and say why $\displaystyle a=e$ makes $\displaystyle \ln a = 1$.

§3.5 is implicit differentiation — what you do when the equation will not solve for $\displaystyle y$. The Chain Rule is the entire engine of it, so this section does not end here.

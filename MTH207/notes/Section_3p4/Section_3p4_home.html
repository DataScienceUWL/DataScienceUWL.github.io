Something You Cannot Differentiate Yet

You finished §3.3 with six trig derivatives and three rules. Here are two functions. One of them you can already handle.

Your turn

Differentiate each, or get as far as you can.

$$\text{(a)}\;\; y = \left(x^{2}+1\right)^{3} \qquad\qquad \text{(b)}\;\; y = \sin\!\left(x^{3}\right)$$

For (a), do not multiply it out. Write it as $\displaystyle \left(x^{2}+1\right)\left(x^{2}+1\right)\left(x^{2}+1\right)$ and use yesterday's three-factor Product Rule.

(a) Three factors, all the same. Name them and their derivatives:

$$f = g = h = x^{2}+1 \qquad\qquad f' = g' = h' = 2x$$

Yesterday's rule for a product of three:

$$\left(fgh\right)' = f'gh + fg'h + fgh'$$

Substituting — and every term comes out the same, because all three factors are identical:

$$y' = \underbrace{(2x)\left(x^{2}+1\right)\left(x^{2}+1\right)}_{\text{first differentiated}} + \underbrace{\left(x^{2}+1\right)(2x)\left(x^{2}+1\right)}_{\text{second}} + \underbrace{\left(x^{2}+1\right)\left(x^{2}+1\right)(2x)}_{\text{third}}$$

Three copies of the same thing, so collect them:

$$y' = 3\left(x^{2}+1\right)^{2}\cdot 2x$$

Look at what that says: the exponent $\displaystyle 3$ came down, the inside was left alone, and it is multiplied by the derivative of the inside. Hold on to that.

(b) There is nothing to multiply out. $\displaystyle \sin\!\left(x^{3}\right)$ is not a product, not a quotient, and not a power of $\displaystyle x$. Every rule you own needs the function to be built out of pieces that are added, multiplied or divided — and this one is built by substitution: take $\displaystyle x$, cube it, then take the sine of the result.

A function built by feeding one function into another is called a composite. You have no rule for composites. That is the whole of §3.4. After that we have only to determine how to differentiate logarithms and $\displaystyle a^x$ - then we'll have rules for all standard functions.

Inside and Outside

Before differentiating one, you have to be able to see one. Every composite has an inside and an outside: the inside is what happens to $\displaystyle x$ first, the outside is what happens to that result.

My turn

The last row is the one that costs points. Watch $\displaystyle y = \tan^{2}x$ once.

First, rewrite it so the composition is visible. $\displaystyle \tan^{2}x$ does not mean the tangent of $\displaystyle x^{2}$ — it means the tangent, then squared:

$$y = \tan^{2}x \;=\; \left(\tan x\right)^{2}$$

Now the inside and outside are in plain sight:

$$\text{inside } g(x) = \tan x \qquad\qquad \text{outside } f(u) = u^{2}$$

Chain Rule: derivative of the outside, inside left alone, times the derivative of the inside — and $\displaystyle \frac{d}{dx}\tan x = \sec^{2}x$:

$$y' = 2\left(\tan x\right)^{1}\cdot\sec^{2}x \;=\; 2\tan x\,\sec^{2}x$$

Every trig power is written backwards. $\displaystyle \sin^{3}x$, $\displaystyle \cos^{2}x$, $\displaystyle \sec^{4}x$ — the exponent is printed on the function name but it applies to the output. Before you differentiate one, rewrite it with the parentheses: $\displaystyle \left(\sin x\right)^{3}$, $\displaystyle \left(\cos x\right)^{2}$, $\displaystyle \left(\sec x\right)^{4}$. It costs one line and it makes the composite impossible to miss.

The Chain Rule

Theorem — The Chain Rule

If $\displaystyle g$ is differentiable at $\displaystyle x$ and $\displaystyle f$ is differentiable at $\displaystyle g(x)$, then

$$\frac{d}{dx}\,f\!\left(g(x)\right) \;=\; f'\!\left(g(x)\right)\cdot g'(x)$$

Same rule, in the words you have been using all period:

$$\frac{d}{dx}\,\text{out}\!\left(\text{in}(x)\right) \;=\; \text{out}'\!\left(\text{in}(x)\right)\cdot\text{in}'(x)$$

Derivative of the outside, left alone on the inside, times the derivative of the inside. The part everyone drops is the second factor. The part everyone gets wrong is the first: it is $\displaystyle f'$ evaluated at $\displaystyle g(x)$, not at $\displaystyle x$ — which the second line says out loud: $\displaystyle \text{out}'$ is evaluated at $\displaystyle \text{in}(x)$.

Back to the wall from the start of class:

$$\frac{d}{dx}\sin\!\left(x^{3}\right) \;=\; \underbrace{\cos\!\left(x^{3}\right)}_{\text{outside}'\text{, at the inside}}\cdot\underbrace{3x^{2}}_{\text{inside}'}$$

And back to the one you did by hand:

$$\frac{d}{dx}\left(x^{2}+1\right)^{3} \;=\; 3\left(x^{2}+1\right)^{2}\cdot 2x$$

which is exactly what multiplying out and differentiating gave you.

$\displaystyle \cos\!\left(x^{3}\right)$ alone is wrong. It is the most common error in this section by a distance. The inside leaves a fingerprint — if $\displaystyle g'(x)$ is not in your answer, you have not used the rule.

Now You: Outside, Then Inside

Your turn

Differentiate. For each one, say the inside out loud before you write anything.

$$\text{(a)}\;\; y = \left(3x+1\right)^{4} \qquad \text{(b)}\;\; y = \sin\!\left(5x\right) \qquad \text{(c)}\;\; y = e^{\,x^{2}+\sin x} \qquad \text{(d)}\;\; y = \sqrt{x^{2}+9}$$

(a) inside $\displaystyle 3x+1$, outside $\displaystyle u^{4}$: $\displaystyle \;y' = 4\left(3x+1\right)^{3}\cdot 3 = 12\left(3x+1\right)^{3}$

(b) inside $\displaystyle 5x$, outside $\displaystyle \sin u$: $\displaystyle \;y' = \cos(5x)\cdot 5 = 5\cos(5x)$

(c) inside $\displaystyle x^{2}+\sin x$, outside $\displaystyle e^{(\;)}$. The inside is itself a sum, so differentiate it term by term: $\frac{d}{dx}\left(x^{2}+\sin x\right) = 2x+\cos x$. Then

$$y' = e^{\,x^{2}+\sin x}\cdot\left(2x+\cos x\right) \;=\; \left(2x+\cos x\right)e^{\,x^{2}+\sin x}$$

(d) rewrite as $\displaystyle \left(x^{2}+9\right)^{1/2}$, inside $\displaystyle x^{2}+9$:

$$y' = \tfrac12\left(x^{2}+9\right)^{-1/2}\cdot 2x \;=\; \dfrac{x}{\sqrt{x^{2}+9}}$$

Every one of these is two steps and a multiplication. If your answer does not have the inside's derivative multiplied onto it, go back.

Now You: A Tangent Line

Your turn

Find the equation of the tangent line to $\displaystyle y = \sin^{2}x$ at $\displaystyle x = \frac{\pi}{4}$.

First rewrite it so the composite is visible: $\displaystyle y = \sin^{2}x = \left(\sin x\right)^{2}$.

Start from the equation of any line through $\displaystyle (x_0,y_0)$ with slope $\displaystyle m$:

$$y - y_0 = m\left(x - x_0\right)$$

Three numbers are needed, and none of them may still contain an $\displaystyle x$.

  • $\displaystyle x_0 = \frac{\pi}{4}$, given.
  • $y_0 = \sin^{2}\!\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right) = \left(\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\right)^{2} = \frac{2}{4} = \frac12$.
  • $\displaystyle m = y'\!\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right)$. The slope function is $\displaystyle y' = 2\left(\sin x\right)^{1}\cdot\cos x = 2\sin x\cos x$, so

    $$y'\!\left(\tfrac{\pi}{4}\right) = 2\cdot\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\cdot\frac{\sqrt2}{2} = 2\cdot\frac{2}{4} = 1$$

Substitute all three:

$$y - \frac12 = 1\left(x - \frac{\pi}{4}\right) \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad y = x - \frac{\pi}{4} + \frac12$$

Four things had to go right: rewriting $\displaystyle \sin^{2}x$ as $\displaystyle \left(\sin x\right)^{2}$, the Chain Rule, the unit circle at $\displaystyle \frac{\pi}{4}$, and evaluating before assembling the line. Miss any one and the answer is not a line.

Why It Is a Product

The derivation says the rule is true. This says why it had to be.

A derivative is a rate: how fast one thing changes compared to another. Suppose

$$y \text{ changes } 3 \text{ times as fast as } u, \qquad\qquad u \text{ changes } 2 \text{ times as fast as } x.$$
Your turn

How many times as fast as $\displaystyle x$ does $\displaystyle y$ change? Then write one sentence saying why — not the number, the reason.

$\displaystyle \mathbf{6}$. Each unit of $\displaystyle x$ moves $\displaystyle u$ by $\displaystyle 2$; each unit of $\displaystyle u$ moves $\displaystyle y$ by $\displaystyle 3$; so each unit of $\displaystyle x$ moves $\displaystyle y$ by $\displaystyle 3 \times 2 = 6$.

In Leibniz notation that sentence is the whole rule:

$$\frac{dy}{dx} \;=\; \frac{dy}{du}\cdot\frac{du}{dx}$$

and written that way it looks like the $\displaystyle du$'s cancel. They are not fractions and nothing is canceling — but the notation was built to make the rule look inevitable, and it is a good way to remember it.

Rates multiply through a chain. That is the idea; the limit argument we ran through earlier is the bookkeeping.

Which Rule, Before Any Work

You now own four rules: Power, Product, Quotient, Chain. Picking the right one is a separate skill from running it — and it is the one that decides most exam questions.

Your turn

Name the rule you would use first. Do not differentiate anything.

  1. $\displaystyle y = x^{2}\sin x$
  2. $\displaystyle y = \sin\!\left(x^{2}\right)$
  3. $\displaystyle y = \dfrac{\sin x}{x^{2}}$
  4. $\displaystyle y = \dfrac{x^{2}}{5}$
  5. $\displaystyle y = \left(\dfrac{x+1}{x-1}\right)^{3}$

1. Product — two things multiplied.   2. Chain — one thing fed into another.   3. Quotient — one thing over another.

4. Power, not Quotient. The denominator is the constant $\displaystyle 5$, so this is $\displaystyle \tfrac15 x^{2}$ and $\displaystyle y' = \tfrac25 x$. A fraction bar is not by itself a reason to reach for the Quotient Rule.

5. Chain first, then Quotient inside it. The outermost operation is the cubing, so that is where you start — and the inside needs its own rule to differentiate.

Ask one question: what is the LAST thing done to $\displaystyle x$? That is the outermost operation, and it names the rule.

Two Rules at Once

Most real problems are a composite inside a product, or a chain inside a chain. The method does not change: start from the outside and work in.

Now these two.

$$\text{(a)}\;\; y = \left(x^{2}+1\right)^{4}\cos x \qquad\qquad \text{(b)}\;\; y = \sin\!\left(\cos x\right)$$

(a) The outermost operation is a product, so start there:

$$f = \left(x^{2}+1\right)^{4} \qquad\qquad g = \cos x,\quad g' = -\sin x$$

$\displaystyle f$ is a composite, so finding $\displaystyle f'$ needs the Chain Rule — outside $\displaystyle (\;)^{4}$, inside $\displaystyle x^{2}+1$:

$$f' = 4\left(x^{2}+1\right)^{3}\cdot\frac{d}{dx}\left(x^{2}+1\right) = 4\left(x^{2}+1\right)^{3}(2x) = 8x\left(x^{2}+1\right)^{3}$$

Now the Product Rule $\displaystyle \left(fg\right)' = f'g + fg'$:

$$y' = \underbrace{8x\left(x^{2}+1\right)^{3}}_{f'}\underbrace{\cos x}_{g} + \underbrace{\left(x^{2}+1\right)^{4}}_{f}\underbrace{\left(-\sin x\right)}_{g'}$$
$$y' = 8x\left(x^{2}+1\right)^{3}\cos x - \left(x^{2}+1\right)^{4}\sin x$$

(b) A composite with no product anywhere. Outside is $\displaystyle \sin$, inside is $\displaystyle \cos x$. The Chain Rule is

$$\frac{d}{dx}f\!\left(g(x)\right) = f'\!\left(g(x)\right)\cdot g'(x)$$

Here $\displaystyle f' = \cos$ and $\displaystyle g' = -\sin x$, so the outer derivative is evaluated at the inside:

$$y' = \cos\!\left(\cos x\right)\cdot\left(-\sin x\right) = -\sin x\,\cos\!\left(\cos x\right)$$

Note what did not happen: the outer $\displaystyle \cos$ is evaluated at $\displaystyle \cos x$, not at $\displaystyle x$.

The Factor You Can See Coming

When the Chain Rule runs inside a Product or Quotient Rule, the answer always factors — and you can say what the common factor is before you differentiate anything.

The Chain Rule on $\displaystyle \left(\text{stuff}\right)^{n}$ leaves $\displaystyle \left(\text{stuff}\right)^{n-1}$ — one power lower than the copy sitting in the other term. So the lower power is a common factor, every time.

Your turn

Let $\displaystyle h(x) = f\!\left(g(x)\right)$. Find $\displaystyle h'(1)$.

The rule says $\displaystyle h'(x) = f'\!\left(g(x)\right)\cdot g'(x)$, so

$$h'(1) = f'\!\left(g(1)\right)\cdot g'(1)$$

From the table, $\displaystyle g(1) = 2$. So the first factor is $\displaystyle f'(2) = 6$, not $\displaystyle f'(1)$. And $\displaystyle g'(1) = 5$.

$$h'(1) = 6 \cdot 5 = \mathbf{30}$$

The whole question is which row you read $\displaystyle f'$ from. It is the row numbered by $\displaystyle g(1)$, not by $\displaystyle 1$. Writing $\displaystyle f'\!\left(g(x)\right)\cdot g'(x)$ down before touching the table is what stops the mistake.

Any Base at All

§3.1 left one loose end. You know $\displaystyle \frac{d}{dx}e^{x} = e^{x}$, and you know the Power Rule cannot touch $\displaystyle 2^{x}$ — the variable is in the exponent. So what is $\displaystyle \frac{d}{dx}2^{x}$?

The move is to notice that every exponential is $\displaystyle e$ to something. Since $\displaystyle a = e^{\ln a}$,

$$a^{x} \;=\; \left(e^{\ln a}\right)^{x} \;=\; e^{\,x\ln a}$$

and that is a composite — which you can now differentiate.

My turn

Watch $\displaystyle 2^{x}$ once, all the way through.

Rewrite it as $\displaystyle e$ to something:

$$y = 2^{x} = e^{\,x\ln 2}$$

Now it is a composite. Remember $\displaystyle \ln 2$ is a number, about $\displaystyle 0.693$:

$$\text{inside } = x\ln 2 \qquad\qquad \text{outside } = e^{(\;)}$$

Chain Rule: derivative of the outside, inside left alone, times the derivative of the inside. The outside $\displaystyle e^{(\;)}$ is its own derivative, and the inside is a number times $\displaystyle x$:

$$\frac{d}{dx}e^{\,x\ln 2} = e^{\,x\ln 2}\cdot\frac{d}{dx}\left(x\ln 2\right) = e^{\,x\ln 2}\cdot\ln 2$$

And $\displaystyle e^{\,x\ln 2}$ is what we started with, so put $\displaystyle 2^{x}$ back:

$$\frac{d}{dx}2^{x} \;=\; 2^{x}\ln 2$$
Theorem — Derivative of a General Exponential

For any base $\displaystyle a > 0$,

$$\frac{d}{dx}\,a^{x} \;=\; a^{x}\ln a$$

Check it against what you already know. Put $\displaystyle a = e$: then $\displaystyle \ln e = 1$ and the rule gives $\displaystyle \frac{d}{dx}e^{x} = e^{x}\cdot 1 = e^{x}$. The rule you learned in §3.1 was the special case all along — $\displaystyle e$ is the base whose $\displaystyle \ln$ is $\displaystyle 1$, which is exactly why it is the convenient one.

Your turn

Differentiate.

$$\text{(a)}\;\; y = 3^{x} \qquad \text{(b)}\;\; y = 2^{5x} \qquad \text{(c)}\;\; y = x\,4^{x}$$
$$\frac{d}{dx}3^{x} = 3^{x}\ln 3$$

Outside is $\displaystyle 2^{(\;)}$, inside is $\displaystyle 5x$:

$$\frac{d}{dx}2^{5x} = \underbrace{2^{5x}\ln 2}_{\text{derivative of the outside}}\cdot \underbrace{\frac{d}{dx}(5x)}_{\text{derivative of the inside}} = 2^{5x}\ln 2 \cdot 5$$
$$\frac{d}{dx}2^{5x} = 5\ln 2\,\cdot 2^{5x}$$

Name the factors: $\displaystyle f = x$ so $\displaystyle f' = 1$;   $\displaystyle g = 4^{x}$ so $\displaystyle g' = 4^{x}\ln 4$. The Product Rule is $\displaystyle \left(fg\right)' = f'g + fg'$:

$$y' = (1)\left(4^{x}\right) + (x)\left(4^{x}\ln 4\right) = 4^{x} + x\,4^{x}\ln 4$$

Both terms carry $\displaystyle 4^{x}$, so it factors:

$$y' = 4^{x}\left(1 + x\ln 4\right)$$

What You Should Be Able to Do

  • Look at a function and say what is inside and what is outside — the calculator test: which button first?
  • Differentiate a composite: outer derivative evaluated at the inner function, times the inner derivative.
  • Say why $\displaystyle \cos\!\left(x^{3}\right)$ is not the derivative of $\displaystyle \sin\!\left(x^{3}\right)$.
  • Explain in one sentence why the rule is a product — rates multiply through a chain.
  • Choose between Power, Product, Quotient and Chain by asking what the last thing done to $\displaystyle x$ is.
  • Combine the Chain Rule with the Product and Quotient Rules, and handle a composite inside a composite.
  • Use the Chain Rule on a function given only by a table.
  • Differentiate any exponential: rewrite $\displaystyle a^{x} = e^{\,x\ln a}$, or quote $\displaystyle \frac{d}{dx}a^{x} = a^{x}\ln a$ — and say why $\displaystyle a=e$ makes $\displaystyle \ln a = 1$.

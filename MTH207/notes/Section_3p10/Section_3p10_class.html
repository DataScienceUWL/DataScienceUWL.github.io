3.10  Linear Approximation and Differentials

MTH 207 Calculus I — Differentiation Rules

What the Tangent Line Was For

For five weeks we have computed tangent lines. Here is what they are for.

A tangent line is something you can evaluate with arithmetic. The function usually is not — nobody computes $\displaystyle \sqrt{9.2}$ by hand. But near the point of tangency, the line and the curve are the same thing.

Zoom in far enough and every differentiable function looks like its tangent line. That is what differentiable means. The approximation is good near $\displaystyle a$ and gets worse as you leave — which is the opposite of what a surprising number of students write down.

It Is Point-Slope. That Is the Whole Formula

Start from the equation of any line through $\displaystyle (x_0,y_0)$ with slope $\displaystyle m$ — §2.1, unchanged:

$$y - y_0 = m\left(x - x_0\right)$$

We want the line that touches $\displaystyle y=f(x)$ at $\displaystyle x=a$. So three things get named, and none of them is new:

  • $\displaystyle x_0 = a$, the point you are approximating near.
  • $\displaystyle y_0 = f(a)$.   From $\displaystyle f$, not from $\displaystyle f'$.
  • $\displaystyle m = f'(a)$, the slope the derivative was invented to give you.

Substitute and solve for $\displaystyle y$:

$$y = f(a) + f'(a)\left(x - a\right)$$
Definition — Linearization

The linearization of $\displaystyle f$ at $\displaystyle a$ is

$$L(x) = f(a) + f'(a)\left(x - a\right),$$

and the approximation $\displaystyle f(x) \approx L(x)$ for $\displaystyle x$ near $\displaystyle a$ is called the linear approximation of $\displaystyle f$ at $\displaystyle a$.

$\displaystyle L$ is not a new object. It is the tangent line at $\displaystyle a$, written as a function so you can plug numbers into it.

Watch One: $\sqrt{9.2}$

My turn

Find the linearization of $\displaystyle f(x)=\sqrt{x}$ at $\displaystyle a=9$, and use it to approximate $\displaystyle \sqrt{9.2}$. Watch this one all the way through. You get the next one.

Show: why $\displaystyle a=9$, and not something closer to $\displaystyle 9.2$

$\displaystyle a$ has to be a number where $\displaystyle f(a)$ and $\displaystyle f'(a)$ are exact and easy. $\displaystyle 9$ is the nearest perfect square to $\displaystyle 9.2$, so $\displaystyle \sqrt{9}=3$ costs nothing. Choosing $\displaystyle a=9.2$ would be circular — it is the value we are trying to find.

the linearization, one move at a time

$\displaystyle f(x) = \sqrt{x}, \qquad a = 9$what we are given
$\displaystyle f(9) = \sqrt{9} = 3$$\displaystyle f(a)$, the height
$\displaystyle f(x) = x^{1/2}$rewrite the root as a power for differentiating
$\displaystyle f'(x) = \tfrac{1}{2}x^{-1/2}$Power Rule: $\displaystyle \frac{d}{dx}x^{n} = nx^{n-1}$
$\displaystyle f'(x) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}$back to a root, so it is easy to evaluate
$\displaystyle f'(9) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{9}} = \frac{1}{6}$$\displaystyle f'(a)$, the slope
$\displaystyle L(x) = f(a) + f'(a)\left(x-a\right)$the linearization, written before any number goes in
$\displaystyle L(x) = 3 + \frac{1}{6}\left(x - 9\right)$the three numbers into their slots
$\displaystyle L(9.2) = 3 + \frac{1}{6}\left(0.2\right) = 3 + \frac{1}{30} \approx 3.0333$evaluate at the number we actually wanted

A calculator gives $\displaystyle \sqrt{9.2} = 3.03315\ldots$, so one subtraction and one division got within $\displaystyle 0.0002$ — the first three decimal places are right.

Now You Finish It: $\sqrt[3]{27.5}$

Same shape, one rung up. I have done the two derivative computations for you — you write $\displaystyle L$ and evaluate it.

$$f(x) = \sqrt[3]{x} = x^{1/3}, \qquad a = 27$$
$$f(27) = 3, \qquad f'(x) = \tfrac{1}{3}x^{-2/3}, \qquad f'(27) = \frac{1}{3\left(27\right)^{2/3}} = \frac{1}{27}$$
Your turn

Write $\displaystyle L(x)$, then use it to approximate $\displaystyle \sqrt[3]{27.5}$.

Show: $\displaystyle L$, then the number
$$L(x) = 3 + \frac{1}{27}\left(x - 27\right)$$
$$L(27.5) = 3 + \frac{1}{27}\left(0.5\right) = 3 + \frac{1}{54} \approx 3.0185$$

The true value is $\displaystyle 3.01841\ldots$, so this one is within $\displaystyle 0.0001$ — closer than the square root was, off a point that is further away.

Nobody Hands You $f$ and $a$

In the homework and on the exam the problem says estimate this number. It does not say which function, and it does not say which $\displaystyle a$.

Read the number you want as $\displaystyle f$ evaluated somewhere awkward. Then let $\displaystyle a$ be the nearest place where $\displaystyle f(a)$ and $\displaystyle f'(a)$ are exact.

Your turn

Estimate $\displaystyle e^{0.1}$.

Show: choose, then compute

$\displaystyle f(x) = e^{x}$ and $\displaystyle a = 0$, because $\displaystyle e^{0}=1$ exactly.

$$f'(x) = e^{x}, \qquad f'(0) = e^{0} = 1$$
$$L(x) = 1 + 1\left(x - 0\right) = 1 + x$$
$$L(0.1) = 1.1$$

True value $\displaystyle 1.10517\ldots$, so this one came out low.

Your turn

Estimate $\displaystyle \tan\left(46^{\circ}\right)$. Something has to happen before you can start. What?

Show: first, the thing that has to happen

Degrees are not radians. $\displaystyle \frac{d}{dx}\tan x = \sec^{2}x$ is true only when $\displaystyle x$ is in radians, so convert before anything else:

$$46^{\circ} = \frac{46\pi}{180}, \qquad 45^{\circ} = \frac{\pi}{4} \approx 0.7854$$
Show: now it is an ordinary one

$\displaystyle f(x) = \tan x$ and $\displaystyle a = \frac{\pi}{4}$, where the tangent is exactly $\displaystyle 1$.

$$f'(x) = \sec^{2}x, \qquad f'\!\left(\tfrac{\pi}{4}\right) = \left(\sqrt{2}\right)^{2} = 2$$
$$L(x) = 1 + 2\left(x - \tfrac{\pi}{4}\right)$$

The gap is one degree, which is $\displaystyle \frac{\pi}{180} \approx 0.01745$ radians:

$$L \approx 1 + 2\left(0.01745\right) = 1.0349$$

True value $\displaystyle 1.03553\ldots$ — low again.

Over or Under? Look at the Bend

Three estimates so far. $\displaystyle \sqrt{9.2}$ came out high; $\displaystyle e^{0.1}$ and $\displaystyle \tan 46^{\circ}$ came out low. That is not luck, and you can see why without computing anything.

Your turn

Sketch $\displaystyle y=\sqrt{x}$ and its tangent line at $\displaystyle (9,3)$. Does the tangent line sit above the curve or below it? What does that tell you about the estimate $\displaystyle 3.0333$?

Show: the picture answers it

$\displaystyle \sqrt{x}$ keeps rising but flattens out — it bends downward as you go right. A straight line cannot flatten, so once it touches at $\displaystyle (9,3)$ the curve falls away beneath it on both sides and the line stays above. So $\displaystyle L(9.2)$ is bigger than $\displaystyle \sqrt{9.2}$: an overestimate. And indeed $\displaystyle 3.03333 > 3.03315$.

Show: the general picture — it goes both ways

A curve that bends up leaves its tangent line underneath — the estimate is too small. A curve that bends down leaves its tangent line on top — the estimate is too big.

So “a linear approximation always underestimates” is false. Which way it errs depends on which way the graph bends, and you read that off a sketch — not by computing the true value, which is the thing you could not compute in the first place.

Show: a name for it, and a promise

The two shapes have names: a curve bending up is concave up, one bending down is concave down. In §4.3 we will find a test that decides which, using the second derivative $\displaystyle f''$ — the derivative of the derivative, which you have already computed. For now the sketch is the whole method, and it is enough.

Differentials: the Same Idea, New Notation

We did not cover this in class, and it is not on Exam 2. It is here because the textbook's §3.10 includes it and it is worth a read — but nothing in Chapter 3 or 4 needs it, and the notation returns properly when we reach substitution in §5.5.

Rearrange the linear approximation. If $\displaystyle x$ moves from $\displaystyle a$ to $\displaystyle a + dx$, then

$$f(a + dx) - f(a) \;\approx\; f'(a)\,dx.$$

The left side is the true change in $\displaystyle y$; call it $\displaystyle \Delta y$. The right side gets its own name.

Definition — Differentials

For $\displaystyle y = f(x)$, the differential $\displaystyle dy$ is defined by

$$dy = f'(x)\,dx,$$

where $\displaystyle dx$ is any change in $\displaystyle x$. Then $\displaystyle \Delta y \approx dy$ for small $\displaystyle dx$.

$\displaystyle dy$ is the rise along the tangent line; $\displaystyle \Delta y$ is the rise along the curve. The whole section is the claim that these are close when $\displaystyle dx$ is small.

Here $\displaystyle \Delta y$ is the larger of the two, and you already know why: this curve bends upward, so the tangent line sits below it and $\displaystyle dy$ falls short. Bend it the other way and $\displaystyle dy$ would overshoot — the same picture as “Over or Under?”, in the other notation.

What an Error in the Measurement Costs You

A sphere's radius is measured as $\displaystyle 21$ cm, with a possible error of at most $\displaystyle 0.05$ cm. How far off could the computed volume be?

My turn

Watch this one. It is the first time the notation does a job that $\displaystyle L(x)$ would not do as naturally — the measurement error is the $\displaystyle dx$.

how far off can the volume be?

$\displaystyle V = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^{3}$the formula tying volume to radius
$\displaystyle \frac{dV}{dr} = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi \cdot 3r^{2}$differentiate — Power Rule on $\displaystyle r^{3}$
$\displaystyle \frac{dV}{dr} = 4\pi r^{2}$the $\displaystyle 3$ cancels the $\displaystyle 3$ underneath
$\displaystyle dV = 4\pi r^{2}\,dr$written as a differential — this is $\displaystyle dy = f'(x)\,dx$ with new letters
$\displaystyle dV = 4\pi\left(21\right)^{2}\left(0.05\right)$now the numbers — the measurement error is $\displaystyle dr$
$\displaystyle dV = 4\pi\left(441\right)\left(0.05\right)$square the radius
$\displaystyle dV = 88.2\pi \approx 277\ \text{cm}^{3}$the most the computed volume could be off

Is that a lot? On its own the number means nothing — it has to be compared with the volume it is an error in.

put that beside the volume itself

$\displaystyle V = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi\left(21\right)^{3} = 12348\pi \approx 38{,}792\ \text{cm}^{3}$the volume being measured
$\displaystyle \frac{dV}{V} = \frac{88.2\pi}{12348\pi} \approx 0.71\%$the relative error in the volume
$\displaystyle \frac{dr}{r} = \frac{0.05}{21} \approx 0.24\%$the relative error in the radius — three times smaller

The error was tripled, and not by accident. Divide the two general expressions and everything cancels — the $\displaystyle \pi$, the $\displaystyle 4$, and most of the $\displaystyle r$:

$$\frac{dV}{V} = \frac{4\pi r^{2}\,dr}{\frac{4}{3}\pi r^{3}} = 3\,\frac{dr}{r}$$

A relative error in $\displaystyle r$ comes out multiplied by the exponent. Volume goes as $\displaystyle r^{3}$, so the factor is exactly $\displaystyle 3$ — for any sphere, any radius, any small error.

What You Should Be Able to Do

  • Write $\displaystyle L(x) = f(a) + f'(a)\left(x-a\right)$ and recognise it as point–slope, not a new formula.
  • Given a number to estimate, choose $\displaystyle f$ and choose $\displaystyle a$ — the nearest point where $\displaystyle f(a)$ and $\displaystyle f'(a)$ are exact.
  • Convert degrees to radians before differentiating anything trigonometric.
  • Sketch the curve and its tangent to say whether the estimate is an over- or underestimate — bends up, line below, estimate low; bends down, line above, estimate high — without computing the true value.
  • Write $\displaystyle dy = f'(x)\,dx$, and read $\displaystyle \Delta y \approx dy$ as “along the curve” $\displaystyle \approx$ “along the tangent”.

No new calculus here either — this is a tangent line from §2.1 and a derivative you already know how to take. What is new is the use: a line you can evaluate standing in for a function you cannot.

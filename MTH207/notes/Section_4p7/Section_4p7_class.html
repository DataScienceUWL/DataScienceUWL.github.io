4.7  Optimization Problems

MTH 207 Calculus I — Applications of Differentiation

Every One of These Is the Same Problem

A word problem asks for the best something — the largest area, the cheapest can, the closest point. Underneath, they are all built the same way.

There are always two quantities and one equation tying them together.

  • The constraint is the equation you are stuck with — the fence you own, the volume the box must hold.
  • The objective is the quantity to make as big or as small as possible.

All the calculus is §4.1 and §4.3, which you already have. The new work is turning a sentence into those two lines.

Eight Steps, and Three of Them Are Where Points Are Lost

Steps 6 is the only one that is new calculus, and it is not new — it is §4.1. ⚠ The three in red are the ones students skip, and they are exactly where an exam takes points off: reducing to one variable, the domain, and saying why your answer is the max or min.

Step 1 is two words. Before any equation, fill in the blanks — maximize or minimize, and the name of the quantity. A student who cannot fill those two in has not found the problem yet, and no amount of algebra will find it for them.

My Turn: A Pen Against a Barn

My turn

You have $\displaystyle 400$ meters of fence and a long straight barn wall. You want to fence a rectangular pen using the wall as one side. What dimensions give the largest area?

Show: steps 1–3 — draw it, then the two lines 1. Draw it / name it. The picture above, every length lettered. In words: maximize the AREA. 2. Constraint (the fence you own): $\displaystyle 2x + y = 400$. 3. Objective (what to make big): $\displaystyle A = xy$.
Show: step 4 — get down to ONE variable

The objective has two letters in it, so it cannot be differentiated yet. Use the constraint to kill one:

$$y = 400 - 2x \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad A(x) = x\left(400-2x\right) = 400x - 2x^{2}$$

⚠ This is the step students skip, and nothing after it works without it.

Show: step 5 — the domain, which the problem gives you

$\displaystyle x$ is a length, so $\displaystyle x > 0$; and $\displaystyle y = 400-2x$ is also a length, so $\displaystyle 400 - 2x \ge 0$, giving $\displaystyle x \le 200$.

$$0 \le x \le 200$$

A closed interval — so §4.1's Extreme Value Theorem applies and a maximum is guaranteed to exist.

steps 6–8 — the derivative, then the candidates

$\displaystyle A'(x) = 400 - 4x$Power Rule, term by term
$\displaystyle 400 - 4x = 0$set it to zero
$\displaystyle x = 100$the only critical number
Show: justify it, then answer the question

Justify. $\displaystyle A''(x) = -4 \lt 0$ everywhere, so the graph is concave down and $\displaystyle x=100$ is a maximum. (Or compare the three candidates: $\displaystyle A(0)=0$, $\displaystyle A(100)=20{,}000$, $\displaystyle A(200)=0$.) Answer. $\displaystyle x = 100$ m, $\displaystyle y = 400 - 200 = 200$ m, giving $\displaystyle A = \mathbf{20{,}000\ \text{m}^{2}}$ — with units.

Notice the shape of the answer: the side parallel to the wall came out twice the other. That is not a coincidence and it is worth remembering as a sanity check — but it is not a justification, and on an exam it earns nothing on its own.

Your Turn: Two Numbers

Your turn

Two positive numbers add to $\displaystyle 30$. What is the largest their product can be? Work all eight steps — the first three take ten seconds each here, which is the point. ⚠ There is nothing to draw in this one. Step 1 is still step 1: name the two numbers with letters, and fill in the two words. A problem with no picture is not a problem with no step 1.

Show: 1. Draw / name it

1. Draw / name it. Nothing to draw — let the numbers be $\displaystyle x$ and $\displaystyle y$. In words: maximize the PRODUCT. 2. Constraint: $\displaystyle x + y = 30$.    3. Objective: $\displaystyle P = xy$. 4. One variable: $\displaystyle y = 30 - x$, so $\displaystyle P(x) = x(30-x) = 30x - x^{2}$. 5. Domain: both numbers positive, so $\displaystyle 0 \lt x \lt 30$. 6. Optimize: $\displaystyle P'(x) = 30 - 2x = 0$ gives $\displaystyle x = 15$. 7. Justify: $\displaystyle P''(x) = -2 \lt 0$ everywhere, so the curve is concave down and $\displaystyle x=15$ is the absolute maximum — there are no endpoints to check, because the domain is open. 8. Answer: the numbers are $\displaystyle 15$ and $\displaystyle 15$, and the largest product is $\displaystyle \mathbf{225}$.

⚠ The domain here is an open interval, $\displaystyle 0 \lt x \lt 30$, so the Extreme Value Theorem does not promise you anything — there are no endpoints to check. The second derivative is what settles it. That is the difference between this problem and the fence, and it is a true/false item waiting to happen.

Where We Are

  • Two quantities, one constraint. Name both before touching calculus.
  • The objective must be a function of one variable before you differentiate it.
  • A closed domain guarantees a max and a min exist; an open one does not.

The Step That Earns the Points

A critical number is a candidate. Saying “$\displaystyle f'=0$ at $\displaystyle x=100$, so the area is largest” asserts the thing you were asked to show.

Three ways to justify, and any one of them is complete.

  • Closed interval: compare $\displaystyle f$ at every critical number and at both endpoints. The biggest wins. (§4.1)
  • First Derivative Test: $\displaystyle f'$ goes $\displaystyle +$ to $\displaystyle -$ at $\displaystyle c$, so a maximum. (§4.3)
  • Second Derivative Test: $\displaystyle f''(c) \lt 0$, so a maximum — and if $\displaystyle f'' \lt 0$ on the whole domain, that single critical number is the absolute maximum. (§4.3)

⚠ The third one is usually quickest in an optimization problem, because these objectives are often concave down everywhere. But it is silent when $\displaystyle f''(c)=0$ — which Quiz 8 asked about — and then you fall back to the first.

My Turn: A Tray From a Square Sheet

My turn

A $\displaystyle 30$ cm square of card has a square of side $\displaystyle x$ cut from each corner; the flaps fold up into an open tray. What $\displaystyle x$ gives the largest volume?

Show: steps 1–4 1. Draw it / name it. The two pictures above. In words: maximize the VOLUME. 2. Constraint — and here it is geometric rather than an equation: folding up a flap of height $\displaystyle x$ takes $\displaystyle x$ off each end of the base, so the base is $\displaystyle 30-2x$ on a side. 3. Objective: $\displaystyle V = (\text{base})^{2}\times(\text{height})$, so
$$V(x) = x\left(30-2x\right)^{2}$$

4. One variable: already done — the geometry did it, so step 4 came free. That is what a geometric constraint buys you.

Show: step 5 — the domain, and why it is not all of $\displaystyle \mathbb{R}$

You cannot cut a negative corner, and you cannot cut more than half the sheet away, or there is no base left:

$$0 \le x \le 15$$

Closed again, so a maximum exists. Note $\displaystyle V=0$ at both ends — no corner cut means no height, and $\displaystyle x=15$ means no base.

steps 6–8 — differentiate, then FACTOR, do not expand

$\displaystyle V'(x) = 1\cdot\left(30-2x\right)^{2} + x\cdot\frac{d}{dx}\left[\left(30-2x\right)^{2}\right]$Product Rule
$\displaystyle V'(x) = \left(30-2x\right)^{2} + x\cdot 2\left(30-2x\right)\left(-2\right)$Chain Rule on the square
$\displaystyle V'(x) = \left(30-2x\right)\left[\left(30-2x\right) - 4x\right]$factor out the common $\displaystyle (30-2x)$
$\displaystyle V'(x) = \left(30-2x\right)\left(30-6x\right)$tidy the bracket
$\displaystyle x = 15, \ x = 5$a product is zero when a factor is
Show: justify it, then answer the question

$\displaystyle x=15$ is the degenerate end of the domain, so the only interior candidate is $\displaystyle x=5$. Justify by comparing all three candidates on the closed interval: $\displaystyle V(0) = 0$, $\displaystyle V(5) = 5\cdot 20^{2} = 2000$, $\displaystyle V(15) = 0$. Answer: cut $\displaystyle x = 5$ cm squares, giving $\displaystyle V = \mathbf{2000\ \text{cm}^{3}}$.

⚠ Factor, do not expand. $\displaystyle x(30-2x)^{2}$ multiplies out to a cubic, and then the critical numbers need the quadratic formula. Factoring the derivative hands you $\displaystyle x=5$ and $\displaystyle x=15$ by inspection — and the same move is the whole of the Simplifying Derivatives handout.

Your Turn: Three Pens

Your turn

A rectangular field is divided into three equal pens by two fences parallel to one side. You have $\displaystyle 600$ m of fence in total. What dimensions give the largest total area? Show your justification.

Show: Solution 1 — $\displaystyle x$ is the whole side 1. Draw it / name it. $\displaystyle x$ = the whole horizontal side, $\displaystyle y$ = each of the four parallel fences. In words: maximize the AREA. 2. Constraint: two sides of length $\displaystyle x$, four of length $\displaystyle y$:
$$2x + 4y = 600$$

3. Objective: $\displaystyle A = xy$. 4. One variable: $\displaystyle y = 150 - \frac{x}{2}$, so

$$A(x) = x\left(150 - \frac{x}{2}\right) = 150x - \frac{x^{2}}{2}$$

5. Domain: $\displaystyle 0 \le x \le 300$. 6. Optimize: $\displaystyle A'(x) = 150 - x = 0$ gives $\displaystyle x = 150$. 7. Justify: $\displaystyle A''(x) = -1 \lt 0$ everywhere, so concave down and this is the absolute maximum. 8. Answer: $\displaystyle y = 150 - 75 = 75$. The field is $\displaystyle 150$ m by $\displaystyle 75$ m, area $\displaystyle \mathbf{11{,}250\ \text{m}^{2}}$.

Show: Solution 2 — $\displaystyle x$ is ONE pen's width 1. Draw it / name it. $\displaystyle x$ = the width of one pen, $\displaystyle y$ = each of the four parallel fences. The whole field is then $\displaystyle 3x$ across. 2. Constraint: the top and bottom are each $\displaystyle 3x$, so they use $\displaystyle 6x$, and the four parallel fences use $\displaystyle 4y$:
$$6x + 4y = 600 \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad 3x + 2y = 300$$

3. Objective: the field is $\displaystyle 3x$ by $\displaystyle y$, so $\displaystyle A = 3xy$. 4. One variable: $\displaystyle y = 150 - \frac{3x}{2}$, so

$$A(x) = 3x\left(150 - \frac{3x}{2}\right) = 450x - \frac{9x^{2}}{2}$$

5. Domain: $\displaystyle 0 \le x \le 100$. 6. Optimize: $\displaystyle A'(x) = 450 - 9x = 0$ gives $\displaystyle x = 50$. 7. Justify: $\displaystyle A''(x) = -9 \lt 0$ everywhere, so again the absolute maximum. 8. Answer: $\displaystyle y = 150 - 75 = 75$, and the field is $\displaystyle 3x = 150$ across. Same field: $\displaystyle 150$ m by $\displaystyle 75$ m, area $\displaystyle \mathbf{11{,}250\ \text{m}^{2}}$.

⚠ Both are right, and $\displaystyle x$ does not mean the same thing. Solution 1 ends at $\displaystyle x = 150$, Solution 2 at $\displaystyle x = 50$ — and they describe the same $\displaystyle 150 \times 75$ field. The letters are yours to choose; what is not negotiable is that step 2 counts the fence in your own picture, and step 8 answers the question that was asked rather than reporting whatever $\displaystyle x$ happened to come out as.

The only hard step was the constraint: four fences of length $\displaystyle x$, not two. Everything after it was the fence problem again. ⚠ If your picture is wrong, your calculus cannot save you — which is why step 1 is draw it.

For Reference: Closest Point, and a Trick

Not worked in class — here because HW37 asks two of these.

Your turn

Find the point on the parabola $\displaystyle y = x^{2}$ closest to $\displaystyle (0,3)$.

Show: 1. Draw it / name it

1. Draw it / name it. Sketch the parabola and mark $\displaystyle (0,3)$ above it; let $\displaystyle (x,y)$ be the point on the curve. In words: minimize the DISTANCE. 2. Constraint: the curve itself, $\displaystyle y = x^{2}$. 3. Objective: $\displaystyle d = \sqrt{x^{2} + \left(y-3\right)^{2}}$. 4. One variable: $\displaystyle d(x) = \sqrt{x^{2} + \left(x^{2}-3\right)^{2}}$. The trick: minimize $\displaystyle D = d^{2}$ instead. A square root is increasing, so whatever makes $\displaystyle d^{2}$ smallest makes $\displaystyle d$ smallest — and it saves you the Chain Rule on a root. $\displaystyle D(x) = x^{2} + \left(x^{2}-3\right)^{2}$. 5. Domain: all of $\displaystyle \mathbb{R}$ — every $\displaystyle x$ gives a point on the curve. 6. Optimize: $\displaystyle D'(x) = 2x + 2\left(x^{2}-3\right)(2x) = 2x\left(2x^{2}-5\right)$, so the critical numbers are $\displaystyle x = 0$ and $\displaystyle x = \pm\sqrt{5/2} = \pm\frac{\sqrt{10}}{2}$. 7. Justify: no endpoints to check, so compare the critical values. $\displaystyle D(0) = 9$, but $\displaystyle D\!\left(\pm\frac{\sqrt{10}}{2}\right) = \frac{11}{4}$, which is smaller — so those two are the minima and $\displaystyle x=0$ is not. 8. Answer: the two points $\displaystyle \left(\pm\frac{\sqrt{10}}{2},\ \frac{5}{2}\right)$, at distance $\displaystyle \frac{\sqrt{11}}{2} \approx 1.66$.

⚠ Minimize the square. It is legal because squaring is increasing on $\displaystyle d \ge 0$, and it turns a root into a polynomial. Say so in one line when you use it — an unexplained switch looks like a different problem.

What You Should Be Able to Do

  • Turn a sentence into a constraint and an objective.
  • Use the constraint to write the objective in one variable, and state the domain that the physical problem forces.
  • Find the critical numbers, and justify which is the max or min — closed interval, first derivative test, or second.
  • Answer the question that was asked, with units.

Exam 3 asks for a box with a square base and an open top holding a fixed volume, using the least material — and it asks, in those words, for units and for justification. HW37 problem 2 is that problem with different numbers. Do it on paper before the exam, start to finish, including the sentence that says why it is a minimum.

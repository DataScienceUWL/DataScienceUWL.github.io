Section 2.1 — Secant lines and average rate of change

Class day 2, Wednesday September 9

Your turn

For $f(x)=x^{2}-2x$, find the average rate of change on $[1,4]$, then on $[1,2]$, then on $[1,1.5]$. What number are they heading for?

Show the solution

On $[1,4]$: $f(4)=8$ and $f(1)=-1$, so the rate is $\displaystyle\frac{8-(-1)}{4-1}=3$.

On $[1,2]$: $f(2)=0$, so $\displaystyle\frac{0-(-1)}{2-1}=1$.

On $[1,1.5]$: $f(1.5)=-0.75$, so $\displaystyle\frac{-0.75-(-1)}{0.5}=0.5$.

$3$, then $1$, then $0.5$ — they are heading for $\mathbf{0}$.

Each one is a secant slope. Shrinking the interval is the whole idea of the chapter, done with arithmetic only.

Your turn

Let $g(x)=x^{2}+x$, let $P=(2,6)$, and let $Q=(x,\,x^{2}+x)$ be any other point on the curve. Simplify the slope $m_{PQ}$, then say what it approaches as $Q$ slides toward $P$.

Show the solution

$\displaystyle m_{PQ}=\frac{(x^{2}+x)-6}{x-2}$.

Factor the top: $x^{2}+x-6=(x+3)(x-2)$.

So $\displaystyle m_{PQ}=\frac{(x+3)(x-2)}{x-2}=x+3$, valid for every $x\neq 2$.

As $Q\to P$, $x\to 2$ and $m_{PQ}\to \mathbf{5}$.

The cancellation is legal precisely because $x\neq 2$ — $Q$ is never $P$. That one point is what §2.2 is about.

Your turnstretch

A ball is thrown upward and its height after $t$ seconds is $h(t)=40t-5t^{2}$ meters. Find the average velocity on $[1,3]$ and on $[1,1.1]$. What is the velocity at $t=1$ heading toward?

Show the solution

$h(1)=35$, $h(3)=120-45=75$, so on $[1,3]$ the average velocity is $\displaystyle\frac{75-35}{2}=20$ m/s.

$h(1.1)=44-6.05=37.95$, so on $[1,1.1]$ it is $\displaystyle\frac{37.95-35}{0.1}=29.5$ m/s.

The shorter interval gives $29.5$, so the instantaneous velocity at $t=1$ is heading for $\mathbf{30}$ m/s.

Average velocity over an interval; instantaneous velocity at an instant. The second is what the first approaches.

Section 2.2 — The limit of a function

Class day 3, Thursday September 10

Concept

True or false? Say why in one sentence.

If $f(2)=5$, then $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 2}f(x)=5$.

Show the answer

FALSE — The converse also fails – it needs continuity. A jump at $2$ breaks it.

Concept

True or false? Say why in one sentence.

If $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 3^-}f(x)=4$ and $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 3^+}f(x)=4$, then $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 3}f(x)=4$.

Show the answer

TRUE — A two-sided limit exists exactly when both one-sided limits exist and agree.

Your turn

Let $\displaystyle f(x)=\frac{x^{2}-1}{x-1}$. Evaluate $f$ at $x=0.9,\,0.99,\,1.01,\,1.1$, then say what $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 1}f(x)$ is. What is $f(1)$?

Show the solution

First simplify, so the arithmetic is not four separate slogs: $\dfrac{x^{2}-1}{x-1}=\dfrac{(x-1)(x+1)}{x-1}=x+1$ for every $x\neq 1$.

So $f(0.9)=1.9$, $f(0.99)=1.99$, $f(1.01)=2.01$, $f(1.1)=2.1$.

From the left the values climb toward $2$; from the right they fall toward $2$.

$\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 1}f(x)=\mathbf{2}$

But $f(1)$ substitutes to $\dfrac{1-1}{1-1}=\dfrac{0}{0}$, which is undefined — the function has no value at $1$ at all.

That is the whole point: the limit describes where the function is heading, not where it is.

The limit exists and the value does not. A limit never asks what happens AT the point.

Your turn

Let $g(x)=x+2$ for $x<1$, $g(1)=5$, and $g(x)=3x$ for $x>1$. Find $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 1^-}g(x)$, $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 1^+}g(x)$, $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 1}g(x)$, and $g(1)$.

Show the solution

From the left, $g(x)=x+2$, so $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 1^-}g(x)=3$.

From the right, $g(x)=3x$, so $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 1^+}g(x)=3$.

The two sides agree, so $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 1}g(x)=3$.

But $g(1)=5$, because that is how $g$ was defined.

The limit is $3$ and the value is $5$, and neither one is wrong. Same lesson as the problem above, with the value present rather than missing.

Your turnstretch

Let $\displaystyle h(x)=\frac{|x-2|}{x-2}$. Find the two one-sided limits at $x=2$, and say whether $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 2}h(x)$ exists.

Show the solution

For $x>2$, $x-2$ is positive, so $|x-2|=x-2$ and $h(x)=1$. Hence $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 2^+}h(x)=1$.

For $x<2$, $x-2$ is negative, so $|x-2|=-(x-2)$ and $h(x)=-1$. Hence $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 2^-}h(x)=-1$.

The one-sided limits are $1$ and $-1$. They disagree, so $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 2}h(x)$ does not exist.

Splitting on the sign of what is inside the absolute value is the move. You will use it again on $\sqrt{x^{2}}$ in §2.6 and on $|x|$ in §2.8.

Section 2.2 — One-sided and infinite limits

Class day 4, Monday September 14

Concept

True or false? Say why in one sentence.

If $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to a}f(x)$ does not exist, then $f$ is not defined at $a$.

Show the answer

FALSE — Existence of the limit and existence of the value are independent. $f(x)=\frac{|x|}{x}$ with $f(0)=1$ is defined at $0$ but has no limit there.

Concept

True or false? Say why in one sentence.

A function can approach two different limits as $x\to a$.

Show the answer

FALSE — Limits are unique. Different one-sided limits mean the limit does not exist – not that there are two of them.

Your turn

Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0^-}\frac{3}{x}$ and $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0^+}\frac{3}{x}$. Then say what $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{3}{x}$ is.

Show the solution

From the right, $x$ is small and positive, so $3/x$ is large and positive: $+\infty$.

From the left, $x$ is small and negative, so $3/x$ is large and negative: $-\infty$.

The two sides disagree, so $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{3}{x}$ does not exist.

Sign first, size second. No values plugged in anywhere.

Your turn

Find every vertical asymptote of $\displaystyle f(x)=\frac{x+2}{x^2-x-6}$, and say what happens on each side of each one.

Show the solution

Factor: $x^2-x-6=(x-3)(x+2)$, so $\displaystyle f(x)=\frac{x+2}{(x-3)(x+2)}$.

At $x=-2$ the factor cancels, so there is a hole, not an asymptote.

At $x=3$ only the denominator vanishes, so $x=3$ is a vertical asymptote.

Just right of $3$: numerator near $5$, denominator small positive, so $+\infty$. Just left: $-\infty$.

A zero denominator is not enough. Check the numerator at the same point.

Your turnstretch

Sketch one function $f$ with $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 2^-}f(x)=1$, $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 2^+}f(x)=\infty$, and $f(2)=4$.

Show the solution

Left of $2$: any curve leveling off at height $1$, with an open circle at $(2,1)$.

Right of $2$: a branch climbing without bound as $x$ approaches $2$ from that side.

Then a solid dot at $(2,4)$, which is the value, unrelated to either limit.

Three independent facts. The point of the problem is that nothing forces them to agree.

Common errors and misconceptions

Common error

Quiz 2, problem 2. Thirteen of 25 circled True. Of the twelve who circled False, eight gave this one-sided reason and one gave the reason below. One person in the room got the whole way there — so if you did not, you were not behind, you were with everybody.

$f(0.1)$, $f(0.01)$ and $f(0.001)$ are all $2$, so $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}f(x)=2$ — but only from the right. We are not told what happens from the left, so we cannot be sure.

What is wrong with it

Why it looks rightThe one-sidedness is real, and spotting it is a genuinely good instinct — it is the right objection to a different problem.

What is actually trueIt is not what breaks the statement. Add $f(-0.1)$, $f(-0.01)$ and $f(-0.001)$ to the table and the statement is still false. Three points are three points. $\displaystyle f(x)=\sin\!\left(\frac{\pi}{x}\right)+2$ passes through exactly $2$ at every one of those inputs while swinging between $1$ and $3$ everywhere in between. No finite table is evidence for a limit, from either side or both.

Common error

Quiz 2, problem 5 — about four of you substituted. About ten wrote the sign-and-size version straight out (“$+$big over $-$small”, “$4$ over small neg”), which is the largest agreement on any single method anywhere on the quiz.

To decide $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to3^-}\frac{x+1}{x-3}$, work out $\displaystyle\frac{2.9+1}{2.9-3}=\frac{3.9}{-0.1}$ and read the sign off that.

What is wrong with it

Why it looks rightBecause it is reasonable. With $2.9$ the arithmetic is easy enough to do in your head, and it gets the sign right — so this is not a shortcut, it is a different method that happens to work here.

What is actually trueNothing above is wrong, and on this problem it costs nothing. It just does not scale. One sample is not a limit; $2.9$ is not $3$; and the moment the numerator is something like $\sqrt{x+1}-2$ the arithmetic stops being free while the reasoning stays the same length. Naming the two factors never breaks: near $x=3$ the numerator is close to $4$, so positive and not small, and $x-3$ is small and negative just to the left. Positive over small negative is large and negative.

Common error

Quiz 2, problem 5 — six of 25 lost the point here after getting both limits right. It is the cheapest point on the page to give away.

The vertical asymptote of $\displaystyle f(x)=\frac{x+1}{x-3}$ is $(-\infty,3)\cup(3,\infty)$. Or: $y=3$. Or: $3$.

What is wrong with it

Why it looks rightAll three know exactly where the trouble is. Only the notation is wrong, which is why it is easy to let slide.

What is actually trueAn asymptote is a line, so the answer is an equation of a line: $x=3$. The interval is the domain — a different question with the same $3$ in it. And $y=3$ is a horizontal line through height $3$, which is somewhere else entirely.

Section 2.3 — Limit laws

Class day 5, Tuesday September 15

Concept

True or false? Say why in one sentence.

$\displaystyle\lim_{x\to a}[f(x)+g(x)]=\lim_{x\to a}f(x)+\lim_{x\to a}g(x)$ for all functions $f$ and $g$.

Show the answer

FALSE — The Sum Law requires both limits on the right to exist first. Without that the equation is meaningless.

Concept

True or false? Say why in one sentence.

If $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to a}[f(x)+g(x)]$ exists, then $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to a}f(x)$ and $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to a}g(x)$ both exist.

Show the answer

FALSE — Two failures can cancel. With $f(x)=\frac1{x}$ and $g(x)=-\frac1{x}$ near $0$, the sum is $0$ but neither limit exists.

Your turn

Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 4}\frac{x^2-16}{x-4}$.

Show the solution

Substituting $x=4$ gives $\dfrac{16-16}{4-4}=\dfrac{0}{0}$, so the quotient has to be rewritten before the limit can be taken.

Factor the numerator as a difference of squares: $x^{2}-16=(x-4)(x+4)$.

$\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 4}\frac{(x-4)(x+4)}{x-4}=\lim_{x\to 4}(x+4)$

The cancellation is legal because $x\neq 4$ throughout the limit — $x$ approaches $4$ but never equals it.

Now substitution works: $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 4}(x+4)=4+4=\mathbf{8}$

Your turn

Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{\sqrt{x+9}-3}{x}$.

Show the solution

Substituting gives $\frac00$. Multiply by the conjugate.

$\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{\sqrt{x+9}-3}{x}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{x+9}+3}{\sqrt{x+9}+3}=\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{x}{x\left(\sqrt{x+9}+3\right)}$

$\displaystyle=\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{1}{\sqrt{x+9}+3}=\frac16$

The conjugate turns a difference of roots into a difference of squares, which cancels.

Common errors and misconceptions

Common error

Quiz 2, problem 1, and it was the hardest question on the page. Fourteen of 25 circled True, eleven circled False — and across all 25 papers, one person wrote anything at all about the denominator (“we do not have $\frac00$, so we can do it”). Not one in five, one in twenty-five. Whatever you put down, almost the whole room was missing the same thing you were.

$\displaystyle\lim_{x\to1}\frac{x-3}{x^2+2x-4}=\frac{\lim_{x\to1}(x-3)}{\lim_{x\to1}(x^2+2x-4)}$ is FALSE — you cannot split one limit into two, and substituting gives $\frac{-2}{-1}$, so the two sides are different functions.

What is wrong with it

Why it looks rightSplitting a limit does feel like it needs permission, and the fraction $\frac{\lim}{\lim}$ looks as though something ought to cancel.

What is actually trueThe Quotient Law says exactly this, and it is true as long as the limit of the denominator is not zero. Here that limit is $1+2-4=-1$, which is not zero, so the law applies and the statement is TRUE. Substituting does not disprove anything — it is how you check the hypothesis.

Common error

Why is it true? "Because the limit laws let you divide." / "Because $x$ is approaching the same number in both." / "Because both limits exist."

What is wrong with it

Why it looks rightEvery one of these is a true sentence, and each is a real part of the reason.

What is actually trueNone of them is the reason, because none of them could ever come out false. A law with a hypothesis is only quoted correctly when the hypothesis is checked out loud: the denominator limit is $-1\neq0$. The Sum, Difference, Product and Constant Multiple Laws have no hypothesis; the Quotient Law does, and that is the only thing worth saying about it.

Common error

Quiz 2, problem 4 — the most common single deduction on the quiz.

$\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sqrt{x+4}-2}{x} =\frac{(x+4)-4}{x\left(\sqrt{x+4}+2\right)} =\frac{x}{x\left(\sqrt{x+4}+2\right)} =\frac{1}{\sqrt{x+4}+2}=\frac14$

What is wrong with it

Why it looks rightEvery algebraic step is correct, the answer is right, and writing $\lim$ six times is tedious.

What is actually trueThe $\lim$ is not decoration. Canceling $\frac{x}{x}=1$ is legal only because $x\neq0$ — and once the $\lim_{x\to0}$ is gone, nothing on the page says $x\neq0$ any more, so that line divides by zero. It also claims a function equals the number $\frac14$, which is false everywhere except in the limit. Carry $\lim$ on every line until the step where you actually take it.

Common error

Quiz 2, problem 4 — five copies lost three or four points to radical algebra alone, with the calculus done correctly around it.

$\sqrt{x+4}-2$, so $\sqrt{x}+\sqrt{4}-2=\sqrt{x}$ — or, after the conjugate, $\left(\sqrt{x+4}\right)^2-2^2=x+4-2$.

What is wrong with it

Why it looks rightRoots distribute over products, and $\sqrt{4}=2$ is sitting right there asking to be used.

What is actually true$\sqrt{a+b}\neq\sqrt a+\sqrt b$ — test it: $\sqrt{9+16}=5$, not $3+4=7$. And squaring $2$ gives $4$, not $2$: $\left(\sqrt{x+4}\right)^2-2^2=(x+4)-4=x$. That $x$ is the whole point of the conjugate; if it does not appear, the rewrite has not bought anything.

Section 2.3 — Squeeze theorem and limit strategies

Class day 6, Wednesday September 16

Concept

True or false? Say why in one sentence.

$\displaystyle\lim_{x\to a}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}=\frac{\lim_{x\to a}f(x)}{\lim_{x\to a}g(x)}$ whenever both limits exist.

Show the answer

FALSE — Both limits existing is not enough — the Quotient Law also needs $\lim g \neq 0$. When it is $0$ the right-hand side is not even a number, while the left may exist perfectly well: $\lim_{x\to0}\frac{x}{x}=1$.

Concept

True or false? Say why in one sentence.

$\lim_{x\to4}\left(\frac{2x}{x-4}-\frac{8}{x-4}\right)=\lim_{x\to4}\frac{2x}{x-4}-\lim_{x\to4}\frac{8}{x-4}$

Show the answer

FALSE — The Difference Law needs both limits on the right to exist; each is infinite. The left side is $\lim\frac{2x-8}{x-4}=2$.

Your turn

Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 3}\frac{\frac1x-\frac13}{x-3}$.

Show the solution

Substituting gives $\frac00$. Clear the small fractions first.

$\displaystyle\frac1x-\frac13=\frac{3-x}{3x}$, so the whole thing is $\displaystyle\frac{3-x}{3x(x-3)}$.

$3-x=-(x-3)$, so this is $\displaystyle\frac{-(x-3)}{3x(x-3)}=\frac{-1}{3x}$.

$\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 3}\frac{-1}{3x}=-\frac19$

A complex fraction is a common denominator problem wearing a disguise.

Your turn

Given $-x^2+2 \le g(x) \le x^2+2$ for all $x$, find $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0}g(x)$.

Show the solution

Both bounds are polynomials, so substitute: each tends to $2$ as $x\to 0$.

$g$ is trapped between two things that both approach $2$.

By the Squeeze Theorem, $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0}g(x)=2$.

You never find a formula for $g$, and you do not need one.

Common errors and misconceptions

Common error

Quiz 2 — the Squeeze Theorem was invoked on three different problems it has nothing to do with, by three different people.

"By the Squeeze Theorem, the left and right sides both give $3$, so the limit is $3$." / "Both sides of the squeeze theorem have to be equal for it to be continuous."

What is wrong with it

Why it looks rightThe Squeeze Theorem is about two things closing in on a third, and two one-sided limits closing in on a value sounds like the same picture.

What is actually trueDifferent theorem. The Squeeze Theorem needs two known functions trapping an unknown one: $g(x)\le f(x)\le h(x)$ with $\lim g=\lim h=L$, and its whole job is to find a limit you could not compute directly. Two one-sided limits of the same function agreeing is just the definition of the limit existing — no theorem required, and nothing is being trapped.

Section 2.5 — Continuity

Class day 7, Thursday September 17

Concept

True or false? Say why in one sentence.

If $f$ is continuous at $a$, then $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to a}f(x)=f(a)$.

Show the answer

TRUE — That equation IS the definition, so the implication is immediate. Notice how much it packs in: for it to hold, $f(a)$ must exist, the limit must exist, and the two must agree.

Concept

True or false? Say why in one sentence.

To say $f$ is continuous at $a$ requires exactly two things: that $f(a)$ is defined and that $\lim_{x\to a}f(x)$ exists.

Show the answer

FALSE — There is a third: the two must be equal. A removable discontinuity satisfies both stated conditions and is still not continuous.

Your turn

Where is $\displaystyle f(x)=\frac{x+1}{x^2-4}$ continuous? Give the answer in interval notation.

Show the solution

A rational function is continuous everywhere its denominator is non-zero.

$x^2-4=0$ at $x=\pm2$.

So $f$ is continuous on $(-\infty,-2)\cup(-2,2)\cup(2,\infty)$.

Three intervals, not one set minus two points — write it the way it is asked for.

Your turn

Find $c$ so that $f(x)=x^2$ for $x\le 2$ and $f(x)=cx+1$ for $x>2$ is continuous at $x=2$.

Show the solution

Continuity at $x=2$ needs three things to agree: the left limit, the right limit, and the value $f(2)$.

Value and left limit. For $x\le 2$ the rule is $x^{2}$, so $f(2)=2^{2}=4$ and $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 2^-}f(x)=4$.

Right limit. For $x>2$ the rule is $cx+1$, so $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 2^+}f(x)=c(2)+1=2c+1$.

Set the two sides equal: $2c+1=4$.

$2c=3$, so $c=\mathbf{\dfrac{3}{2}}$.

Common errors and misconceptions

Common error

Quiz 2, problem 3(e). About three of you blamed the limit this way, and two answered that $g$ is continuous. About seven said in their own words that the limit and the value disagree — “lim $g(x)=3$ while $g(1)=5$, these do not match” — which was full credit. The numbered list is ours, not yours: the requirement does not have to be named by number.

"$g$ is not continuous at $x=1$ because it does not approach $5$ from both sides — it approaches $3$."

What is wrong with it

Why it looks rightIt is pointing at the right pair of numbers, and it knows they disagree.

What is actually trueIt blames the wrong one. The limit is perfectly fine: $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to1}g(x)=3$, both sides agree, requirement 2 is met. The defect is $g(1)=5$, the single point the formula defines somewhere else. What fails is requirement 3 — the limit exists, the value exists, and they are not equal.

Common error

Quiz 2, problem 3 — about five of you called it a jump. Several of those had the picture exactly right first (“from the left and the right it gets closer to $3$, but at $x=1$ it jumps to $5$”) and then reached for the wrong name for it. That is a vocabulary problem, not a calculus problem, and it is worth fixing because Exam 1 will ask for the name.

"$g$ is not continuous — it is a jump discontinuity." / "It is not continuous because you cannot draw it without lifting your pencil."

What is wrong with it

Why it looks rightThe graph really does have a break in it, and the pencil test really does fail.

What is actually trueThere is no jump: both one-sided limits are $3$, so the curve does not step from one height to another — one single point has been lifted off it. That is a removable discontinuity, and the name is literal: redefine $g(1)=3$ and it is gone. A jump needs the two one-sided limits to be different, which would make the repair impossible.

Section 2.5 — IVT and types of discontinuity

Class day 8, Monday September 21

Concept

True or false? Say why in one sentence.

If $f$ is continuous on $[0,1]$ with $f(0)=-1$ and $f(1)=1$, then $f(c)=0$ for some $c$ in $[0,1]$.

Show the answer

TRUE — Every hypothesis is present: $f$ is continuous on a CLOSED interval, and $0$ lies between $f(0)=-1$ and $f(1)=1$. The theorem then guarantees such a $c$ exists — it does not say where, and there may be several.

Concept

True or false? Say why in one sentence.

The function $h(x)=\frac{|x-4|}{x-4}$ can be given a value at $x=4$ that makes it continuous there.

Show the answer

FALSE — The one-sided limits at $4$ are $-1$ and $1$, so no single value of $h(4)$ can bridge them. A jump is not removable.

Your turn

Show that $x^3+x-1=0$ has a solution between $0$ and $1$.

Show the solution

Let $f(x)=x^3+x-1$. It is a polynomial, so it is continuous on $[0,1]$.

$f(0)=-1<0$ and $f(1)=1>0$.

$0$ lies between $-1$ and $1$, so by the Intermediate Value Theorem there is a $c$ in $(0,1)$ with $f(c)=0$.

Name the continuity out loud. That is the hypothesis the theorem needs, and it is where the points are.

Your turn

Classify the discontinuity of $\displaystyle f(x)=\frac{x^2-1}{x-1}$ at $x=1$, and say whether it can be repaired.

Show the solution

$\displaystyle\frac{(x-1)(x+1)}{x-1}=x+1$ for $x\neq1$, so $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to1}f(x)=2$.

$f(1)$ is undefined, so requirement 1 fails — but the limit exists.

That makes it removable: defining $f(1)=2$ makes $f$ continuous there.

Removable is the only kind you can patch, and the limit existing is exactly what makes it so.

Common errors and misconceptions

Common error

Quiz 2, problem 6 — the best-answered question on the quiz. Almost everyone found $c=2$, by both routes. Worth knowing: the question that looked hardest on the page was the one the class did best on.

To find $c$ making $h$ continuous, write $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to3^-}h(x)=\lim_{x\to3^+}h(x)$ — but that needs limits, and limits of piecewise functions are hard.

What is wrong with it

Why it looks rightIt is what the definition of continuity literally says, so it feels like the required route.

What is actually trueIt is correct, and it is also more machinery than the problem needs. Both pieces are polynomials, so each is continuous on its own side and the only place continuity can fail is the junction — which means the two pieces just have to meet there: $3^2-1=3c+2$, so $c=2$. Writing the limits is not wrong; it is Direct Substitution restated, because for a polynomial the one-sided limit is the value.

Section 2.6 — Limits at infinity

Class day 9, Tuesday September 22

Concept

True or false? Say why in one sentence.

The graph of a function can cross its horizontal asymptote.

Show the answer

TRUE — $f(x)=\frac{\sin x}{x}$ crosses $y=0$ infinitely often while still approaching it.

Concept

True or false? Say why in one sentence.

If $f$ is defined at $x=2$, then the line $x=2$ cannot be a vertical asymptote of $y=f(x)$.

Show the answer

FALSE — A vertical asymptote is a statement about the limit, not the value. A piecewise function can blow up near $1$ and still assign $f(1)$ a value.

Your turn

Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{4x^2-x}{3x^2+7}$.

Show the solution

Divide top and bottom by $x^2$, the highest power in the denominator.

$\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{4-\frac1x}{3+\frac{7}{x^2}}$

Every $1/x^n\to0$, and the denominator tends to $3\neq0$, so the limit is $\frac43$.

Your turn

Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{5x+2}{x^3-1}$ and $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{x^4}{x^2+3}$.

Show the solution

First: degree $1$ over degree $3$. Divide by $x^3$ to get $\displaystyle\frac{5/x^2+2/x^3}{1-1/x^3}\to\frac{0}{1}=0$.

Second: degree $4$ over degree $2$. Divide by $x^2$ to get $\displaystyle\frac{x^2}{1+3/x^2}$, whose numerator grows without bound.

So the second limit does not exist; we write $\infty$ to record how it fails.

Common errors and misconceptions

Common error

$\sqrt{x^2}=x$, so $\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{9x^2+1}}{x}\to3$ as $x\to-\infty$.

What is wrong with it

Why it looks rightSquaring and square-rooting are inverse operations, and they do undo each other for every $x$ anyone writes down on purpose.

What is actually true$\sqrt{\phantom{x}}$ always returns the non-negative root, so $\sqrt{x^2}=|x|$ — which is $x$ when $x\ge0$ and $-x$ when $x<0$. Heading to $-\infty$ you are in the second case, so the answer is $-3$. This is the one place a sign error is invisible in the algebra and fatal in the answer.

Common error

Divide the top and bottom by $x$, because $x$ is the variable.

What is wrong with it

Why it looks rightIt works on $\displaystyle\frac{2x+1}{3x-4}$, which is usually the first example anyone sees.

What is actually trueDivide by the highest power of $x$ in the denominator. On $\displaystyle\frac{2x+1}{3x-4}$ that happens to be $x$; on $\displaystyle\frac{2x^2+1}{3x^3-4}$ it is $x^3$, and dividing by $x$ leaves you with $\infty/\infty$ all over again.

Section 2.6 — Horizontal asymptotes

Class day 10, Wednesday September 23

Concept

True or false? Say why in one sentence.

If $f$ has no horizontal asymptote, then $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)=\pm\infty$.

Show the answer

FALSE — $f(x)=\sin x$ has no horizontal asymptote and no infinite limit – it just oscillates.

Concept

True or false? Say why in one sentence.

If $f$ has domain $[0,\infty)$ and has no horizontal asymptote, then $\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)=\infty$ or $\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)=-\infty$.

Show the answer

FALSE — $f(x)=\sin x$ neither settles nor blows up.

Your turn

Find all horizontal asymptotes of $\displaystyle f(x)=\frac{5x}{\sqrt{4x^2+1}}$.

Show the solution

Dividing inside a root means dividing by $\sqrt{x^2}=|x|$, which is $x$ when $x>0$ and $-x$ when $x<0$.

As $x\to\infty$: $\displaystyle\frac{5x}{|x|\sqrt{4+1/x^2}}\to\frac{5}{2}$.

As $x\to-\infty$ the $|x|$ contributes $-x$, giving $-\frac52$.

Two horizontal asymptotes: $y=\frac52$ and $y=-\frac52$.

Your turnstretch

Does $\displaystyle f(x)=\frac{\cos x}{x}$ have a horizontal asymptote as $x\to\infty$? Does its graph cross it?

Show the solution

$-1\le\cos x\le 1$, so $\displaystyle-\frac1x\le\frac{\cos x}{x}\le\frac1x$ for $x>0$.

Both bounds tend to $0$, so by the Squeeze Theorem the limit is $0$ and $y=0$ is a horizontal asymptote.

It crosses that asymptote every time $\cos x=0$ — infinitely often.

An asymptote constrains the tail, not any particular point.

Common errors and misconceptions

Common error

A horizontal asymptote is a line the graph gets closer to but never touches.

What is wrong with it

Why it looks rightIt is true of every example in 2.6, because they are all quotients of polynomials.

What is actually true"Never touches" is not part of the definition, which only says the graph approaches the line as $x\to\pm\infty$. $\displaystyle f(x)=\frac{\sin x}{x}$ has the horizontal asymptote $y=0$ and crosses it infinitely often. Vertical asymptotes genuinely cannot be crossed; horizontal ones can, and only the behavior far out matters.

Common error

Quiz 3, problem 1 — 16 of 26 lost points, and 12 of them lost exactly 2 of 3: the circle was right and the reason was not. That is the largest single block of lost points on the quiz.

Why can a function have two different horizontal asymptotes? Because $\sin x$ stays between $y=1$ and $y=-1$. Or because $y=\sqrt[3]{x}$ has a positive one and a negative one. Or because you could use a piecewise function.

What is wrong with it

Why it looks rightEvery one of these is reaching for the right idea — a graph that settles toward two different heights — and the circle was correct on all of them. The gap is between knowing the answer and having an example that survives being checked.

What is actually trueBeing bounded is not the same as having an asymptote. $\sin x$ stays between $-1$ and $1$ forever and approaches neither; it never settles. $\sqrt[3]{x}$ has no horizontal asymptote at all — it grows without bound, just slowly. The example that works is $\tan^{-1}x$, which approaches $\frac\pi2$ one way and $-\frac\pi2$ the other, or $\displaystyle\frac{3x}{\sqrt{x^{2}+1}}$ from the §2.6 notes. Test any candidate by asking what it does as $x\to\infty$ and then as $x\to-\infty$, and whether it settles on a number.

Common error

Quiz 3, problem 6 — 12 of 26 lost points and 9 scored zero on a two-point question. Only one of those nine made an arithmetic slip; the rest ran a different procedure entirely.

To find the horizontal asymptote of $\displaystyle f(x)=\frac{3x^{2}-1}{x^{2}+4}$, solve $x^{2}+4=0$. Or solve $3x^{2}-1=0$. Or substitute $x=2$. Or write a difference quotient.

What is wrong with it

Why it looks rightEach is a real procedure from this course, correctly executed. Setting the denominator to zero really does find a vertical asymptote; setting the numerator to zero really does find a root. Nothing is wrong except which question is being answered.

What is actually trueA horizontal asymptote is a statement about $x\to\pm\infty$, so the only move that can find one is a limit at infinity: divide top and bottom by the highest power in the denominator. $\displaystyle\frac{3-1/x^{2}}{1+4/x^{2}}\to\frac31$, so the answer is the line $y=3$ — not $3$, and not an interval. Nothing is ever substituted into $x$.

Section 2.7 — Derivatives and rates of change

Class day 11, Thursday September 24

Concept

True or false? Say why in one sentence.

$f'(a)$ is the limit of average rates of change of $f$ over intervals shrinking to $a$.

Show the answer

TRUE — $f'(a)=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}$, and each quotient is an average rate of change.

Concept

True or false? Say why in one sentence.

If $f'(2)$ exists, then $f'(2)$ is the slope of the secant line through $x=2$ and a nearby point.

Show the answer

FALSE — It is the limit of those secant slopes, not any one of them. Every secant slope is an approximation; the derivative is what they approach.

Your turn

Let $f(x)=x^2+1$. Find $f'(2)$ from the definition.

Show the solution

$\displaystyle f'(2)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(2+h)-f(2)}{h}=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\left[(2+h)^2+1\right]-5}{h}$

$\displaystyle=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{4+4h+h^2+1-5}{h}=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{4h+h^2}{h}$

$\displaystyle=\lim_{h\to0}\,(4+h)=4$

Keep $\lim$ on every line until you actually take it.

Your turn

For the same $f$, write the equation of the tangent line at $x=2$.

Show the solution

Start from the equation of any line through a point $(x_{0},y_{0})$ with slope $m$: $$y - y_{0} = m\left(x - x_{0}\right)$$

So three numbers are needed, and none of them may still contain an $x$: $x_{0}$, $y_{0}$ and $m$.

$x_{0}=2$, given.

$y_{0}=f(2)=2^{2}+1=5$. This comes from $f$ itself — the derivative does not give it to you.

$m=f'(2)=4$, computed in the previous problem.

Substitute all three: $y-5=4(x-2)$.

Expand for slope-intercept form: $y=4x-8+5=\mathbf{4x-3}$.

Your turn

A tank drains so that $V(t)$ liters remain after $t$ minutes, and $V'(10)=-3$. Give the units and say in one sentence what it means.

Show the solution

Units: liters per minute.

Ten minutes in, the volume is falling at about $3$ liters each minute.

The sign is the draining. A positive number here would mean it was filling.

Common errors and misconceptions

Common error

$\displaystyle\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}$ with $h=0$ gives $\frac{f(a)-f(a)}{0}=\frac00$, so the derivative is $\frac00$.

What is wrong with it

Why it looks rightThe tangent line is what you get when the two points come together, so setting $h=0$ sounds like the right move.

What is actually trueSame rule as every $\frac00$ in 2.3 — and it is the same reason. $h$ is never $0$ inside $\displaystyle\lim_{h\to0}$; it only goes there. Simplify the quotient first, cancel the $h$ (legal precisely because $h\neq0$), and take the limit last. If you can set $h=0$ before simplifying, you have not found a derivative.

Common error

The average rate of change of $f$ on $[1,4]$ and the instantaneous rate at $x=1$ are both computed from $f(4)$ and $f(1)$.

What is wrong with it

Why it looks rightBoth are "rate of change of $f$", and both live on the same graph.

What is actually trueAverage rate of change is the slope of a secant through two points and needs both. Instantaneous rate is the slope of a tangent at one point and needs a limit — there is no second point to subtract. Same formula, but the instantaneous one has a $\lim$ wrapped around it, and that is the entire content of 2.7.

Concept

Concept

If $f$ is continuous on $[0,4]$ and $f'(2)$ does not exist, then $f$ must have a jump at $x=2$.

Show the answer

Your turn

Find $f'(x)$ from the definition for $f(x)=x^{2}+5x$, then use it to write down $f'(0)$ and $f'(-3)$.

Show the solution

$\displaystyle f'(x)=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\left[(x+h)^{2}+5(x+h)\right]-\left[x^{2}+5x\right]}{h}$

Multiply out and distribute the minus: $\displaystyle=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{2xh+h^{2}+5h}{h}$

Every surviving term has an $h$, so factor and cancel: $\displaystyle=\lim_{h\to 0}(2x+h+5)=2x+5$

$f'(0)=5$ and $f'(-3)=-1$ — substitution, no new limits.

The payoff of 2.8 in one problem: one limit, then every point for free.

Your turn

Find $f'(x)$ from the definition for $\displaystyle f(x)=\frac1x$.

Show the solution

$\displaystyle f'(x)=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\frac{1}{x+h}-\frac1x}{h}$

Common denominator on top: $\displaystyle\frac{1}{x+h}-\frac1x=\frac{x-(x+h)}{x(x+h)}=\frac{-h}{x(x+h)}$

The bar is a division, so multiply by $\frac1h$: $\displaystyle=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{-h}{h\,x(x+h)}$

The fraction-inside-a-fraction move from the §2.7 at-home page, on a derivative.

Your turnstretch

$f'$ is negative on $(-\infty,2)$, zero at $x=2$, and positive on $(2,\infty)$. Describe the shape of $f$.

$f'<0$ means $f$ is falling, so $f$ decreases on $(-\infty,2)$.

$f'>0$ means $f$ is rising, so $f$ increases on $(2,\infty)$.

$f'(2)=0$ is a horizontal tangent, and the curve turns from falling to rising there — a low point at $x=2$.

Reading the graph of $f$ off $f'$, which is the previous skill run backwards.

What is wrong with it

Class day 13, Tuesday September 29

Concept

True or false? Say why in one sentence.

If $\lim_{x\to a}f(x)\neq f(a)$, then $f'(a)$ does not exist.

Show the answer

TRUE — Contrapositive of 'differentiable implies continuous'.

Concept

True or false? Say why in one sentence.

If $\lim_{x\to a}f(x)$ exists, then $f$ is differentiable at $a$.

Show the answer

FALSE — A limit existing says nothing about a tangent line. $f(x)=|x|$ again, at $a=0$.

Your turn

Where is $f(x)=|x-3|$ not differentiable, and what goes wrong there?

Show the solution

Everywhere except $x=3$ the graph is a straight line, so $f'$ exists and is $-1$ to the left, $+1$ to the right.

Concept

Show the answer

FALSE — The Product Rule gives $f'g+fg'$. Test it on $f=g=x$: the left side is $2x$, the false rule gives $1$.

Concept

Your turn

Differentiate two: (a) $\displaystyle y=5x^{3}-\frac{2}{x}$ and (b) $\displaystyle y=\frac{x^{2}+\sqrt{x}}{x}$

Show the solution

$y'=15x^{2}+2x^{-2}=\mathbf{15x^{2}+\dfrac{2}{x^{2}}}$

(b) Divide through first, again because there is no Quotient Rule yet:

$\dfrac{x^{2}+\sqrt{x}}{x}=\dfrac{x^{2}}{x}+\dfrac{x^{1/2}}{x}=x+x^{-1/2}$

$y'=1-\dfrac{1}{2}x^{-3/2}=\mathbf{1-\dfrac{1}{2x^{3/2}}}$

Both parts are really algebra problems. The differentiating is one line once the rewriting is done.

Common errors and misconceptions

Common error

$\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}\left(e^{x}\right) = x\,e^{x-1}$

$\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}\left[f(x)g(x)\right] = f'(x)\,g'(x)$

What is wrong with it

Product Rule route. $u=x^{2}+1$ and $v=x^{3}-4x$, so $u'=2x$ and $v'=3x^{2}-4$.

$(uv)'=u'v+uv'$. Substituting:

$y'=(2x)\left(x^{3}-4x\right)+\left(x^{2}+1\right)\left(3x^{2}-4\right)$

Add and collect: $y'=2x^{4}-8x^{2}+3x^{4}-x^{2}-4=\mathbf{5x^{4}-9x^{2}-4}$

Common errors and misconceptions

Common error

$\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}\left[(x^{2}+1)(x^{3}-4x)\right]=(2x)\left(3x^{2}-4\right)$

What is wrong with it

Why it looks rightIt is what the Sum Rule would lead you to expect. Derivatives split across $+$ and $-$ perfectly well, so splitting across $\times$ feels like the same kind of move.

What is actually trueDifferentiation does not distribute over multiplication. Check it on the simplest case: $\frac{d}{dx}\left(x\cdot x\right)=\frac{d}{dx}x^{2}=2x$, but $f'g'=1\cdot1=1$. The Product Rule is $f'g+fg'$ — two terms, each keeping one factor undifferentiated.

Common error

$\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{f}{g}\right]=\frac{f\,g'-f'g}{g^{2}}$

What is wrong with it

Why it looks rightEvery piece is correct and the denominator is right. Only the order in the numerator is wrong, which is exactly the part no mnemonic makes obvious.

What is actually trueIt is $\dfrac{f'g-fg'}{g^{2}}$ — lo D hi minus hi D lo, bottom first. Getting it backwards flips the sign of every answer. Test it on $\frac{x^{2}}{x}=x$, whose derivative is $1$: the right order gives $\frac{2x\cdot x-x^{2}}{x^{2}}=1$, the wrong order gives $-1$.

Common error

$\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{x}{x^{2}+1}\right]=\frac{\left(x^{2}+1\right)(1)-x(2x)}{x^{2}+1}$

What is wrong with it

Why it looks rightThe numerator is completely correct — the whole Quotient Rule has been applied properly right up to the last symbol.

What is actually trueThe denominator is squared: $\left(x^{2}+1\right)^{2}$. It is the most common way to lose a point on an otherwise perfect quotient, and it is worth one deliberate glance at the bottom before you move on.

Section 3.3 — Derivatives of trig functions

Class day 19, Thursday October 8

Concept

True or false? Say why in one sentence.

If $f(x)=\sin x$, then the fourth derivative $f^{(4)}(x)$ is $\sin x$ again.

Show the answer

TRUE — The cycle is $\sin\to\cos\to-\sin\to-\cos\to\sin$, returning after four steps.

Concept

True or false? Say why in one sentence.

$\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}(\cos x)=\sin x$.

Show the answer

FALSE — It is $-\sin x$. The minus sign is not decoration: $\cos$ is decreasing just to the right of $0$, so its derivative must be negative there.

Your turn

Differentiate $y=x^{2}\cos x$ and $y=\dfrac{\sin x}{x}$.

Show the solution

First one is a product. $u=x^{2}$ and $v=\cos x$, so $u'=2x$ and $v'=-\sin x$.

The Product Rule is $(uv)'=u'v+uv'$. Substituting:

$y'=(2x)(\cos x)+(x^{2})(-\sin x)$

$y'=\mathbf{2x\cos x-x^{2}\sin x}$

Second one is a quotient. $u=\sin x$ and $v=x$, so $u'=\cos x$ and $v'=1$.

The Quotient Rule is $\left(\dfrac{u}{v}\right)'=\dfrac{u'v-uv'}{v^{2}}$. Substituting:

$y'=\dfrac{(\cos x)(x)-(\sin x)(1)}{x^{2}}=\mathbf{\dfrac{x\cos x-\sin x}{x^{2}}}$

Yesterday's rules, today's functions. Nothing new happens — the only new thing is that $\frac{d}{dx}\cos x$ carries a minus sign.

Your turn

Let $f(x)=\cos x$. Find $f^{(43)}(x)$ without computing forty-three derivatives.

Show the solution

Differentiate one at a time and write every one down:

$f(x)=\cos x$

$f'(x)=-\sin x$

$f''(x)=-\cos x$

$f'''(x)=\sin x$

$f^{(4)}(x)=\cos x$ — back to where it started.

So the list repeats with period $4$, and only the remainder of $43$ on division by $4$ matters.

$43=4(10)+3$, so the remainder is $3$.

That makes $f^{(43)}$ the same as $f^{(3)}=f'''$, so $f^{(43)}(x)=\mathbf{\sin x}$.

The period-four cycle is the whole trick. Check the small cases by hand once and you never have to again.

Your turn

Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{\theta\to0}\frac{\sin 5\theta}{2\theta}$.

Show the solution

Substituting $\theta=0$ gives $\dfrac{0}{0}$, so there is algebra to do first.

The limit you know is $\displaystyle\lim_{u\to0}\frac{\sin u}{u}=1$, and it only applies when the inside of the sine matches the denominator exactly.

Here the inside is $5\theta$ but the denominator is $2\theta$. Build a $5\theta$ underneath by multiplying by $\dfrac{5}{5}$:

$\dfrac{\sin 5\theta}{2\theta}=\dfrac{\sin 5\theta}{2\theta}\cdot\dfrac{5}{5}=\dfrac{5}{2}\cdot\dfrac{\sin 5\theta}{5\theta}$

As $\theta\to0$ the inside $5\theta\to0$ as well, so $\dfrac{\sin 5\theta}{5\theta}\to1$.

$\displaystyle\lim_{\theta\to0}\frac{\sin 5\theta}{2\theta}=\frac{5}{2}\cdot 1=\mathbf{\frac{5}{2}}$

Every one of these is the same move: make the inside match the bottom, and pay for it with a constant out front.

Common errors and misconceptions

Common error

$\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}\cos x=\sin x$

What is wrong with it

Why it looks rightThe pair swap into each other, so remembering that they swap is most of the work — and the minus sign is the easiest thing in the section to drop.

What is actually true$\frac{d}{dx}\cos x=-\sin x$. Read it off the graph rather than memorizing it: just to the right of $x=0$, $\cos x$ is falling, so its derivative must be negative there — and $\sin x$ is positive there, so the derivative cannot be $+\sin x$.

Common error

$\displaystyle\lim_{\theta\to0}\frac{\sin 5\theta}{2\theta}=1$

What is wrong with it

Why it looks rightIt is the limit everyone just learned, and the expression really is a sine over a multiple of the same variable.

What is actually true$\frac{\sin u}{u}\to1$ only when the inside of the sine and the bottom are the same thing. Here they are $5\theta$ and $2\theta$. Rewrite as $\frac{5}{2}\cdot\frac{\sin 5\theta}{5\theta}$, which now matches, and the answer is $\frac{5}{2}$.

Concept

The formula $\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}(\sin x)=\cos x$ is only correct when $x$ is measured in radians.

Show the answer

TRUE — In degrees the derivative is $\frac{\pi}{180}\cos x$. Radians are a hypothesis of the formula, not a preference — they are what makes $\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\sin h}{h}=1$ true.

Concept

True or false? Say why in one sentence.

The derivative of $\sin x$ depends on the fact that $\displaystyle\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\sin h}{h}=1$.

Show the answer

TRUE — Expanding $\sin(x+h)$ in the difference quotient leaves exactly that limit, which is why 2.3 proved it with the Squeeze Theorem before this section could exist.

Your turn

Differentiate $y=\sec x\tan x$.

Show the solution

It is a product of two of the six, so $u=\sec x$ and $v=\tan x$.

The two derivatives you need: $u'=\sec x\tan x$ and $v'=\sec^{2}x$.

The Product Rule is $(uv)'=u'v+uv'$. Substituting:

$y'=(\sec x\tan x)(\tan x)+(\sec x)(\sec^{2}x)$

Multiply out each piece: $(\sec x\tan x)(\tan x)=\sec x\tan^{2}x$ and $(\sec x)(\sec^{2}x)=\sec^{3}x$.

$y'=\mathbf{\sec x\tan^{2}x+\sec^{3}x}$

Both terms carry a $\sec x$, so it also factors: $y'=\sec x\left(\tan^{2}x+\sec^{2}x\right)$.

Both derivatives here are ones you have to know cold: $\frac{d}{dx}\sec x=\sec x\tan x$ and $\frac{d}{dx}\tan x=\sec^{2}x$.

Your turn

Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\sec\!\left(\frac{\pi}{3}+h\right)-\sec\frac{\pi}{3}}{h}$. You should not need to compute a limit.

Show the solution

Do not start computing. Look at the shape first: $\displaystyle\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}$ is the definition of $f'(a)$.

Match the pieces: $f(x)=\sec x$ and $a=\dfrac{\pi}{3}$.

So the whole limit is just $f'\!\left(\dfrac{\pi}{3}\right)$, and no limit has to be evaluated at all.

$f'(x)=\sec x\tan x$, so $f'\!\left(\dfrac{\pi}{3}\right)=\sec\dfrac{\pi}{3}\cdot\tan\dfrac{\pi}{3}$.

$\sec\dfrac{\pi}{3}=\dfrac{1}{\cos(\pi/3)}=\dfrac{1}{1/2}=2$, and $\tan\dfrac{\pi}{3}=\sqrt{3}$.

$f'\!\left(\dfrac{\pi}{3}\right)=2\cdot\sqrt{3}=\mathbf{2\sqrt{3}}$

A difference quotient in disguise. If you find yourself expanding $\sec\left(\frac{\pi}{3}+h\right)$, stop and look at the shape again.

Your turn

Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\tan 3x}{x}$ and $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin^{2}x}{x}$.

Show the solution

First limit. Substituting gives $\dfrac{0}{0}$. Write the tangent in terms of sine and cosine:

$\dfrac{\tan 3x}{x}=\dfrac{\sin 3x}{x\cos 3x}=\dfrac{\sin 3x}{x}\cdot\dfrac{1}{\cos 3x}$

The first factor needs $3x$ underneath to match the inside of the sine, so multiply by $\dfrac{3}{3}$:

$\dfrac{\sin 3x}{x}=3\cdot\dfrac{\sin 3x}{3x}\to 3\cdot 1=3$

The second factor is continuous at $0$: $\dfrac{1}{\cos 3x}\to\dfrac{1}{\cos 0}=\dfrac{1}{1}=1$.

$\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\tan 3x}{x}=3\cdot 1=\mathbf{3}$

Second limit. Split the square into two factors rather than reaching for the known limit:

$\dfrac{\sin^{2}x}{x}=\dfrac{\sin x\cdot\sin x}{x}=\dfrac{\sin x}{x}\cdot\sin x$

The first factor $\to1$, and the second $\to\sin 0=0$.

$\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin^{2}x}{x}=1\cdot 0=\mathbf{0}$

Both are built by splitting off a $\frac{\sin(\text{something})}{\text{same something}}$ and seeing what is left over. The second one catches people who assume every $\frac{\sin}{x}$ limit is $1$.

Common error

To evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\sec\!\left(\frac{\pi}{3}+h\right)-\sec\frac{\pi}{3}}{h}$, start by expanding $\sec\!\left(\frac{\pi}{3}+h\right)$ with an addition formula.

What is wrong with it

Why it looks rightIt is a real technique and it is what the expression looks like it is asking for. It is also what you would have to do if you did not recognize the shape.

Common error

$\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin^{2}x}{x}=1$

What is wrong with it

Why it looks rightThere is a $\sin$ on the top and an $x$ on the bottom, and that pattern has meant $1$ every time so far today.

What is actually trueSplit it: $\frac{\sin^{2}x}{x}=\frac{\sin x}{x}\cdot\sin x$. The first factor goes to $1$, but the second goes to $\sin 0=\mathbf{0}$, so the product goes to $0$. Count the sines before reaching for the rule.

Common error

For $g(x)=x^{3}-4x^{2}+3x+2e^{x}$, the tangent line at $x=0$ is $y-g(x)=g'(x)(x-0)$, that is $y-(x^{3}-4x^{2}+3x+2e^{x})=(3x^{2}-8x+3+2e^{x})(x-0)$.

What is wrong with it

Why it looks rightThe point-slope formula is quoted perfectly and both pieces are in the right slots. Nothing has been mis-differentiated. Three of you wrote a version of this on Exam 1, and one went on to expand it faultlessly into y = 4x³ - 12x² + 6x + 2eˣ + 2xeˣ.

What is actually trueCount the x's. A line has exactly one, and that one is the input — everything else has to be a number before you write it down. $g(x)$ and $g'(x)$ are functions; what point-slope wants is $g(a)$ and $g'(a)$, both evaluated at the single $a$ you were given. Common error

$g'(x)=3x^{2}-8x+3+2e^{x}$, so $g'(0)=5$.  [and then nothing]

What is wrong with it

Why it looks rightIt is correct, it is the hard part, and it is the part the section spent two days on. It feels like the answer.

What is actually trueIt is one of the three numbers a tangent line needs, and the question asked for the line. Section 3.4 — The chain rule

Class day 21, Tuesday October 13

Concept

True or false? Say why in one sentence.

$\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}f\big(g(x)\big)=f'\big(g'(x)\big)$.

Show the answer

FALSE — The Chain Rule is $f'(g(x))\cdot g'(x)$ – evaluate the outer derivative at the inner function, then multiply by the inner derivative.

Concept

True or false? Say why in one sentence.

The rules $\frac{d}{dx}f(g(x))=f'(g(x))\,g'(x)$ and $\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{dy}{du}\cdot\frac{du}{dx}$ say the same thing.

Show the answer

TRUE — Put $y=f(u)$ and $u=g(x)$: then $\frac{dy}{du}=f'(g(x))$ and $\frac{du}{dx}=g'(x)$. Two notations, one rule.

Your turn

Differentiate. (a) $y=(3x^{2}+5)^{4}$ (b) $y=\sin 7x$ (c) $y=\sqrt{x^{2}+1}$

Show the solution

(a) Outside is $(\ )^{4}$, inside is $3x^{2}+5$.

Chain Rule: differentiate the outside leaving the inside alone, then multiply by the derivative of the inside.

$y'=4(3x^{2}+5)^{3}\cdot\dfrac{d}{dx}\left(3x^{2}+5\right)=4(3x^{2}+5)^{3}\cdot 6x$

$y'=\mathbf{24x(3x^{2}+5)^{3}}$

(b) Outside is $\sin$, inside is $7x$.

$y'=\cos(7x)\cdot\dfrac{d}{dx}(7x)=\cos(7x)\cdot 7=\mathbf{7\cos 7x}$

(c) Rewrite the root as a power first: $y=\left(x^{2}+1\right)^{1/2}$. Outside is $(\ )^{1/2}$, inside is $x^{2}+1$.

$y'=\dfrac{1}{2}\left(x^{2}+1\right)^{-1/2}\cdot\dfrac{d}{dx}\left(x^{2}+1\right)=\dfrac{1}{2}\left(x^{2}+1\right)^{-1/2}\cdot 2x$

The $2$ cancels, and a negative power moves to the denominator:

$y'=\dfrac{2x}{2\sqrt{x^{2}+1}}=\mathbf{\dfrac{x}{\sqrt{x^{2}+1}}}$

Say the two words before you write anything — outside, inside. Every one of these is the same move; only the outside function changes.

Your turn

Find the equation of the tangent line to $y=(2x-1)^{3}$ at $x=1$.

Show the solution

Start from the equation of any line through a point $(x_{0},y_{0})$ with slope $m$: $$y - y_{0} = m\left(x - x_{0}\right)$$

So three numbers are needed, and none of them may still contain an $x$: $x_{0}$, $y_{0}$ and $m$.

$x_{0}=1$, given.

$y_{0}=y(1)=\left(2(1)-1\right)^{3}=(1)^{3}=1$, so the point is $(1,1)$.

$m=y'(1)$. Chain Rule for the slope function, outside $(\ )^{3}$ and inside $2x-1$:

$y'=3(2x-1)^{2}\cdot\dfrac{d}{dx}(2x-1)=3(2x-1)^{2}\cdot 2=6(2x-1)^{2}$

Now evaluate it — this is the step that turns a function into a number: $y'(1)=6\left(2(1)-1\right)^{2}=6$.

Substitute all three: $y-1=6(x-1)$, so $\mathbf{y=6x-5}$.

The derivative must be evaluated at $x=1$ before it goes in the line. A tangent line has one slope, a number — if an $x$ is still in it, it is not a line.

Your turn

The chain rule says $\frac{d}{dx}\sin(x^{2})=\cos(x^{2})\cdot 2x$. Explain in one sentence why the answer is not $\cos(x^{2})$, without computing anything.

Show the solution

Going one unit in $x$ does not move $x^{2}$ by one unit — near $x=3$ it moves it by about $6$.

$\cos(x^{2})$ is the rate at which $\sin$ changes per unit of the inside, so it has to be converted into rate per unit of $x$.

The factor $2x$ is exactly that conversion, which is why the two rates multiply.

This is the sentence to be able to say out loud. If the chain rule is only a formula, the $2x$ looks optional, and that is the single most common way to lose these points.

Your turn

Differentiate. (a) $y=\cos^{3}x$ (b) $y=e^{\,x^{2}-3x}$

Show the solution

(a) First rewrite it so the composite is visible: $\cos^{3}x=\left(\cos x\right)^{3}$. The exponent is printed on the function name but it applies to the output.

Outside is $(\ )^{3}$, inside is $\cos x$. Chain Rule, and $\frac{d}{dx}\cos x=-\sin x$:

$y'=3\left(\cos x\right)^{2}\cdot\left(-\sin x\right)=\mathbf{-3\cos^{2}x\,\sin x}$

(b) Outside is $e^{(\ )}$, inside is $x^{2}-3x$. The outside is its own derivative, so the $e$ part is unchanged:

$y'=e^{\,x^{2}-3x}\cdot\left(2x-3\right)=\mathbf{\left(2x-3\right)e^{\,x^{2}-3x}}$

Find the equation of the tangent line to $y=\sin^{2}x$ at $x=\frac{\pi}{4}$.

Show the solution

Rewrite first: $y=\sin^{2}x=\left(\sin x\right)^{2}$.

Start from the equation of any line through $(x_{0},y_{0})$ with slope $m$: $$y-y_{0}=m\left(x-x_{0}\right)$$

Three numbers are needed, and none may still contain an $x$.

$x_{0}=\frac{\pi}{4}$, given.

$y_{0}=\sin^{2}\!\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right)=\left(\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\right)^{2}=\frac{2}{4}=\frac12$.

Suppose $g(1)=4$, $g'(1)=-2$, and $f'(4)=5$. Find $(f\circ g)'(1)$. You are not given a formula for either function.

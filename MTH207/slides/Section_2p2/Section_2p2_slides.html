What Section 2.1 Owed Us

Two problems, one answer. The tangent slope and the instantaneous velocity both

came out as the same move: compute something you can on an interval, then

shrink the interval.

Both times we said the numbers “pile up near” a value and moved on.

Today we say what “piles up near” actually means. That is the one new idea the whole course rests on.

Say it plainly: everything in 2.1 was algebra they already had. The limit is the first genuinely new thing.

What a Limit Means

Limit of a function

We write \(\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L\) if we can make the values of \(f(x)\) as close

to \(L\) as we like by taking \(x\) close enough to \(a\), but not equal to \(a\).

Read the last four words again. They are doing all the work.

What a Limit Means

  • The limit does not care whether \(f(a)\) exists.
  • If \(f(a)\) does exist, the limit does not care what it equals.

\(f(a)\) and \(\lim_{x\to a} f(x)\) are answers to two different questions. Getting them confused is the most expensive mistake in this chapter.

The guide's own text question is exactly this: what is the difference between "\(f(a)=L\)" and "\(\lim f(x) = L\)"? Worth asking aloud.

Your turn

From the graph: \(\lim_{x\to1}f(x)\), \; \(f(1)\), \; \(\lim_{x\to3}f(x)\), \; \(\lim_{x\to5}f(x)\), \; \(f(5)\). Say if one does not exist.

Reading Limits off a Graph

\[\text{At } x=1:\quad \lim_{x\to1}f(x) = 2, \qquad f(1) = 3.2\]

\[\text{At } x=3:\quad \lim_{x\to3}f(x) \ \mathbf{\text{does not exist}}\]

\[\text{At } x=5:\quad \lim_{x\to5}f(x) = 3.5 = f(5)\]

Reading Limits off a Graph

Three different things happen at \(x=1\), \(x=3\) and \(x=5\). Only one of them is a function behaving itself.

The graph stays on screen while they work — that is the whole point of this layout. Let them argue about \(x=3\) before you name one-sided limits; that is Monday.

When a Table Lies to You

A table is evidence, not proof. Here is \(f(x) = \sin(\pi/x)\) near \(0\).

Your turn

Compute \(f(x)\) at \(x = 1\), \(\tfrac12\), \(\tfrac13\), \(\tfrac14\), \(\tfrac15\). What would you conclude?

Every one gives \(\sin(n\pi) = 0\). A table says the limit is \(0\).

When a Table Lies to You

The limit does not exist. The function swings between \(-1\) and \(1\) infinitely often, and your table happened to sample the zeros every time.

Get their answer of 0 on the board BEFORE showing the graph — the reveal is the whole frame. This is the guide's "dangers of a calculator" point and objective o22-4. Tie back to Day 1: an answer that feels right is not evidence.

Approaching from One Side

At \(x=3\) on Thursday's graph the limit failed — but not because nothing

happened. Two different things happened, one from each side.

One-sided limits

\(\lim_{x \to a^-} f(x) = L\) means \(f(x) \to L\) as \(x\) approaches \(a\) through

values less than \(a\). The right-hand limit \(\lim_{x \to a^+}\) uses values

greater than \(a\).

Approaching from One Side

Your turn

Go back to the graph. What are \(\lim_{x\to3^-}f(x)\) and \(\lim_{x\to3^+}f(x)\)?

\(4\) from the left, \(2.5\) from the right.

\(\lim_{x\to a}f(x) = L\) exactly when both one-sided limits exist and both equal \(L\). At \(x=3\) they exist and disagree, so the two-sided limit does not exist.

Limits That Grow Without Bound

My turn

Look at \(g(x) = \dfrac{1}{x^2}\) as \(x \to 0\). As \(x\) gets small, \(x^2\) gets very small and positive, so \(1/x^2\) gets enormous — from both sides.

We write \(\lim_{x\to0} \dfrac{1}{x^2} = \infty\).

This is not a number. Writing \(\infty\) is shorthand for “the values grow past any bound you name” — it is a description of how the limit fails, not a value it reaches.

Limits That Grow Without Bound

Your turn

Now try \(h(x) = \dfrac{1}{x}\) as \(x \to 0\). Check both sides separately. Is the answer the same?

\[\text{From the right:}\quad \frac1x \to \infty\]

\[\text{From the left:}\quad \frac1x \to -\infty\]

The two sides disagree, so \(\lim_{x\to0}\frac1x\) does not exist — not even as \(\infty\).

The contrast between \(1/x^2\) and \(1/x\) is the whole frame. Give them two minutes on \(1/x\) before revealing.

Vertical Asymptotes

Vertical asymptote

The line \(x = a\) is a vertical asymptote of \(y=f(x)\) if at least one of the

one-sided limits at \(a\) is \(\infty\) or \(-\infty\).

Note what this does not say. It says nothing about whether \(f(a)\)

exists — only about how \(f\) behaves nearby.

Vertical Asymptotes

Your turn

Find the vertical asymptotes of \(f(x) = \dfrac{x+1}{(x-2)(x+3)}\). Then decide the sign of \(f\) just to the right of \(x=2\).

\[\text{Denominator} = 0 \text{ at } x = 2 \text{ and } x = -3\]

\[\text{Just right of } 2:\quad x+1 \approx 3 > 0, \quad x-2 = \text{small} > 0, \quad x+3 \approx 5 > 0\]

\[\text{All three positive, so } f \to +\infty\]

Finding where an asymptote is takes algebra. Finding which way the graph goes takes a sign check on each factor.

Where This Goes

We can now estimate a limit from a table or a graph, and say precisely when one

fails to exist.

What we can do

Read a limit off a picture. Recognise a jump, a hole, an oscillation, an asymptote.

What we cannot do yet

Compute a limit exactly, from a formula, without guessing from numbers.

Where This Goes

What You Should Be Able to Do

By the end of this section — and on the quiz and the exam:

  • I can estimate a limit from a table or a graph, and explain why the value of the function at the point does not affect it.
  • I can decide whether a two-sided limit exists by comparing the one-sided limits.
  • I can recognise an infinite limit and locate the vertical asymptotes of a function.
  • I can explain how a table of values or a calculator can mislead me about a limit.

If you cannot honestly tick one of these off, that is the one to work on — not the one that felt hardest in class.

Visit these if there is time; if not, say where they live. They are the blueprint for the quiz and the exam, so a student who can do all of them is prepared by construction.

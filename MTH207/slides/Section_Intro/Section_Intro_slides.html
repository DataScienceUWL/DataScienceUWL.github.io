Calculus Asks More of Your Logic

In algebra, trigonometry, and precalculus, roughly 95% calculation and 5% logic.

In calculus, closer to 70% calculation and 30% logic.

You will be asked to state theorems, not just use them — because knowing exactly what a theorem requires is what tells you whether you are allowed to use it.

The calculation is not the hard part of calculus. Knowing which calculation you are entitled to do is.

“If A, then B”

Theorem: The Moldy Bread Theorem

If I eat moldy bread, then I will get sick.

Here \(A\) (“I eat moldy bread”) is the hypothesis and \(B\) (“I get sick”) is the conclusion.

This is not the same statement as “If I am sick, then I ate moldy bread.” I could be sick for all sorts of reasons.

“If A then B” is not “if B then A.” Think of the hypothesis as a requirement you must check before the theorem is available to you.

The Squeeze Theorem in 2.3 is the first place this bites. Foreshadow it.

A Definition Is Not an Example

What is the definition of a car?

  • “A Ford Focus.”
  • “A four-wheeled vehicle used on paved streets to transport people and puppies.”

Only the second one lets you decide whether a thing you have never seen before is a car.

The first time I taught this course, a student in office hours gave me an example of continuity when I asked for the definition — and the example could not solve the problem in front of us.

A picture is an example. Pictures are useful and pictures can deceive. Learn the definition too.

Reading a Definition Carefully

Ginormous

A natural number \(n\) is ginormous if \(n\) has more than \(n/2\) distinct factors.

Testing small numbers against the definition of ginormous
\(n\)factorsginormous?
\(1\)\(1\)yes
\(2\)\(1, 2\)yes
\(3\)\(1, 3\)yes
\(4\)\(1, 2, 4\)yes
\(5\)\(1, 5\)no
\(6\)\(1, 2, 3, 6\)yes
\(8\)\(1, 2, 4, 8\)?

\(8\) has exactly four factors, and \(8/2 = 4\). “More than” means strictly more — so \(8\) is not ginormous.

Reading a Definition Carefully

Nobody has any intuition about ginormous numbers. All you can do is read the definition and apply it exactly. That is the skill.

Let them argue about 8. The point lands harder if someone gets it wrong first.

So What Is Calculus?

Here is my honest summary of this course:

Calculus is algebra and trigonometry, plus one new idea.

Everything you already know still applies — factoring, fractions, exponents, logarithms, the unit circle. You will use all of it, constantly, and rusty algebra will cost you far more points than calculus ever will.

What is new is a single idea, and we will spend the next five weeks learning to handle it.

The One New Idea: The Limit

Every question in this course has the same shape:

  • The quantity I want cannot be computed directly.
  • But I can compute an approximation to it.
  • And I can make that approximation as good as I like.

A limit is what you get by pushing that process as far as it goes.

Two questions in particular are worth a whole course. Both were open problems for two thousand years, and both are answered by limits.

Big Limit #1: How Fast, Right Now?

A ball is thrown straight up. Its height after \(t\) seconds is \(s(t) = 64 - 16(t-1)^2\) feet.

The question. How fast is it moving at the instant \(t = 0.5\)?

The trouble. Speed is distance divided by time — but an instant has no duration. There is nothing to divide by.

Your turn

Over an interval you can do it with algebra you already have. Compute the average velocity \(\dfrac{s(b)-s(a)}{b-a}\) on \([0.5, 1]\), then on \([0.5, 0.6]\).

Big Limit #1: How Fast, Right Now?

On \([0.5,1]\): \(\frac{64-60}{0.5} = 8\) ft/sec. On \([0.5,0.6]\): \(\frac{61.44-60}{0.1} = 14.4\) ft/sec. Keep shrinking and they pile up near \(16\).

Give them three or four minutes on the two intervals before showing anything. Then drag the slider and ask them to call out the number before it settles.

Big Limit #2: Area by Exhaustion

The question. What is the area of a circle of radius \(1\)?

The trouble. We know the area of a triangle. We know the area of a rectangle. A circle has no straight edges at all.

Archimedes' idea. Draw a polygon inside the circle. You can find its area. Then add sides.

Your turn

Find the exact area of the inscribed square, then the inscribed regular hexagon. Geometry only — no calculus, no calculator.

Big Limit #2: Area by Exhaustion

Square: \(\frac{1}{2}(2)(2) = 2\). Hexagon: six equilateral triangles of side \(1\), so \(\frac{3\sqrt{3}}{2} \approx 2.598\). Both under \(\pi \approx 3.14159\), and the hexagon is closer.

Let them wrestle with the hexagon — the equilateral-triangle decomposition is the insight. Only then push the slider to 96, which is exactly as far as Archimedes got, by hand, around 250 BC.

The Same Trick, on Any Curve

Archimedes needed a circle. In Chapter 5 we do it with rectangles, and then it works under any curve.

The Same Trick, on Any Curve

Same move both times: approximate with something you can compute, then let the approximation run to its limit.

Two Limits, One Course

The derivative

How fast, right now. Chapters 2 through 4 — rates of change, tangent lines, optimization.

The integral

How much, in total. Chapters 5 and 6 — area, accumulation, net change.

They look like opposite questions. In Chapter 5 we will prove they are inverses of each other, which is such a surprise that it is called the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus.

Starting tomorrow we chase the first one. We cannot finish it until we know what a limit really is — that is Sections 2.2 through 2.6.

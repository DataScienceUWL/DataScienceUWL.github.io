Secant Lines and Average Rate of Change

MTH 207 — Calculus I
Limits and Derivatives

A Ball in the Air

A ball is thrown straight up. Its height in feet after \(t\) seconds is

\[ s(t) = 64 - 16(t-1)^2,    0 \le t \le 3. \]

  • Where is the ball when \(t = 0\)?
  • When does it reach its highest point?
  • When does it land?

This is a downward parabola with vertex at \(t = 1\), \(s = 64\).

Build the table with them before showing the graph. The three landmark points — release, peak, landing — are the ones to label.

Reading the Motion off the Graph

\(t\)\(s(t)\)
\(0\)\(48\)initial height
\(1\)\(64\)peak
\(2\)\(48\)
\(3\)\(0\)landing
  • On \(0 < t < 1\) the ball is rising.
  • On \(1 < t < 3\) the ball is falling.
  • At \(t = 1\) it turns around — stopped for an instant.

“Stopped for an instant” is the idea we are about to make precise.

Average Velocity

Example 1

Compute

\[ AV_{[0.5, 1]} = \frac{s(1) - s(0.5)}{1 - 0.5} \]

and say what it measures. What are its units?

Compute the average velocity on \([0.5, 1]\).

\(s(1) = 64\) and \(s(0.5) = 60\), so

\[ AV_{[0.5,1]} = \frac{64 - 60}{1 - 0.5} = \frac{4}{1/2} = 8 \text{ft/sec}. \]

This is the slope of the secant line joining the two points on the graph. It is the ball's average velocity over that half second.

From Secant Lines to Tangent Lines

We want the slope of the tangent line at a single point \((a, f(a))\).

The problem: slope needs two points.

\[ m = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1} = \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a} \]

That is the slope of a secant line, not a tangent.

The curve must be smooth — no corners, jumps or gaps.

The Difference Quotient

Slope of the secant line through \((a, f(a))\) and a second point:

\[ m_{PQ} = \frac{f(x) - f(a)}{x - a}   \text{or}   m_{PQ} = \frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h} \]

Difference quotient

Either expression above is the difference quotient of \(f\) at \(a\).

The calculus step. Take the limit as the second point slides toward \(a\):

\[ m = \lim_{x \to a} \frac{f(x) - f(a)}{x - a} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h} \]

New problem: how do we deal with a limit? That is Section 2.2.

Approximating a Tangent Slope

Example 2

Approximate the slope of the tangent line to \(f(x) = x^2\) at \(x = 3\) by computing secant slopes using a second point at \(x = 6,\ 5,\ 4,\ 3.5,\ 3.1\).

Compute the secant slope from \(a=3\) to each listed \(x\).

\[ \frac{6^2-3^2}{6-3} = \frac{27}{3} = 9    \frac{5^2-3^2}{5-3} = \frac{16}{2} = 8    \frac{4^2-3^2}{4-3} = \frac{7}{1} = 7 \]

\[ \frac{3.5^2-3^2}{3.5-3} = \frac{3.25}{0.5} = 6.5    \frac{3.1^2-3^2}{3.1-3} = 6.1 \]

The slopes are marching toward \(6\).

Computing the Limit Exactly

\[ m = \lim_{x \to 3} \frac{x^2 - 3^2}{x - 3} \]

\[ = \lim_{x \to 3} \frac{(x-3)(x+3)}{x-3} \]

\[ = \lim_{x \to 3} (x + 3) = 6 \]

The tangent line to \(y = x^2\) at \(x = 3\) has slope \(6\) — matching the pattern \(9, 8, 7, 6.5, 6.1, \ldots\)

Cancelling \((x-3)\) is legitimate because \(x \neq 3\) in the limit — worth saying out loud now, it recurs constantly in \S2.3.

Two Interpretations

Slope of the secant on \([a,b]\)

The average rate of change of \(y\) as \(x\) changes from \(a\) to \(b\). For the ball: average velocity.

Slope of the tangent at \(a\)

The instantaneous rate of change of \(y\) with respect to \(x\) at \(a\). For the ball: the speedometer reading.

Everything in Chapter 3 is a technique for computing that second slope quickly.
